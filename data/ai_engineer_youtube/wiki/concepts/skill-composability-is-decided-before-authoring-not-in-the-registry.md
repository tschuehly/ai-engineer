# Skill Composability Is Decided Before Authoring, Not in the Registry

Summary: A catalog can make skills findable, versioned, and permissioned, but it cannot make independently written skills compose. Composability is a property of how the domain was carved before anyone wrote a skill, so it has to be agreed by named humans across architecture, infrastructure, and security — which is the point where, as Touil puts it, "technology stop solving the problem."

Use when:
- Standing up a skills platform and estimating what the catalog will and will not fix.
- Diagnosing skills that duplicate each other or conflict when loaded together, after a registry is already in place.
- Deciding who has to be in the room before an org-wide skills effort starts.
- Pushing back on a plan whose entire governance answer is a tool.

Details:
- **The claim.** "Composability is not something that comes by default. You need to have a governance way to align what to build and how to design it. Think about the domain-driven approach that we have been taken also for many years — is similar to how you shape your skills catalog." ([Touil](../sources/20260828_M05vON8i0aI.md), 12:22-12:37) The design principle it enforces is one task per skill: "you should not build like a one skill like a monolith, it should be specialized to define one task specifically," and the failure it prevents is stated in terms of run time — "so you don't have duplication across your skills, when you're trying to run them conflicts each other." (09:08-09:24)
- **Why a registry cannot supply this after the fact.** A catalog answers *does this exist* and *who owns it*. Two skills written independently against overlapping slices of the same domain are both findable, both owned, both current — and still overlap. The conflict only appears when they are loaded into the same context, which is downstream of everything the catalog controls. Discovery is necessary and it is not sufficient; the boundary has to have been drawn first, which is the same lesson service decomposition produced.
- **Where the tooling argument ends, quoted.** "All of this is actually play around a governance, and this is where technology stop solving the problem. So you figure out all of this, all good — now who's going to govern this? And that is where it really depend how your organization is structured today. That is where you should have your architects, your engineer leads, infra leads etc, and cyber leads actually sitting down owning part of those domains and making sure that the skills we need to get updated is actually according to the policies you want to adhere within your organization." (14:47-15:17) The three named constituencies map onto the three ways a skill can be wrong: architecturally misplaced, operationally unrunnable, or unsafe to grant.
- **The ownership half, which is separable from the carve.** "Without a governance you cannot really discover it. Think about it as the Backstage, the IDP… I need to understand who owns this microservice back in the days. I don't need to talk to anyone, I just need to tap into the service catalog and immediately find who actually owns it… If you don't have an owner, then no one will be able to maintain, scale those skills." (11:56-12:23) Ownership is what a catalog *can* deliver; the domain carve is what it cannot. Both are needed and only one is a tooling purchase.
- **What the platform still owes, once the carve exists.** A catalog with metadata that is discoverable and searchable; an MCP plugged into that catalog plus a CLI to pull skills into an IDE or a sandbox; declared dependencies between skills; versioning and lifecycle so "the agents automatically capture that there is a latest version of the skill and pull it"; access control, because "if you don't know who is accessing what, that is a huge gap"; and evaluation and observability. (13:44-14:47) Note that dependencies-between-skills is the mechanism composability is *expressed* through — it presumes the boundaries it cannot decide.
- **The adoption ladder this sits on top of.** Individual (create, test, improve and use, "it shouldn't be random, it should be structured way," with the org agreeing which tool), then team (share on creation; because teams share a stack and products "it's going to evolve really quickly"), then the centralized platform. (13:13-13:44) The governance forum is what makes rung three different in kind from rungs one and two rather than just larger.
- **Relation to the platform-obligations page this wiki already carries.** [Distributed Rule Authoring Is a Platform Problem, Not an Authoring Problem](distributed-rule-authoring-is-a-platform-problem.md) is Uber's account of the same territory and the two divide cleanly on timing. Uber's four obligations — bind to the existing ownership model, co-locate rules with code, route deterministically, return per-rule telemetry to the author — are all *post*-authoring: they operate, route, and garbage-collect rules that already exist, and the feedback loop is what retires bad ones. Touil's claim is *pre*-authoring: the loop cannot converge on a coherent library if the units overlap, because the telemetry will show two half-used skills rather than one unused one. Read together, they say a distributed rule library needs both a carve at the front and a feedback path at the back, and neither substitutes for the other.
- **The counter-reading worth holding.** Domain-driven decomposition is expensive, frequently done badly, and its microservices precedent is not an unambiguous success — the same era produced distributed monoliths carved by committees who guessed wrong. Touil imports the analogy approvingly and does not address its failure mode. The cheap hedge is that skills are far easier to merge and split than services, so an early carve can be wrong at lower cost; that argument is this page's, not the source's.
- Limits. Nothing in this talk is measured, and this claim in particular is asserted from experience with no deployment behind it. The prescribed end state — a centralized platform plus a standing governance body — is also the kind of engagement the speaker's firm sells. See the source's [Provenance and Limits](../sources/20260828_M05vON8i0aI.md).
- **A concrete carve, agreed between two named functions.** Cloudflare's skill files are cut by role first — "role-specific skill files, which have the context of the business information tying it to the data" for technical and non-technical users — and the intended shape of the library is "an expert-level knowledge skill at every level," agreed between the go-to-market team and the operations team at review time. The decision this page says cannot be made in a catalog is here made by two constituencies at intake, which is the smallest version of the cross-functional agreement it argues for: business owners decide the procedure is right, data owners decide the query is right. ([Joyce](../sources/20260826_Qw_tC68KKes.md), 06:47-07:05, 13:36-14:02)
