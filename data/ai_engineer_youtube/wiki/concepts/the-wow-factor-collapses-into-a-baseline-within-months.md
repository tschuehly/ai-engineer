# The Wow Factor Collapses Into a Baseline Within Months

Summary: A capability that delighted users becomes their habit, and a habit becomes the floor they judge you against. Four to six months after a successful launch the same people return with complaints, so a static internal product is on a decay curve even when nothing about it got worse.

Use when:
- An internal AI product launched well and the roadmap has slowed because it "works."
- Usage or sentiment is falling with no corresponding regression in quality.
- Arguing for continued investment in a feature that is already considered delivered.
- Deciding how much of the roadmap must be new capability rather than refinement.

Details:
- The arc, four to six months out: "you successfully launched the product. You are first like rockstars in the company. People literally show you in the corridor, 'hey, your product is awesome. We can talk to our data now. We don't need to wait on the queue to get access to analysts.' And after a couple of months, they start coming back to you with frustrations. 'I cannot do this in the product anymore. I saw this other AI product that does this.'" ([Izmit](../sources/20260826_DrTdD-ttjCY.md), 08:52-09:28)
- **The mechanism is habituation, not regression.** "This is what I call the collapsing of the wow factor. Initially you are cool, but then that becomes a habit. You basically change their habit and it becomes standard for them. Now you need to raise the bar again." The product did not get worse; the comparison set moved, because the product itself moved it. Success is what destroys the surplus. (09:26-09:38)
- The consequence for a stalled roadmap is displacement, and quickly: "if you just do the first stage, and if you just wait there, you will get disrupted in a month or two, because now you already raised their expectations, that already became a baseline, and then they will find another product that does better than you, and right now the switch is very easy. They're going to just switch overnight." Internal products are not protected from this by being internal; the alternative is whatever tool a user can adopt on their own. (11:14-11:35)
- The operating posture he draws from it inverts the usual reading of positive feedback: "every time people are happy, you should be paranoid. You should be like, 'okay, what am I going to show them in a month or two now?'" (17:22-17:33)
- **This is why external pace, not internal quality, sets the schedule.** The same logic appears in his architecture argument — "the longer you wait, the more you lose towards your competition, because if your competition is doing these kind of things 3-4 months ahead of you, that means that they're also getting more customers" (13:55-14:06) — and the two combine into a single claim: the reference point your users hold is refreshed by the market on a cadence you do not control ([Budget a Third of Sprint Capacity for Re-Architecture](budget-a-third-of-sprint-capacity-for-re-architecture.md)).
- The remedy is a staged capability ladder rather than more polish on the delivered rung ([Stage the Internal Agent Roadmap From Answers to Automation to Team-Built Tooling](stage-the-internal-agent-roadmap-from-answers-to-automation-to-team-built-tooling.md)).
- Limits: the "four to six months" and "month or two" intervals are recollections, not measurements; no retention or sentiment curve is shown for the decay, and no example of an actually lost internal user is given. This is also a period of unusually fast external capability improvement, which is part of why the reference point moves so quickly — the effect may be smaller when the frontier is flatter. ([Provenance and Limits](../sources/20260826_DrTdD-ttjCY.md))
