# Closing the Contribution Channel Is Where Slop Filtering Ends

Summary: Effort filters on AI-generated contributions are a stage, not an equilibrium. When the filter's cost still exceeds the channel's yield, maintainers close the channel — banning AI from pull requests, issues and comments, auto-closing every pull request, ending a decades-old bug bounty, or switching off third-party pull requests at the platform level. What is lost is not review throughput but the contributor pipeline, because the review conversation was how projects grew people they could trust.

Use when:
- Designing or escalating a contribution policy for a project receiving AI-generated submissions.
- Building a tool or agent that files issues, patches, or vulnerability reports into repositories you do not own.
- Assessing whether an upstream dependency will still accept your fix, and planning for the case where it will not.
- Arguing about whether an AI-provenance ban is about quality or about something else.

Details:
- **The four escalation steps, in the order Rizwan presents them.** Zig's code of conduct "essentially ban[s] all use of AI. You can't use it on pull requests or issues or even comments." curl's lead reports the project "being DDoSed by AI generated bug reports" and is "considering shutting down their bug bounty program for the first time in decades." tldraw is "automatically just closing all pull requests whether they're AI generated or not." And GitHub "added a feature to disable third-party pull requests altogether… which is really sad because pull requests were the thing that made GitHub what it is today. And we're probably going to see a lot of big open source projects opt into this." ([Rizwan](../sources/20260807_CoEIs6Xm8m8.md), 02:22-03:44)
- **The escalation is monotone and each step is cheaper than the one before it.** A human-effort filter ([Gate AI-generated open-source contributions through human-effort filters](gate-ai-generated-open-source-contributions-through-human-effort-filters.md)) still requires reading the filtered submissions. A provenance ban requires adjudicating provenance, which is unfalsifiable. Auto-closing everything requires nothing. Disabling the channel at the platform level requires less than nothing — it removes the notification too. Where a maintainer lands is set by the ratio between the yield of the channel and the cost of triaging it, and AI has moved both terms in the same direction.
- **The tldraw step is the informative one, because it abandons the provenance question.** Closing "all pull requests whether they're AI generated or not" concedes that you cannot cheaply tell, so you stop trying. This is the practical consequence of the wiki's [Treat slop as a quality failure, not an AI provenance label](treat-slop-as-a-quality-failure-not-an-ai-provenance-label.md) argument running the other way: if provenance is undetectable and quality assessment is the expensive part, a maintainer under load rationally drops the whole input rather than sorting it.
- **The loss is the apprenticeship, not the patches.** Rizwan reports Zig's stated reason as an ordering of values: the core team "value contributors more than they do the contributions… the primary goal for reviewing PRs and things isn't to add new code, but it's to help grow new contributors who can become trusted over time. And AI assistance completely breaks that." (02:37-02:57) This reframes what a review channel is for. If review is a code-quality gate, an AI-written patch that passes the gate is fine. If review is how a project identifies people it will later trust with commit rights, a patch whose author cannot explain it teaches the maintainer nothing about the author, and the channel stops producing its actual output. A project can be simultaneously receiving more usable code and losing its succession plan.
- **The counter-pressure that makes the closure stick.** Rizwan's own conclusion — "when I say that open source is dead, I mean some parts of it like the community — it's just not worth cultivating anymore, especially because building software is so cheap" (03:45-03:56) — adds the demand-side reason: if you can generate the feature yourself in an afternoon, the value of an outside contribution falls at the same time its triage cost rises. Community cultivation was already a cost centre justified by the code it produced; both sides of that justification moved.
- **The consequence for anyone building agents that submit upstream.** A tool that files issues, patches, or vulnerability reports into third-party repositories is aiming at a target that is closing. Design for it: assume some fraction of upstreams will not accept machine-originated submissions at all, make human authorship and review of the submission a first-class step rather than a formality, and treat a maintainer's provenance policy as a hard precondition to check before filing rather than an etiquette question. The wiki's [Wire Issue-Filing Authority Before Giving Agents a Tracker](wire-issue-filing-authority-before-giving-agents-a-tracker.md) covers the internal version of the same discipline; this is the external one, where you do not control the receiving policy.
- **The related failure this connects to.** The security-report channel breaks first and hardest, because a report costs nothing to generate and a bug bounty pays for volume — see [AI-Generated Security Reports Need Maintainer Triage](ai-generated-security-reports-need-maintainer-triage.md) for the mechanics and OpenClaw's 1,142-advisory figure. curl considering the end of a bounty running for decades is the same curve reaching its terminus.
- Provenance: these are four externally reported policy changes summarized in a keynote by a speaker arguing a thesis, with slides shown but no links read out. The individual policies are checkable; the characterization of Zig's reasoning is Rizwan's reading of their code of conduct, and "the CEO of curl" is his framing of a project lead. Nothing here measures how much genuine contribution the closures cost, which is the number that would settle whether the trade is a good one.
