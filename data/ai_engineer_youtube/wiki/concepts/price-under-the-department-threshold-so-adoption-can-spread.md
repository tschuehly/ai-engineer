# Price Under the Department Threshold So Adoption Can Spread

Summary: A high per-seat price does not just reduce revenue per account, it changes the decision the buyer makes: instead of buying for the company, they scope the purchase to a subset. A low base fee plus usage removes that scoping decision, so the product spreads and consumption grows into the revenue the seat price was trying to capture up front.

Use when:
- Setting the first price for a product with no market comparison.
- Buyers are converting but restricting the purchase to one team, one function, or "just the developers."
- A competitor has entered materially below your price and you are deciding whether to follow.
- Choosing between defending a seat price and adding a usage component beneath it.

Details:
- **The first price was derived from cost to serve, and that is named as the error.** "We set it initially ChatGPT Enterprise was $60 per user per month. Now, we were really the first ones on the market, and we didn't know what pricing was going to be. So, we basically priced it according to how expensive it was for us to serve it." Cost-plus is what you fall back on when no comparable exists, and it optimizes the wrong quantity — it says nothing about the buyer's willingness to deploy broadly. The talk's own name for the pattern is "building the Porsche before the Prius." ([Rosenthal](../sources/20260826_wdTRsfw0KG0.md), 09:06-09:44)
- **What reset the price was competitive entry, not internal analysis.** "And then all the other products came out on the market, Copilot and Gemini and Anthropic at a much lower price point. And we realized we had set the price too high." Worth reading honestly: the arrival of funded substitutes is a sufficient explanation for a price reset on its own, independent of whether $60 was ever mispriced against value. (09:26-09:44)
- **The mechanism, which is the durable part.** "When it was $60 per month, organizations would come in and say, 'well, I'm only buying this for a subset of my team or only for my developers.' … I'm not going to buy it for my whole company. It's too expensive. But once we lowered the threshold, it spread like wildfire." A price above a department's discretionary threshold forces a scoping conversation, and every scoping conversation caps the account's ceiling at the scope chosen. The cost is not the lost margin per seat; it is that the deployment decision is made once, early, at the smallest defensible size. (09:56-10:19)
- **The structure that replaced it.** "We changed the model to basically a license fee, a base fee to use the product, and then move to usage based… the barrier to entry went down. So, we had so many more companies signing licenses and signing contracts with us. And then usage went up over time as the barrier to entry went down." This is the hybrid shape the wiki already documents ([Use Hybrid AI Pricing to Balance Predictable Revenue and Margin Protection](use-hybrid-ai-pricing-to-balance-predictable-revenue-and-margin-protection.md)), reached from a different direction: not margin protection against power users, but removing the up-front decision that limits blast radius. (09:44-10:19)
- **A different reason seats fail than the one the wiki has recorded.** [Per-Seat Pricing Loses Its Referent When One Agent Does the Work of Many Logins](per-seat-pricing-loses-its-referent-when-agents-do-the-work.md) argues seats break because headcount stops tracking value once an agent operates the product. This case is earlier and simpler: seats broke while humans were still doing the work, because the price per seat exceeded what a buyer would authorize company-wide. The two are independent failures of the same metric, and only the second is fixed by lowering the number.
- **The next objection, and its answer.** "I know over time now seats are out. This has become common knowledge. We learned it the hard way. And I also know that now usage is also getting pushed back on as companies are seeing cost of usage skyrocket. There's easy ways around this. So, you could for example, cap. You can say, 'hey, we have a dashboard. You can cap your spend per month.' Most companies don't end up using that, but they like to know that it's there. You can cap certain employees' spend." The observation that caps are mostly unused but still required is the useful one — the cap is bought as insurance against an unbounded liability, so it has to exist and be visible, not be exercised. See [Prevent AI Billing Surprises With Caps, Notifications, and Rate Limits](prevent-ai-billing-surprises-with-caps-notifications-and-rate-limits.md). (10:19-10:51)
- **A separate lever, easy to conflate.** The same talk says "fewer options is better on pricing," which is about the complexity of the decision rather than its size. Reducing the price and reducing the number of plans both remove friction, but they answer different objections and can be traded independently. (06:31-06:48)
- **Limits.** Every outcome here is directional recollection: "so many more companies," "usage went up over time," "spread like wildfire," with no figures, window, retention, or net revenue comparison against the seat model. The price change is also confounded with competitive entry and with the product's own improvement over the period. And the threshold argument is a claim about enterprise buying behavior in one category — it predicts that lowering price expands scope, not that the resulting consumption revenue exceeds what the seat price would have produced.
