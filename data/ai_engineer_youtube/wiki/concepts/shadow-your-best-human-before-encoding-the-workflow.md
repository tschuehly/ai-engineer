# Shadow Your Best Human Before Encoding the Workflow

Summary: The specification for an agentic workflow is what your best performer actually does, including the tab-switching mess they do it in — so watch them work before designing, pick the most legible workflow to encode first, and expect the payoff to be a higher floor for everyone rather than a higher ceiling for the person you shadowed.

Use when:
- Writing the spec for an agent that automates work an internal team currently does by hand.
- Choosing which of several candidate workflows to automate first.
- Justifying an internal agent project whose ROI is being argued in terms of the top performer's time.

Details:
- The instruction is first in the talk's takeaway list: "before you build, shadow your best human. I talked to many sales reps, and when they opened their computers I saw how many tabs and tools they were navigating between, and that was chaos, but it was also the spec." ([Liu](../sources/20260826_L4I7WgiEquo.md), 19:26-19:47)
- **The failure mode is stated as a one-line rule: "if you encode a mediocre process you get a mediocre agent."** Automation preserves the quality of whatever process it copies, which makes the choice of whom to shadow a determinant of the system's ceiling. (19:47-19:51)
- Ordering rule for what to encode first: "start with the most legible workflow — that's the one that's documented and repeated," paired with "let humans stay in the loop where there are risky possibilities." Legibility and risk are separate axes; the first picks the workflow, the second picks the approval policy. (19:51-20:01)
- What gets extracted is repetition, not judgment: "we shadowed our best reps to capture something that was the most repetitive part of our job and encoded it as a durable multi-agent workflow." The reps keep the human signal-reading their strength was described as — "sussing out human signals during the buying process." (02:42-02:49, 13:04-13:12)
- **The stated goal is distributional.** "This does more than help one rep be productive. Our goal is actually to raise the floor for the entire team": a new rep can "learn the Notion sales process, understand what signals are important to look for, understand which playbooks are effective, and basically know what good follow-up looks like," and ramping reps "can still learn from the patterns of the strongest reps without needing every lesson to be passed down manually." (16:42-17:14)
- Rather than replacing judgment, the encoded workflow removes the blank page: the human "still adds their own judgment and taste and sales secret sauce, but they're no longer starting from a blank slate." (16:31-16:41)
- **Limit.** No measurement supports the floor-raising claim — no ramp-time, per-rep variance, or new-hire comparison is reported, and the two figures given (increased qualified opportunities over 13 weeks, 63% more likely to take the next step) are system-level and not broken out by rep tenure. Nor is any method described for choosing which reps count as best. (18:48-19:14)
- **The gap this closes, named independently, and a cheaper encoding vehicle.** Cloudflare calls it the expert gap — the distance between how an expert rep "would approach a situation... how he would handle a customer satisfaction issue" and how someone ramping would — and encodes expert behavior as curated skill files reviewed by the go-to-market and operations teams rather than by shadowing and building a durable workflow. The tradeoff is fidelity for reach: shadowing captures what the expert does including the parts they cannot articulate, while a reviewed skill file captures only what somebody could write down, and scales to every situation a rep meets rather than to the one workflow that was encoded. ([Joyce](../sources/20260826_Qw_tC68KKes.md), 03:37-04:17, 13:36-14:02)
