# Distribution Is the New Bottleneck for Developer Tools

Summary: Because building software has never been easier, the binding constraint for developer-tool and AI startups has shifted from product to distribution — and AI-generated slop makes cutting through the noise harder. This concept packages a diagnosis (PMF Compass: product signal vs. revenue), a six-step GTM-hygiene foundation, and concrete SF-centric distribution tactics.

Use when:
- A team is over-investing in product polish while attention/pipeline stays flat, and you need to decide whether the next unit of effort belongs on product or distribution.
- Planning a devtool/AI-startup go-to-market motion from scratch and want a checklist of foundational steps before spending on channels.
- Choosing among concrete top-of-funnel tactics (location, events, out-of-home ads, launch stunts) for an early-stage technical company.

Details:
- Framing: "Building software has never been easier, which means the bottleneck has moved. Now the bottleneck is distribution." AI made inboxes and feeds "unnavigable" with so much annoying outreach and generated slop that it's hard to tell the real deal and cut through the noise. (01:43-03:20)
- PMF Compass (Evil Martians framework, derived from 37 successful devtools): two axes, product signal and revenue. Signal > revenue → work on distribution; revenue > signal → work on the product; signal ≈ revenue → go full speed. Nine of ten early-stage devtools have signal stronger than revenue "because technical founders really hate marketing," so distribution is the usual gap. (02:07-03:00)
- Six GTM-hygiene foundations: (1) crystallize the value proposition around a painful problem and validate it with customers *willing to pay*; (2) understand your "shelf space" — where in a developer's life you live — which determines channels; (3) become the authority on the problem; (4) map the ecosystem: befriend/cross-pollinate with friends and know your foes so you can differentiate; (5) distribute early, sometimes before the product is ready. (03:32-04:38)
- Location tactic — come to SF: it's a "network game" and an unfair advantage (David Cramer/Sentry: "SF is unfair… just do it"); staying in SF after YC correlates with fundraising "twice as much, twice as fast"; VCs and clients are here for in-person contact. (04:38-05:52)
- Out-of-home tactic — get loud with banners: SF billboards do two jobs, *signaling* (you exist; you may have invented a category) and earning *mind share* (subconscious memorization). Jason Bosco (Typesense) used billboards as a credibility proxy precisely because, as a non-VC-backed company, they had no funding milestones to signal a "good trajectory"; feedback was "I didn't realize you were this big." (05:52-07:11)
- Events tactic: organize or attend events in any format (meetups, launch parties, breakfasts/dinners, padel/poker/boba, user conferences). Learn from the best — Supabase Select ran a second year; earlier-stage startups increasingly host their own user conferences (Paul Copplestone/Supabase describes an internal "no conference policy" giving way to hosting one). (07:11-08:33)
- Stunt/fun tactic: do fun, "crazy" memorable marketing — "whenever you have fun with your marketing, it really comes through." (See also embracing fail stories in [AI Does Not Replace Shareable Product Marketing](ai-does-not-replace-shareable-product-marketing.md).) (08:37-09:00)
