# Pace Against the Cycle, Not the Day, in AI-Speed Teams

Summary: AI teams run against a news cycle that refills faster than a work week, so the default outcome is burnout rather than sustained output. Three counterweights, from someone who has run hypergrowth twice: make being offline explicitly permitted and treat the inability to go offline as a defect, judge yourself on the cycle rather than the day, and name the emotion out loud so the team can act on it instead of absorbing it.

Use when:
- Setting operating norms for a team shipping against frontier-model release cadence.
- Someone on the team cannot take two days off without the work stopping.
- Leading a team through a shutdown, a competitor launch, or a bad week.

Details:
- The intensity is structural, not perceived. At Instagram the calendar risks were "what is Apple going to announce at WWDC" once a year and "maybe a competitor launches every three or four months." Anthropic's Wednesday all-hands carries a "week in AI" slide that is already full mid-week — "and it's only Wednesday" — with a competitor model, a product launch, and something on regulation. ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 22:11-22:49)
- The permission, stated as a rule with a reason attached: "there's no job that is so important that you can't be offline for a couple of days," because recovery is slow — "I've seen it happen unfortunately to people I'm really close to and then it takes a long time to recover from that." Burnout is expensive on a longer timescale than the sprint that caused it. ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 22:51-23:13)
- The corresponding diagnostic turns the exception into a bug report: if you genuinely cannot go offline, "you're probably doing something wrong and you talk to somebody who could be a mentor to figure out how you can unblock that." A single point of failure in a person is an organizational defect, not a badge. ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 23:19-23:25)
- The anti-cycle framing, borrowed from sport: "you're never as good as your best game and you're never as bad as your worst game." Applied to AI's "it's so over / we're so back" oscillation — "if you internalize that that cycle is always going to be at play in some way, you realize it's never that bad" — with the startup version being Ben Horowitz's "we're effed, it's over" chapter, and the resolution being "it's never just about today's model launch... zooming out and not letting your internal sense of self and success be so driven by the day-to-day." ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 23:26-24:42)
- The leadership practice that follows from a shutdown-heavy operating model: verbalize the emotion first, because "if you're feeling something it's really often the case that other people on the team are feeling it too." He opened a meeting after a Labs initiative was wound down with "I'm really sad, I'm frustrated, I wish this thing had worked out," which "holds the space for other people to be like, yeah, I'm pissed off too" — and only then moves to "great, what are we going to do about it." The order matters: the practical question lands better after the acknowledgement than instead of it. ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 24:50-25:36)
- Limits: personal advice from one leader with no data, and it is advice given by someone with unusual leverage over their own schedule. The structural causes of overwork — headcount, deadlines, equity pressure, the two-week kill cadence itself — are not addressed, and "no job is so important" is asserted rather than demonstrated against an on-call or launch-critical role.
