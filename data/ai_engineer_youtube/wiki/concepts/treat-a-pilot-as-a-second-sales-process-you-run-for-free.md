# Treat a Pilot as a Second Sales Process You Run for Free

Summary: A customer pilot is sold, then staffed at your expense, and then — if it succeeds — sold again from the start. Reserve pilots for your largest opportunities and answer the objection with something cheaper: a reference call, an eval on a slice of their data, a demo against their data that you drive, or a signed contract with an opt-out that moves the validation clock onto the buyer.

Use when:
- Every prospect is asking for a POC and the pipeline is filling with them.
- Deciding whether to grant trial access to a product whose value is hard to demonstrate in a demo.
- Distinguishing a self-serve trial from a supervised proof of concept.
- A pilot has succeeded and the deal has not moved.

Details:
- **The cost structure, named.** "Founders say, 'We are stuck in pilot hell. We've got all of these pilots going on. None of them are converting. They're so much work.' Or if they do convert… I sell the pilot. I invest really heavily in managing the pilot. And then I have to start all over again from scratch once I get a yes to move forward on the sales cycle." Two sales processes, one of them unpaid, with the staffing in between. ([Rosenthal](../sources/20260826_wdTRsfw0KG0.md), 06:48-07:27)
- **The allocation rule.** "Use pilots sparingly. They should be not the default, but the option for your most valuable, highest paying, biggest revenue opportunities." A pilot is a discretionary investment sized against deal value, not a stage in a standard process.
- **The leverage argument.** "As soon as you give someone access to your product, you're giving away a lot of power and leverage in the deal cycle." Once the buyer has access, their urgency drops to zero and yours does not.
- **Four substitutes for the objection.** "You can introduce them to another customer. You can do an eval on part of their data. You can do a demo with their custom data and you do it over Zoom rather than handing them access to it. You can give them an opt-out clause… sign a contract, you have 90 days to opt out if it doesn't meet your needs, and that shifts the onus of trying out the product and validating it onto them cuz the clock is ticking on them now." A fifth is a term trade: "our normal contracts are 3 years, but we'll do a 1-year POC with you." (07:27-08:15)
- **For an AI product, the four substitutes are not interchangeable, and the source does not say so.** The buyer's real question is usually behavioral — does this model do the right thing on *our* data, at *our* distribution, with *our* edge cases. Only "an eval on part of their data" answers that question; the reference call and the seller-driven Zoom demo answer a different one (does it work for someone, does it work when an expert drives it). Choosing a substitute that does not address the buyer's actual uncertainty converts a pilot request into a stalled deal. Run the eval, and see [Size Eval Suites to the Error Rate the Consequence Demands](size-eval-suites-to-the-error-rate-the-consequence-demands.md) for how large a slice has to be to say anything.
- **The opt-out clause is priced as leverage, not as confidence.** "It's not something you do when you're a giant company, but when you're a startup, you can get away with these things." Read honestly, the 90-day opt-out shifts the burden of proof by contract rather than by evidence: the product does not have to demonstrate anything, the buyer has to remember to cancel. It works because inertia favors the incumbent contract, which is the same reason it is unavailable once you are the incumbent. (07:55-08:07)
- **A pilot is not a self-serve trial, and conflating them is the most common error here.** Asked directly how pilot-avoidance reconciles with a self-serve-first motion: "PLG, so people coming and self-serving and just using your product, is very different from a POC, which is usually a contracted period of time where they have access to your product and they have support and resources from your team and you're giving them services and support along the way… self-serve access to your product is totally fine and great and you can stage gate that or limit that with consumption, time box it, or feature gate it… When I talk about POCs, I'm specifically referring to more handheld services-oriented POCs that require a sales engineer or salesperson to supervise." **The distinguishing variable is your staffing, not their access.** Free access with no humans attached is a funnel; access with a supervising sales engineer attached is an unpaid engagement. The three named gates on the funnel side — consumption, time box, feature gate — are what keep it from turning into the second thing. (15:03-16:00)
- **The internal-rollout pilot is a third object again.** [Gate Each Rollout Phase on a Different Question](gate-each-rollout-phase-on-a-different-question.md) uses "pilot" for a small, self-selected internal cohort whose job is to prove accuracy before a wider beta. That pilot has no counterparty and no deal cycle, so none of this page's economics apply to it. The word covers three different things — an internal accuracy stage, a self-serve trial, and a supervised customer engagement — and only the third is the one to ration.
- **Limits, and the asymmetry.** This advice optimizes the seller's control of the deal cycle. The buyer-side reasons for wanting a POC — "I need to validate this works in my environment. It meets my needs" — are treated as objections to negotiate around rather than as requirements, and for a probabilistic product some of them are genuine engineering requirements. Nothing here is measured: no conversion rate for pilots versus substitutes, no cost per pilot, no win rate on opt-out contracts.
