# Audit a Refactor Against Having Waited for Better Models

Summary: Any large cleanup now competes against a real alternative — do nothing and let a better model do it more cheaply next year. The deferral discount is genuine and compounding, but so is the debt, and AI-native development manufactures fresh illegible code faster than the discount arrives. Decide on what the refactor unblocks for the business, not on where model capability will be, and prefer the layered option that isolates part of the codebase over an all-or-nothing choice.

Use when:
- A team is arguing whether to start a migration, consolidation, or rewrite now or wait for the next model generation.
- Justifying cleanup work whose payoff is other work getting faster rather than a shipped feature.
- Post-mortem on a large refactor: deciding what evidence would show it was worth doing.

Details:
- **The question, asked honestly and out loud.** WiseDocs spent six months collapsing more than ten repositories into a monorepo, and the talk exists to ask "should we have waited a year to do this refactor as models and harnesses continue to get better, or did it make sense to do it at the time?" The starting conditions were "we're too slow to meet customer demand," an "AI pipeline that we've built [that] is too complicated to update," and an estate where "nobody actually wants to touch the code. It's not a fun experience." ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 00:27-00:54, 12:30-12:39)
- **The case for waiting, stated by the person who did not wait.** "Models are getting better. They can call tools better. We have a lot more infrastructure like sandboxes and monitoring frameworks in order for us to actually understand what's happening under the hood with these models. So, taking on technical debt and refactoring later is getting exponentially easier as the days go by." This is not a straw man — it is the same capability trend the talk's own re-run benchmark measures, where the same task went from three hours and ten mistakes to essentially one-shot. ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 12:40-13:02)
- **The case against waiting, and it is not "models won't improve."** "When you build a lot of code and you do this kind of development in an AI-native world, it starts looking like some of the legacy code we've seen in the past. There's a lot of code written. It's written with low performance or quality, and the broader problem is people don't actually understand what's happening there. So, if you have some issues within the code base or you want to adjust based on customer requirements, it's actually much harder to do so." The pile you plan to hand the future model is growing while you wait, and it is growing in the specific dimension — nobody understands it — that makes it expensive to change on a customer's schedule. Hence: "you do have to make sure that there are appropriate guardrails, whether or not you do a full refactor or only a partial one." ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 13:03-13:32)
- **The discipline that decides it is ROI on debt, not capability forecasting.** Tech debt is framed as financial debt that "compounds in mysterious and sometimes unexpected ways," but with a test: debt taken on to build a feature or land customers has to clear a return, and "if we introduce additional complexity into our code base, we can very quickly outrun the ROI we've generated." The closing instruction is the same shape — "think about the business value of delivering a big refactor and the tradeoffs of doing it now versus in the future." ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 01:55-02:18, 14:43-14:58)
- **The evidence offered that it was worth doing.** The previous repositories "had been around for more than six years" and moved "pretty slow," attributed to both tech debt and the absence of AI coding tools. Then "within the first six months of the rebuild when we got to parity that we had before, that steepness of that curve is immense. And it didn't slow down after," with feature work continuing past the parity line. Business outcomes: pipeline time reduced, costs reduced, larger files supported, and features "that would take multiple months in under a week." The shape of the claim matters — the curve staying steep *after* parity is what distinguishes a refactor that paid from one that merely finished. ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 10:21-10:53, 13:34-14:09)
- **The social evidence is treated as independent, and it is the part hardest to fake.** "Beyond just shipping velocity, developers actually want to work in this codebase. So, everybody comes along and says, 'Hey, can I work in this codebase? It's much cleaner compared to the other ones.'" This became a reach measure: "now almost every developer within the company is committing to this new mono repo even though it might not be their area of expertise, but they might need to make changes to schemas, API calls, and other parts of the stack," and "a lot of the patterns we have adopted here have spread to other repos within the company." Voluntary cross-area contribution is a codebase-health signal no velocity dashboard produces. ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 11:22-11:35, 14:09-14:28)
- **The decision is not binary.** "The AI delivery system is a layered approach. You can isolate different parts of your codebase to avoid a full refactor, but there's so many components that you need to keep in mind." Partial isolation is the middle option that a now-versus-later framing tends to hide; the wiki's batch-decomposition and playbook material is what makes the partial version tractable. ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 14:28-14:43)
- **Where the deferral logic does not survive contact.** Handed the whole job in one shot, GPT 5.5 extra high "completed its goal in 10 minutes and 22 seconds" and produced scaffolding with the models missing. So the capability that would justify waiting is not the capability being measured by "the model is much better at the refactor task I already scoped" — the unattended, unsupervised, whole-system version is a different and unmet bar, and the talk's own prediction that it arrives "in 6 months" is a forecast, not a result. ([Denys Linkov](../sources/20260808_7vn4WpqNpck.md), 11:44-12:27)
- **Caveats that limit how far this transfers.** The talk measures its own refactor entirely in velocity, reach, and cost — commit rate, contributor count, lines of code (self-flagged as "not a great metric"), pipeline duration, feature lead time — while its opening argument is that AI-era teams ship faster with *degrading* quality. No defect rate, incident rate, or static-analysis figure for the new monorepo appears anywhere, so the talk never tests its own thesis on itself. The refactor's cost is also unpriced: no engineer-months, no token spend, no account of what did not ship during the six months, which is exactly the denominator a reader needs to run the same comparison.
