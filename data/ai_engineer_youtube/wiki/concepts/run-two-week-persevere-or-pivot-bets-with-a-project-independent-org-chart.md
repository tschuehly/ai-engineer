# Run Two-Week Persevere-or-Pivot Bets With a Project-Independent Org Chart

Summary: A research-product group can review every project on a two-week "persevere or pivot" cadence and shut something down almost every cycle — but only if the reporting structure is deliberately not aligned to projects, because otherwise the kill cadence forces a re-org at the same frequency. Teams assemble per bet, the bet lead manages nobody, and structure hardens only after a product gets traction.

Use when:
- Designing a labs, incubation, or applied-research group that is expected to kill most of its work.
- Diagnosing why a team says it wants fast kills but never kills anything.
- Deciding when an exploratory effort should get a permanent team.

Details:
- The cadence: "our whole cadence is two-week reviews where every project goes up for we call it persevere or pivot. So basically every project is up for review and either it's time to keep going, persevering, or it's time to pivot it or even shut down." ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 12:12-12:23)
- The kill rate is the design, not a symptom. "We've shut down projects basically every single one of those cycles and the more you do it, the less it's just like, oh no, my project is shut down. I failed. It's like, no, that is definitely the intention of the labs team is to prototype quickly, try to ship internally, maybe get it to early access, and if it doesn't work, wind it down." Frequency is what removes the stigma; a rare shutdown reads as a verdict on people. ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 12:23-12:44)
- The structural precondition most teams miss: "if you align the org chart too much to the individual projects, you're going to end up re-orging every two weeks, which would be a total nightmare." An org chart that tracks projects silently converts every kill decision into a personnel decision, which is the mechanism by which fast-kill cadences stop killing things. ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 12:44-12:54)
- The unit of work is a *bet*, staffed ad hoc — "somebody from product, somebody from the eng team" — with "the concept of a bet lead or a directly responsible individual. But the interesting thing is that they don't manage usually any of the other people." Disbanding then costs nothing: "let's disband and keep going and it's not a big deal." ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 12:54-13:30)
- Engineering management is not deleted, it is repointed. "The death of the engineering manager discipline has been greatly exaggerated" — coaching, interpersonal work, and personal development stay — and the EM's operational job becomes placement: "make sure every individual is assigned to the thing that they're most excited about and that they're working in the best way possible." ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 11:56-12:12, 13:29-13:38)
- Structure is earned by traction, with the sequence named: Claude Design "started in this ad hoc grouped way," then shipped, then a second release in June, then "we've hired people for that specific team and it has more of a structure. So it's loose until it gets solidified down the line." ([How Anthropic Builds: Lessons from Labs — Mike Krieger, Anthropic](../sources/20260827_qqrk7CtkuIw.md), 13:38-13:58)
- Limits: self-reported by one participant, with no shutdown count, no named failed bet, and no view from anyone whose project was killed. Nothing here establishes that the two-week interval is right rather than merely short, and the model presumes a hiring market and a funding position where disbanded people are immediately re-placeable.
