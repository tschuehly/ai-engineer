# Frontier-Training Know-How Is Apprenticeship, Not Literature

Summary: By one practitioner's estimate fewer than 5,000 people in the world know how to train a frontier model at scale, and that knowledge travels as apprenticeship rather than as published method. Because what they hold is largely a set of configuration choices, it is a body of knowledge that automated search can attack directly — which is a different claim from "AI will replace researchers."

Use when:
- Estimating how transferable a capability is by asking whether its knowledge is written down or passed person to person.
- Deciding whether a hard problem in your organization is a research problem or a tacit-configuration problem.
- Explaining why hiring is the bottleneck on a training project rather than compute or data.
- Evaluating a claim that some expert practice is about to be automated.

Details:
- The estimate and the transmission mechanism: "there's very few people who know how to train frontier models. I would say realistically probably less than 5,000 in the world at scale. I think that type of knowledge, that's a very exploitable search space. And actually as humans, all those configurations, we're not particularly good at. It's kind of like secret knowledge we pass as if… we're apprentices." ([Adaption](../sources/20260812_XEd_SRVHBgU.md), 14:03-14:20)
- The inference worth carrying is the one about *shape*, not scarcity. Knowledge that is transmitted by apprenticeship is usually knowledge that resists being written as a procedure — and when its content happens to be configurations rather than judgment, it is exactly the shape a search can enumerate. Scarcity says the skill is valuable; configuration-shaped says it is attackable.
- The historical frame the speaker builds this on: computer science is "only… 77 years" old, "only two generations of people working on these tools," and its path to the frontier narrowed into "the unreasonably narrow path" — right PhD program, right industry lab, right problem at the right time, and only then "you got to contribute to the frontier." That path is a filter on people, and it doubles as the transmission channel for the tacit knowledge. ([Adaption](../sources/20260812_XEd_SRVHBgU.md), 00:29-02:31)
- The cost of the filter, in her terms: "instead of taking years of training to learn how to build the tools, scientists just skipped the questions," restated as "the reason why people did a computer science PhD was to learn the tools to get to the question, and now you can just get to the question." The loss being described is unasked questions, not unemployed researchers. ([Adaption](../sources/20260812_XEd_SRVHBgU.md), 05:15-05:21, 13:13-13:23)
- The follow-through she draws: "once you automate a lot of that knowledge, you just accelerate innovation cycles. Which means that you can explore and do more questions," and "the cost of asking something informs what is asked" — see [Cost Pressure Silently Shrinks Research Scope](cost-pressure-silently-shrinks-research-scope.md) for the same behavioral law observed independently at the query layer. ([Adaption](../sources/20260812_XEd_SRVHBgU.md), 14:20-14:43)
- The practical test this suggests for your own organization: for a capability only a few people have, ask what they would hand a newcomer. If the answer is a document, the bottleneck is documentation. If the answer is "sit next to me for six months," ask what fraction of what they do is choosing values — because that fraction is searchable, and the rest is what a search will not recover.
- Provenance: the 5,000 figure is an off-the-cuff estimate in Q&A ("realistically probably"), from a founder whose product automates exactly this knowledge, with no source or definition of "at scale." Read it as an order-of-magnitude framing for how thin the practitioner pool is, not as a count. ([Adaption](../sources/20260812_XEd_SRVHBgU.md), 14:03-14:20)
