# Seek the AlphaGo Property So Added Compute Keeps Buying Depth

Summary: What separates a training loop that scales from one that plateaus is whether improving the model makes its own training questions harder. AlphaGo had that property; define-a-dataset-and-train does not, which is why every private-corpus adaptation method saturates. Use it as the design test for a learning loop: if the curriculum is fixed while the model improves, the loop has a ceiling you can predict.

Use when:
- Designing a self-improvement, synthetic-data, or RL loop and deciding whether it can compound.
- Diagnosing why a training loop's returns flattened after the first pass.
- Evaluating a vendor's claim that their system "keeps learning" from your data.

Details:
- The property, named through the canonical example: "I think this is actually the magic behind a lot of successful RL systems like AlphaGo is that AlphaGo makes its own training questions harder by getting better through training." Difficulty is generated by the current policy, so the curriculum tracks capability automatically. ([Engram](../sources/20260812_WiqDvX6isc4.md), 17:35-17:44)
- The generalized target: "one thing that everyone is looking for is a technique that can make models better, which makes them train themselves better. Or like this is maybe a long way of saying self-improvement. Like you sort of generate data and then the model gets a bit better and then you generate better data um recursively." ([Engram](../sources/20260812_WiqDvX6isc4.md), 17:44-18:05)
- The test this yields is cheap to apply to any proposed loop: *does the model's improvement change what it is trained on next?* If the dataset is defined once and the model trains against it, the answer is no and [the synthetic data wall](the-synthetic-data-wall-caps-every-define-then-train-loop.md) applies — "eventually it will learn all the data."
- The counter-evidence is the speaker's own: at Engram's start, "no matter sort of how much data we generate or how much we train, we kind of do plateau because there's this almost like natural upper bound to how much you can learn in one go from D." Generating more synthetic data from a fixed corpus, without escalation, reproduces the plateau. ([Engram](../sources/20260812_WiqDvX6isc4.md), 18:05-18:20)
- The claimed fix is stated and withheld: "it turns out that there are more sophisticated things you can do that make the training gradually harder, that make the model better over time." No mechanism, dataset, or curve is given, so treat this as a research direction the speaker's company is pursuing, not a result. ([Engram](../sources/20260812_WiqDvX6isc4.md), 18:20-18:32)
- What success would look like, in his terms: on your emails, "the model is just generating a better and better internal model of who you are and what you do and what your goals are, what your value function looks like, and it can help you more and more." Note that this describes a *target*, not an observed curve. ([Engram](../sources/20260812_WiqDvX6isc4.md), 18:38-18:53)
- **Where the analogy is weaker than it sounds, and worth stating before borrowing it.** AlphaGo's escalation works because self-play supplies both a fresh opponent and an unambiguous outcome signal — the game decides who won. A private corpus has neither: there is no adversary and no ground truth about whether a generated question was answered correctly. Any loop that claims this property over unstructured documents has to say where its difficulty signal and its correctness signal come from. The talk does not, which is exactly the gap the wiki's [verifiable continual learning](verifiable-continual-learning-prove-each-fix-helps-and-breaks-nothing.md) and [environments as eval data and training substrates](treat-environments-as-eval-data-and-training-substrates.md) pages address from the engineering side.
- Provenance: an aspirational framing from a talk that reports no results, by a researcher whose company is selling progress on this exact problem. The one datapoint offered is a plateau, and the claimed escape is asserted. ([Engram](../sources/20260812_WiqDvX6isc4.md), 00:24-00:39, 18:05-18:32)
