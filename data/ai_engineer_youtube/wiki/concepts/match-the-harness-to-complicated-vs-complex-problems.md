# Match the Harness to Complicated vs Complex Problems

Summary: A durable diagnostic for AI engineering: complicated problems are decomposable, knowable, and predictable and fit a fixed factory harness, while complex problems have interacting adaptive parts whose whole cannot be derived from the parts and need an emergent, probe-sense-respond approach; the expensive mistake is treating a complex problem as merely complicated.

Use when:
- Deciding whether a task should be solved with a fixed pre-engineered harness/workflow or with a self-organizing adaptive multi-agent approach.
- Diagnosing why a well-executed agent system still fails on messy, shifting, multi-party real-world work.
- Framing whether a problem space is stable enough to plan up front or is a "mess" of moving relationships.

Details:
- Two kinds of problems: **complicated** (a clock, a jumbo jet) has passive parts that experts can take apart, analyze, plan, predict, and document — hard but knowable and predictable; **complex** (a flock of birds, a market, a human organization) has parts that constantly interact and adapt, so the whole "can't be derived from analyzing the parts." For complex systems "you don't analyze and plan… instead you probe and sense and respond." (15:56-16:59)
- The expensive mistake: "we treat a complex problem like a complicated one… things fail not due to lack of execution, but because there's a failure in categorizing the problem spaces." Getting the category wrong, not poor execution, is the root failure. (17:00-17:22)
- Underneath the two problem types are two ontologies: a **reductionist/analytical** view (the world is made of stable things; relationships are secondary — "the metaphysics of a factory and of nearly all software": make components, wire them, get a product) vs a **systems/relational** view (processes and relationships come first; a stable thing is "a slow pattern in an ongoing flow… more like a flame than a crystal"). (10:58-12:46)
- **Emergence** is why a complex whole escapes its parts: simple local rules produce a new level of order no part designed. Water is wet though its oxygen/hydrogen are not; a flock arises from three local bird rules ("align with your neighbor, don't crash into them, stay close") — "no single bird has a flock in it. The flock lives in the relationships, not the parts." (12:50-14:25)
- Russell Ackoff's **"mess"**: real situations aren't neat separate problems but "tangles of problems that keep changing," a "system of relationships in motion." You cannot decompose a mess into tidy boxes and solve each independently, which is exactly why a fixed-harness factory approach struggles when it collides with live real-world scenarios. (14:57-15:45, 16:57-17:22)
- The engineering implication: "the factory method is the right answer to a fixed problem and the wrong answer to a moving problem." A fixed harness fits closed, deterministic, complicated systems with a well-defined product; complex, shifting, multi-agent/multi-human/physical-world problem spaces call for an emergent adaptive harness. As AI leaves the sandbox to touch the real world, more problems shift from complicated toward complex. (08:16-10:32, 32:34-33:12)
- Complex systems have three ingredients worth naming when you classify a problem: **diverse** agents ("diversity is the fuel"), **local interaction** (no agent sees the whole), and **recursive adaptation** (agents adapt to the results of their own adapting). They settle into **attractors** (stable states) while changing locally, and "nobody's steering — it's self-organizing." (17:24-19:50)
