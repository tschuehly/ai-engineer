# Mark Which Lines a Human Wrote So Readers Can Budget Attention

Summary: Once every document, pull request, and Slack message is three or four times longer while saying the same thing, readers lose the cues that used to tell them where to concentrate. A cheap convention restores them: open the artifact with a short passage a human actually typed, put the generated text below it, and label the boundary — not as a quality verdict, but as a reading guide that tells the reader where to spend scarce attention and how much slop to expect.

Use when:
- Writing a PR description, design doc, or long Slack message that is mostly model-generated.
- A reader complains that recent documents from your team read as slop.
- Sending generated analysis to someone whose judgment you are specifically asking for.
- Deciding what an "AI disclosure" norm inside a team is actually for.

Details:
- **The problem it solves is inflation, not deception.** "All of a sudden all the design docs and all the Slack messages… the emails have gotten three or four times as long and we've gotten two or three times as many emails and they say basically as much as they did before. So, communication has gotten quite inefficient and some of the markers of like what is high quality and important things versus not so much high quality… has become challenging to navigate." Length used to be a weak signal of effort and therefore of importance; generation broke the correlation and left readers with no substitute. ([Blum](../sources/20260828_5Bn0xro2ol8.md), 04:08-04:42)
- **Why the marking is worth the friction.** "In the age of AI, human attention is a scarce resource… You can't get more human attention. So, where you spend your time and what you're reading… becomes really important." And the label's job stated exactly: "marking what was generated by AI versus what was written by human is really helpful to know how much time you need to spend reading this, and how much slop can you expect in this part of the communication." (12:45-13:37)
- **The convention, concretely.** "Every PR description will start with something like that, something that I wrote by hand. It could be very short[,] that I describe what this is in code and what this is doing. And then the AI description is going to come after that, which is[:] I will probably read it. I will probably edit it to remove some wrong things, but the[y] didn't write every line here, so they should be more suspicious and they should pay more attention to what I wrote in the top and they should override it." Note the two asymmetries encoded in the layout: the human line is *first* so it is read even by someone who stops early, and it is declared *authoritative* over the generated text below. (13:37-14:10)
- **What the failure looked like without it.** Blum sent a senior AI-skeptic engineer an analysis of their PR comments, generated by a model and not marked as such: "they got very upset. They're like, 'I did not expect somebody that I respect this much to send me something that's clearly this sloppy.'" The repair names what the marking would have carried: "this is what I wrote. This is what the AI wrote and I need your feedback on that because I don't have the context to know if it is sloppy or not and that's what I'm asking you for." Unmarked, the artifact read as a claim about quality the sender never intended to make; marked, the same text becomes a request for exactly the judgment the recipient was uniquely able to supply. (14:22-15:28)
- **This is in direct tension with the wiki's existing provenance position, and the tension resolves on purpose.** [Treat slop as a quality failure, not an AI provenance label](treat-slop-as-a-quality-failure-not-an-ai-provenance-label.md) argues that provenance should not be the test — the artifact meets the bar or it does not, whoever wrote it. That remains right *as an acceptance criterion*. This page uses provenance for a different job: allocating the reader's attention before the judgment is made. A label that says "spend less time here" is a routing hint; a label that says "this is acceptable because a human wrote it" is the failure the other page rejects. Both can hold as long as the marking never becomes the gate.
- **It scales down further than most attention proposals.** The wiki's other answers to attention scarcity are infrastructure — [Make Intent and Evidence the Review Surface](make-intent-and-evidence-the-review-surface.md) builds an acceptance-criteria and evidence pipeline, and [Treat Human Attention as the Bottleneck for Agentic Work](treat-human-attention-as-the-agentic-bottleneck.md) proposes delegation layers. This costs one sentence per PR and requires no tooling, which is also why it is fragile: nothing enforces it, and a team that stops writing the top line loses the convention silently.
- **Caveats.** No measurement of anything here: the 3–4x and 2–3x figures are impressions, and no evidence is offered that the convention changed review time, review quality, or how the skeptical engineers responded afterward. The rule also has an unaddressed edge — a human-written top line that summarizes generated content the human did not verify is a label with nothing behind it, which is the same laundering problem in a smaller package.
