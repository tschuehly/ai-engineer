# Read Employee-Built Automations as the Productionization Backlog

Summary: When employees connect their own agents to your internal tool server, each connection is a requirement submitted with a working prototype attached — it states the problem and the attempted solution at once. Treat the connected population as the roadmap: find the shapes that repeat, take the author's prompts, skills, and vibe-coded app, and productionize it for everyone with the same problem.

Use when:
- An internal platform team is prioritizing what to build next and relying on interviews or ticket queues.
- Employees are building shadow automations and the instinct is to shut them down rather than harvest them.
- Deciding what telemetry an internal MCP server should capture beyond invocation counts.

Details:
- **The loop, stated as a compounding effect.** "This compounds because when people go and build their own thing and they go and connect to our MCP, they're basically telling us, 'Here is a problem that I have. Here's how I'm trying to solve this problem.' And we can go and work with them to be like, 'Okay, we can just go and productionize this, distribute this to everybody who probably has similar problems.'" ([Vaziri](../sources/20260826_VjEP0xqTUI0.md), 14:57-15:16)
- **The handoff includes the implementation.** "And they give us the prompts and the skills and the, you know, even applications that they're vibe coding, to just really simplify our ability to go and productionize these use cases." This is what makes the signal cheap to act on: the platform team receives a specification, a working draft, and a validated user in one package. (15:16-15:28)
- **The telemetry is the model's reasoning, not the call count.** The analytics shown are "taking the reasoning generated by the MCP tool calls… that are being executed remotely." Counting invocations tells you which tools are popular; reading the reasoning text tells you what the user was trying to accomplish, which is the part that maps to a product. (14:44-14:56)
- **It requires the tool surface to be shared in the first place.** The signal only exists because employees build against the same server the background agents use ([Expose the Background Agents' Tool Surface to Employees Over MCP](expose-the-background-agents-tool-surface-to-employees-over-mcp.md)). An internal platform that ships only finished applications gets no equivalent stream, because its users cannot express a want except as a request.
- **It is a different instrument from mining chat logs.** [Mine Agent Conversation History to Generate Missing Skills](mine-agent-conversation-history-to-generate-missing-skills.md) reads what people asked an existing agent and failed to get. This reads what people built when they had tools and no product — a stronger signal, because the user has already paid a build cost, which filters idle curiosity out of the sample.
- **The bias in the sample is worth stating.** Only employees capable of wiring up their own agent appear in it, so the backlog it produces is weighted toward technically fluent users. The population with the same problem and no ability to prototype is invisible in this channel and has to be reached another way.
- **Limit.** No count of connected users, automations, or productionized use cases is given; "they're building a ton of them" is the whole of the evidence, and no example of something promoted from an employee prototype to a shipped feature is described. Nor is any review step named between "they give us the prompts and the skills" and distributing it to everybody. (14:41-15:28)
