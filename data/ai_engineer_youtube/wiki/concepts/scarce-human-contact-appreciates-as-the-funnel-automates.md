# Scarce Human Contact Appreciates as the Funnel Automates

Summary: Automating outbound, demos, and security review does not reduce the demand for human contact — it concentrates it. The remaining human steps get more valuable because they are rarer, which makes cheap in-person formats effective again and makes "I just need to talk to somebody" a reliable signal for when to hire.

Use when:
- Deciding when an automated go-to-market motion needs its first salespeople.
- Allocating budget between more automation and in-person channels.
- A fully automated funnel is converting at the top and stalling before close.
- Choosing what to keep human in a process you are otherwise automating end to end.

Details:
- **The hiring trigger is an observation from the machine, not a forecast.** "It's basically when you've gone as far as you can in your sales process and you're really having a lot of companies saying, 'I just need to talk to somebody' — whether it's I need a demo, I just need to look someone in the eye and build the trust here. And you're going to have that as you start to move up market naturally." The machine has to exist first for the trigger to be legible; see [Build the Automated Motion First and Hire Into Its Bottlenecks](build-the-automated-motion-first-and-hire-into-its-bottlenecks.md). ([Rosenthal](../sources/20260826_wdTRsfw0KG0.md), 10:51-11:25)
- **The counter-intuitive claim, named "the revenge of the steak dinner."** "As more and more of this becomes automated — as you have the automated outbound, the automated demo, the automated security checklist — more and more companies are craving in-person time with humans. So, conferences, conference booths work really well, dinners work really well. And they're, by the way, not that expensive to set up… you do still at this moment in time need humans to run those dinners." The economics invert: the channels that were expensive per contact when everything was human become the cheap differentiated ones once everything else is free per contact. (12:20-12:46)
- **The capability that has not automated.** "You're not selling your tool. You're not selling your product. You're selling what that tool or that product does to your champion or your buyer. And this is where I do think humans are still very helpful. They're very good at value selling, and I think AI does still have some ways to go on value selling. So, until we totally crack that, I do think we're going to need those steak dinners." The claimed residual is not information transfer — which automates well — but constructing the buyer's internal case for them. Stated as an assessment, with no evidence and an explicit expiry ("at this moment in time"). (14:08-14:29)
- **Who to hire for it.** "You hire someone who is AI native, who really gets AI, who lives it, who can authentically sell it because they're really bought into it… you want them to practice what they preach, you want them to be efficient." The criterion is that the seller operates the tools rather than merely represents them — the same distinction the wiki records for non-engineering GTM staff, who are trained to be expert operators rather than builders ([Staff Forward-Deployed Engineers Who Run Deals and Build the Deal Tooling](staff-forward-deployed-engineers-who-run-deals-and-build-the-deal-tooling.md)). (11:57-12:20)
- **The attached warning, because the trigger and the trap arrive together.** The demand for human contact appears "as you start to move up market naturally," and the same passage says to resist that pull: a large customer "will take so many of your legal, security, product, engineering, sales resources," so "be really, really picky about who those first big enterprise partners are." Hiring in response to the signal is right; reorganizing the company around the customer that produced it is the error. (11:25-11:57)
- **Compensation follows the same staging.** "Those first few sales hires are usually wired a little bit differently. They're there to build the company. They're there for the equity… you can get away with it really for the first two or three sales hires," after which "you'll want more coin-operated enterprise sellers" who are "very motivated by the cash compensation," and "it's really hard to hire the best enterprise sellers in the market if you don't pay a variable." OpenAI ran for years with no comp plans at all, which the speaker attributes to equity appreciation and explicitly does not recommend generalizing. Design rule: "Simpler is better, but have upside for out-performers." (12:46-14:08)
- **The forward-deployed engineer is the same question one layer down, with an explicit expiry.** Asked how FDEs fit the cycle: "in an ideal world, we wouldn't need them. Eventually, hopefully we get continual learning and these models can just figure out the inner organization and immediately become smart." They are supply-constrained — "something like 55,000 job reqs for forward deployed engineers right now and about 5,000 people who know how to do that job," a figure given with a hedge and no source — and the offsetting benefit is retention: "they make the product really sticky cuz once you've invested in that services element… your customer is unlikely to go anywhere soon because they feel like they've got a customized tool that works uniquely for them." Both the dinner and the FDE are framed as human steps whose necessity is expected to shrink. (16:00-17:10)
- **Limits.** Nothing here is measured — no conversion, cost per meeting, or pipeline attribution for dinners or conference booths against automated channels, and the claim that companies are "craving" in-person time is an impression. The value-selling gap is asserted rather than tested, and is exactly the kind of claim that dates quickly.
