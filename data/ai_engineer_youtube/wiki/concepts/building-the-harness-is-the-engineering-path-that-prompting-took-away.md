# Building the Harness Is the Engineering Path That Prompting Took Away

Summary: The "developer becomes a conductor" framing costs you the engineers who identify as technical, because prompting and spec-writing do not feel like engineering. Harness and loop work reopens a genuinely programmatic path — you are building tooling *for* the agent — and that is what pulled resistant developers back in, including the ones angriest about agent output.

Use when:
- Engineers on the team say some version of "we didn't sign up for writing prompts."
- Deciding what to hand a skeptic: a better model, an enablement session, or a piece of the harness.
- Choosing how to describe agentic work in a role definition without describing away the craft.
- An adoption program has plateaued on people who are technically strong and visibly unenthused.

Details:
- **The rejection, in the developers' own terms.** "The narrative that I heard a lot is: the developer eventually becomes more of a conductor and an orchestrator of agents… Now, what I've seen is that a lot of developers told me, 'We didn't sign up for this. We didn't sign up for better prompting, writing better specs. We're engineers. We're technical.' And that creates friction, like, is this the role that we really want to do?" ([Debois](../sources/20260822_zCJtYuqwm7E.md), 02:39-03:21)
- **Context engineering was a partial answer that did not close it.** Testing, evaluating, distributing and optimizing prompts added "a little bit of engineering, but still a lot of developers kind of felt empty just working with a prompt and a specification as such." (03:21-03:41) That matters because context engineering is the wiki's most-recommended response to prompt-cycle work, and here it is reported as insufficient *for this particular complaint*.
- **What actually worked, and why it is not the same as more autonomy.** "When we started introducing harness and loops… a new technical path opened. All of a sudden, we were helping the agent with tooling, building tooling for the agent, and that kind of reignited some of the developers who kind of felt that it wasn't for them… 'Yes, we can do this. We have that knowledge.'" His reading is about where the craft went, not about output quality: "where we say abstraction, abstraction, abstraction, technically, all of a sudden, the craft created some new location for more engineering stuff to go to." (03:41-04:24)
- **The corollary: point the angriest people at the context and the harness.** "These are really great people to engage in creating better context for the agent because you tell them, 'Please improve. Please put all your knowledge to improve the result of the agent.' And the same with the harness… if you have those kind of more resistant people that complain about the quality that things were produced by just the vanilla coding agent, use almost that anger, use that skepticism to make it better." (04:24-05:08)
- **The mentality shift this sits inside.** "Stop fixing the code that the agent produced, but improve the system," which he traces to swyx's "stop building the thing, but build the thing that builds the thing." Applied to a person rather than a codebase, that is the whole move: the engineer who was rewriting agent output is now editing the thing that produced it. (05:08-05:53)
- **How this compares with the wiki's other remedy for the same cost.** [Reduced Developer Agency Is an Adoption Cost, and Planning Is Its Remedy](reduced-developer-agency-is-an-adoption-cost-and-planning-is-its-remedy.md) diagnoses the same injury — the engineer reduced to waiting on output — and relocates the craft *upstream*, into a week of detailed planning where the human makes all the decisions. Debois relocates it *sideways*, into building the machinery. The two remedies are not interchangeable, because they appeal to different people: planning returns authorship over the product, harness work returns programming as an activity. An engineer who objects that prompting is not engineering has already rejected the planning answer, since a plan is a document. Offering both is cheap; assuming one covers the other is what leaves a skeptic unaddressed.
- **How it compares with the wiki's other route through skeptics.** [The Best Engineers Adopt Agents Last, and Their Objections Are the Roadmap](the-best-engineers-adopt-agents-last-and-their-objections-are-the-roadmap.md) treats the skeptic's complaints as a prioritized defect list and hands them the roadmap; the payoff is that the fixes lighten their own load. Debois hands them the *work* rather than the roadmap, and the payoff he names is different — the job becomes technical again. Read together they suggest a sequence rather than a choice: their complaints say what to build, and building it is the assignment that engages them.
- **Caveats.**
  - No numbers of any kind. "Reignited some of the developers" is the entire evidence; no count, no proportion, no report of who stayed unconvinced.
  - It is a claim about a population that can program the harness. Nothing here helps with a developer whose objection is to the output quality itself rather than to the shape of the work, and nothing addresses non-engineering roles facing the same prompt-cycle collapse.
  - The talk also asserts, without support, that harnesses will commoditize ([Assume the Harness Commoditizes and Differentiate on the Organization](assume-the-harness-commoditizes-and-differentiate-on-the-organization.md)). If that is right, the technical path this page describes is a temporary one, and the identity it restores has a shelf life the talk never prices.
