# Commoditize the Layer You Do Not Win On

Summary: Publishing a design you cannot win with can be cheaper than defending it, because a shared standard lets suppliers consolidate production and the price collapse reaches you too. The Open Compute Project is the worked example; the transferable rules are that the industry standardizes on what it can *build on*, not on what is best, and that once it has standardized, marginal quality improvements do not pull it back.

Use when:
- Deciding whether to open a component, format, protocol, or model that is not the thing your business actually wins on.
- Arguing that an ecosystem's price floor, not a single vendor's price list, sets your long-run cost.
- Assessing whether a "best in class but proprietary" option will survive a "good enough and buildable-on" alternative.
- Judging how urgent a standards fight is, given that standardization is path-dependent.

Details:
- **The precedent.** "In 2011, Facebook was just getting started on building out distributed computing infrastructure and data centers. But by the time they built it, Amazon and Google already beat them to the punch. So it wasn't a competitive advantage. So Mark said, 'All right, let's just open source it and see what happens.'" They published "the designs for their data centers and their servers and their networking and cooling racks and all the physical hardware… schematics and CAD files and everything," as the Open Compute Project. ([Rizwan](../sources/20260807_CoEIs6Xm8m8.md), 11:06-11:44)
- **The mechanism, which is a supply-side one.** "Before Open Compute, every company designed its own proprietary servers. So manufacturers were doing small production runs of very custom hardware which was expensive. But when Facebook's designs became this sort of shared open standard, suddenly everyone was ordering the same thing and manufacturers could do these massive standardized production runs, commoditizing these components." The consequence: "no single vendor could charge a premium and the price of everything came down for the whole industry including Facebook itself… by giving these designs away they created the market that drove their own costs down and saved them billions of dollars down the road." (11:44-12:23) The release was not altruism and not a marketing move; it changed the manufacturing batch size, and batch size set the price.
- **The precondition is the part that is easy to skip.** Facebook opened the design *after* concluding it was not a competitive advantage. The rule generalizes as a question rather than a policy: is this layer one where we expect to win, or one where our cost is set by how fragmented the supply side is? Opening a layer you do win on gives the advantage away; keeping a layer you cannot win on closed just means you pay bespoke prices forever.
- **The adoption rule.** "The industry will adopt and standardize on something that they can build on top of even if it isn't the best thing." (12:23-12:32) Buildability — availability, license, absence of a gatekeeper, freedom to modify — outranks quality in the adoption decision, and the wiki's [Own Open Models for Sovereignty and Permissionless Adoption](own-open-models-for-sovereignty-and-permissionless-adoption.md) supplies the concrete version: a custom license can cost a sovereign buyer roughly 18 months of legal review, and moving to Apache 2.0 is what unlocks adoption, independent of capability.
- **Standardization is path-dependent, which is what makes it a timing question.** Rizwan's warning to the American labs: "if the foreign models become the standard there won't be a reason to switch back to GPT or Claude or Gemini no matter what the marginal improvements are." (14:31-14:50) The asymmetry is between the cost of adopting a standard once (low, because everything is already built for it) and the cost of leaving one later (high, because the tooling, the prompts, the harnesses, and the hosting all assume it). A capability lead is a depreciating asset against an installed base.
- **The cost trajectory he uses to argue the layer is commoditizing anyway.** Nearly $3 trillion of capex and over 100 gigawatts of new data-center capacity by 2030, "roughly doubling global capacity today"; hosting providers such as Baseten and Fireworks competing on "dedicated hardware and caching and batching volume tricks and inference specialized silicon"; and an estimate that inference on a trillion-parameter model will cost "90% less than it does today" by 2030. (12:34-13:19) The cloud analogue: Google cut compute 32% and storage 68% at GCP Live in 2014, AWS matched within days on its 42nd price cut, and "from 2015 onwards, once raw compute and storage were a commodity, the hyperscalers stopped competing on it as much and started competing on other things like databases and serverless." (13:28-13:58) The pattern worth carrying is the *second* half: commoditization does not end competition, it relocates it one layer up. Plan for where your differentiation moves, not for holding the layer.
- **The unstated gap in the analogy, and the speaker's own hedge.** Server schematics describe an artifact that manufacturers reproduce; model weights *are* the research output, so releasing them costs a lab something Facebook's CAD files did not cost it. Rizwan addresses this only briefly — published weights let others "extract the traces and train your copycat models on them more easily, but not in a way that can leapfrog," and he explicitly separates weight release from opening research: "I don't mean we need to open source our research. I think that's what gives us the lead." (15:19-15:45) That distillation-does-not-leapfrog claim is asserted, not evidenced, and it is the load-bearing joint of the whole argument. The related distinction the wiki already draws is [Customization Control Is a Separate Question From Open Weights](customization-control-is-a-separate-question-from-open-weights.md): weight distribution, adaptation rights, and deployment locality travel separately, and an argument about one does not settle the others.
- Provenance: a founder keynote three days after launching an open-weights subscription plan, so the conclusion is the speaker's commercial position. The Open Compute history and the 2014 cloud price cuts are checkable; the 2030 projections are unattributed "estimates"; the geopolitical framing around keeping a national lead is advocacy rather than a finding.
- **The buyer-side corollary: rent the layers you do not win on, and keep exactly the one you do.** Notion's lean GTM engineering team names orchestration, email, CRM, and enrichment as bought — "vendors do that really well" — and the context layer as non-negotiable, "because that's where our edge is." The same reasoning that makes a supplier publish a design it cannot win with makes a customer decline to build a mail sender: the decision is about where the differentiation lives, not about capability. ([Liu](../sources/20260826_L4I7WgiEquo.md), 08:44-09:00, 17:52-18:17)
