# Use Design Partner Evidence To Support Early AI Startup Scale

Summary: Before revenue exists, design partners, customer interviews, ICP hypotheses, and pricing hypotheses can make an AI startup's path from prototype to large business more credible.

Use when:
- Turning early customer discovery into fundraising evidence.
- Evaluating whether an AI startup pitch explains how a wedge can become a company.

Details:
- Investors may ask whether an early product is a company or only a feature; the useful answer is a path from the current wedge to a much larger product and business. 19:33-20:08
- Design partners are useful because they show market evidence before the product is fully built; founders can also cite customer interviews or research if "design partner" is too strong a label. 18:21-19:10
- Early revenue narratives can be hypotheses grounded in buyer conversations, likely ACV ranges, and whether customers expect seat-based or usage-based pricing. 18:21-18:52, 24:27-25:00
- Investors will ask about the ideal customer profile; even uncertain ICP hypotheses should include how the founder will test and narrow them. 24:04-24:26
- Pitch meetings should be bidirectional and concise: answering every question with a long monologue can be worse than showing that the founder has thought about the issue and can go deeper when useful. 20:20-22:17
- **How to pick them, from the operator side — and one tactic worth reading critically.** Rosenthal separates the first ten customers from any scalable motion: "your first 10 customers are going to be different. They're going to be really more design partners… they're going to need to be much more handheld," so automated outbound tooling is the wrong instrument for them. Two selection criteria follow. Prefer warm sourcing — "ideally, they're relationships you already have, either companies you've worked with or your investors are introducing you… cuz you want these to be very friendly processes and experiments in the beginning" — and sell the slot on scarcity: "it's sort of a gift to a company to let them be your design partner. There's exclusivity to it, there's scarcity to it." The third is stated plainly and should be weighed rather than adopted: "go to a company that has a great logo, but work on their internal product or some sort of product that's not their main production, so that they're willing to take a little bit more risk with you and bypass some security processes, but then you get the logo on your site." That is advice to route around a buyer's controls, and it makes the resulting logo attest to a workload chosen for being low-stakes — which is exactly the caveat an investor or a later customer should apply to it. ([Rosenthal](../sources/20260826_wdTRsfw0KG0.md), 17:10-18:45)
