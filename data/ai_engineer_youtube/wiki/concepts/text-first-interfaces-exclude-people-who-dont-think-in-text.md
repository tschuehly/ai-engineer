# Text-First AI Interfaces Exclude People Who Don't Think in Text

Summary: Working with AI today is overwhelmingly reading and writing prose, which is an access constraint rather than a neutral default. Keegan McCallum (uRun) argues that once continuous video generation costs dollars per hour instead of dollars per clip, a visual medium becomes affordable as an *interface* — the goal being to give people who think visually the experience engineers already get from coding models.

Use when:
- Deciding whether a non-text modality in an AI product is a feature, an accessibility requirement, or a market expansion.
- Justifying investment in visual or generated-media output beyond content production.
- Evaluating who a chat-shaped product is implicitly excluding.

Details:
- The constraint stated plainly: "working with AI involves a lot of reading and a lot of text. For some people that's more difficult. For some people they just don't think in text. They think visually and learn better that way" (04:31-04:50). Two distinct populations are named — people for whom reading is harder, and people whose thinking is not verbal — and the source does not separate their needs further.
- The benchmark he sets is not "add pictures" but parity of experience: "more opportunities to have companions or visual mediums that are going to allow more people to experience the things a lot of us have with coding models" (04:50-05:02). The reference class is the tight, high-agency loop engineers get from a coding agent, not an illustrated chat transcript.
- The economic precondition is what makes this a 2026 argument rather than a perennial wish: $10 buys ~3 hours of continuously generated video and $50 buys ~15 (03:34-03:52). A visual medium priced per clip is content production; priced per session-hour it can be an interface.
- The consumer-facing shape he demonstrates is video-to-video on a live webcam — "you could ask to see yourself in any outfit… in a car you like or with a haircut you're considering… these are open-ended models that can transform what they're seeing on a webcam in real time" (03:52-04:31). Note what makes this an interface rather than a generator: the input is the user's own live situation, so there is nothing to describe in words.
- Caveats: this is an aspiration inside a vendor talk, with no user research, no accessibility evaluation, and no claim about which specific needs are met. It also inherits the medium's own defects — generated video is strong per frame and weak on session consistency, so an interface built on it fails in ways a text interface does not.
