## Summary

An eighteen-minute talk (about thirteen minutes of content, five of Q&A) by a co-founder of Exa — "a search engine for agents… this web MCP web tool that agents can access," which he says powers Cursor and Cognition (00:40-00:58) — aimed at a room he checks is technical before starting (00:24-00:32). The thesis is that go-to-market is now an AI engineering problem, and the talk is a tour of what one 115-person company built to make that literal: two internal interfaces, two classes of internal agent, and three principles. Nothing in it is measured.

**The framing.** He treats the product-versus-distribution argument as settled by both being required: the Twitter position that "product is the only thing that matters" or "distribution is the only thing that matters" — with Glean named as the example people argue over — gets the answer "you just kind of have to do both… If you don't do both things, then you don't have a company" (01:12-01:50). He names the engineer's failure mode as his own: the bias is "I'm going to just build the thing… make it really really freaking good," and "that's the bias we had when we started [Exa] and we were honestly pretty bad at go-to-market. We were not doing enough marketing, we were not doing enough sales" (01:56-02:18). What changed by 2026 is that the work is automatable — "you can treat go-to-market like an engineering problem and particularly an AI engineering problem… you can do so much as one person" (02:21-02:41).

**Go-to-market is a data problem.** The list of GTM tasks — research your customers, research your targets, find information about targets, find the right people at particular companies, build POCs (02:56-03:22) — has a "grand unifying theme": "go-to-market is a data problem" (03:25-03:30). His proposal for the shape of that data is the durable line: "you need basically a live model of your world that agents can act on" (03:53-03:58). The model spans internal data ("information that you know about your customers, about people that are at your company… data about how people use the product") and external data ("there's over 60 million companies in the world, and there's like billions of people, like over a billion that are on LinkedIn… and all this news"), and the design instruction is to keep all the available sources in mind because they are what is "available to your agents" (04:01-04:42).

**Interface one: the ICP dashboard.** It "answers the question, like what is our world? Like what is the world of customers and use cases that we care about," and the method is exhaustive rather than sampled: "we just classify basically like every possible company that is inside of our total addressable market" (05:22-05:56). The demo shows a category breakdown — model providers, AI coding platforms "like say Cursor," go-to-market intelligence tools — with per-category revenue blurred out, "an understanding of literally like almost every company within those segments," and per-company deep dives (SpaceX is the example) showing "how much annual spend we could anticipate them to have" plus company metadata (05:56-06:28). The mechanism is his own product turned inward: "we take the internet, we crawl it, we train embeddings to do web search really well. And so basically from a technical perspective you can think about Exa as like embeddings over the internet. And when you have embeddings over the internet you have this like arbitrarily powerful semantic filtering and slicing and dicing of any type of data that you want" (06:29-06:58).

**Interface two: Request Lens.** "Anytime something significant happens with any of our customers, we're alerted. Someone signed up, someone used a ton of searches, someone stopped using searches, someone showed up that we really really care about. All these things are signals that we are notified about and that our team can act on" (07:02-07:27). One of the four triggers is an absence.

**Agent class one: a dozen shared agents in Slack.** "Our go-to-market team is crazy crazy crazy deep on agents… you could look at some of their like Devin spend and like other agent spend. It's really freaking high," because "everybody on our go-to-market team is constantly asking agents about our customers." Account executives build demos with them. "We have like maybe a dozen different agents inside of our Slack and anybody can use any of them. They all have access to tons and tons of our internal data" (07:34-08:20).

**Agent class two: Jeffbot, a clone of the speaker.** Built over a week off in Mexico with Opus 4.5 (08:23-08:52). Three components. A measured voice: "analyze like 760 of my emails to figure out what my email voice is. Like, oh, I use 18 words on average per email and I like to end emails with best and not sincerely" (08:52-09:09). A judgment calibration: "I made a decision-making framework where I analyzed hundreds of decisions I've made in the past… I created evals from those decisions and calibrated this agent system to behave like myself" (09:10-09:27). And access: "I gave it like read and write access to all the data that I personally have… I basically have access to every single system at the company… so this thing has access to like everything" (09:29-09:47). The shared capability is narrower than the owner's: "anybody at the company can use Jeffbot to create drafts of Slack messages that are basically like answers or decisions that are made… our go-to-market team uses it to like draft emails" (09:47-10:00). Asked later where the decisions came from, since "usually people don't save their decisions," he answers Slack and email: "a surprisingly large amount of everything that goes on a company is on Slack, so if you just read like a ton of Slack history, you can definitely find hundreds of decisions that you made in the past" (13:36-14:08).

**Three principles.** *Agent-first requires API-first:* "if there did not exist really good APIs on top of any internal and external data we'd be out of luck… If you don't have really good APIs your agents are not going to be able to have data access. So you can think about this as MCP, CLI, whatever… You just need some interface that's programmatic" (10:25-10:56). *Not everything should be a chatbot:* dynamic user interfaces are "amazing" and a model can "produce a new UI for any use case… just to answer a question, it could produce like an HTML markdown file. But I think there is something really nice about being able to visit the same consistent UX for the same use cases over time so that you can like learn how to use some tool," so "crystallized UIs and arbitrarily powerful flexible chat agents are both important components of being agent first" (10:57-11:51). *Buy versus build is a false dichotomy:* "we don't live in a world where the choice is between purchasing SaaS and building things yourself… you should just be using something that is arbitrarily customizable." Building it yourself qualifies trivially; a purchased system qualifies "if you can make that SaaS work on your behalf." The worked example is Salesforce, kept because "it's a really good database. It's made a lot of amazing choices around what sales should look like, choices that we don't want to make ourselves. And then it exposes MCP. So all of our agents have access to Salesforce MCP." His summary: "infinite customizability is really the highest order bit" (11:53-13:16).

**Q&A: security and staffing.** On the objection that Jeffbot with the founder's privileges available to everyone implies one security level, the answer is caller-scoped: "when I use Jeffbot and I call Jeffbot [it] has access to a ton of systems and it can do reads and writes. However, when anybody else calls Jeffbot all it can do is draft messages, and also I don't give Jeffbot permissions to all of our MCPs and tools in the case where other people call it" (15:58-16:58). On the GTM org: account executives who run deals, SDRs for demand generation, and a separate forward-deployed engineering org. Everyone outside FDE "has learned how to use AI really well… they're not generally vibe coding these interfaces that we have, but they're using the tools really really well," supported by training sessions; the FDE org "does a lot of the maintenance and feature building of these AI systems," so they are "both running deals and supporting deals but then also making everything smoother by doing sales but then also building the sales system" (14:09-15:56). The origin story he gives for that role: Palantir coined FDE while other tech companies had solutions engineers, sales engineers, and AEs, and "the thing that has changed is that because of AI, as a technical person that is supporting revenue generation, you can actually not only support the revenue generation, but then very easily build the tooling to smooth everything over… Before that was like two jobs, and now it's like one job" (17:04-18:07). The scale, and his own hedge on it: about eight or nine FDEs at a company of about 115 people, and "will it scale such that everyone does everything? Probably not. But at least right now that's what we have, and I think that's a really good working model to get pretty far" (18:07-18:31).

## Provenance and Limits

- Nothing in the talk is measured. There is no pipeline, conversion, win-rate, coverage, precision, or cost figure anywhere. "Our go-to-market team is very lean, but very productive" (10:08-10:12) is the only outcome claim, and it carries no numbers on either side.
- The ICP dashboard's revenue columns are deliberately blurred in the slide, and the method behind "how much annual spend we could anticipate them to have" is never described. Nor is any accuracy claim made about classifying "basically every possible company" — no sample, spot check, or error rate.
- Jeffbot's evals are the strongest methodological claim in the talk and the least verified: he says he created evals from hundreds of past decisions and calibrated the system against them, and reports no pass rate, no baseline, no held-out set, and no measure of whether the drafts other people receive are actually accepted or edited.
- The voice statistics (760 emails, 18 words average, "best" not "sincerely") are reported as derived facts about his own corpus; no claim is made that matching them improves anything.
- "Over 60 million companies in the world" and "over a billion on LinkedIn" are his figures, given without sources.
- This is a single company's self-report, and the classification substrate is the speaker's own product (Exa). Both the ICP dashboard and the "arbitrarily powerful semantic filtering" claim are demonstrations of Exa by Exa.
- The staffing model is reported as current and explicitly not projected to scale: eight or nine FDEs at ~115 people, with "probably not" as his own answer to whether everyone will keep doing everything.
- The security answer is a description of intent and configuration, not of enforcement. No mechanism is named for how the caller's identity constrains Jeffbot's tools, and no injection, exfiltration, or misuse scenario is discussed.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "X.ai" at 02:12 and 12:53-12:55 is Exa, the speaker's own company (he is introduced as its co-founder and describes launching it in mid-2023); "Jeff Bots" is Jeffbot; "four deployed engineering organization" is forward-deployed engineering; "devin spend" is Devin, the Cognition coding agent. Left as heard: "even though it's way worse than like Fable or whatever" (05:06), his comparison of GPT-4 to a current model; "I was in Puerto No, I was in Mexico" (08:42) is a self-correction, recorded as Mexico.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Treat Go-to-Market as a Live Model of Your World That Agents Act On](../concepts/treat-go-to-market-as-a-live-model-of-your-world.md) - the talk's central reframe: GTM is a data problem, and the deliverable is a queryable joined model of internal and external data rather than a set of research tasks.
- [Classify the Whole Addressable Market Instead of Searching It Account by Account](../concepts/classify-the-whole-addressable-market-instead-of-searching-it.md) - the ICP dashboard classifies "basically every possible company" in the TAM, which is a different operation from per-account lookup and is what embeddings over a corpus make affordable.
- [Alert on Account Change Events, Including the Ones That Are Absences](../concepts/alert-on-account-change-events-including-absences.md) - Request Lens fires on signup, usage surge, usage stop, and arrival of a watched account, one of which is a non-event.
- [Derive an Agent Persona From a Measured Corpus, Not a Described Tone](../concepts/derive-an-agent-persona-from-a-measured-corpus-not-a-described-tone.md) - 760 emails yield 18-word average length and a sign-off convention, replacing adjectives about voice with statistics from the person's own output.
- [Mine Chat History for Past Decisions and Turn Them Into Judgment Evals](../concepts/mine-chat-history-for-past-decisions-and-turn-them-into-judgment-evals.md) - hundreds of decisions recovered from Slack and email become the eval set an agent's judgment is calibrated against.
- [Scope a Person-Cloned Agent by Caller, With Drafts as the Shared Capability](../concepts/scope-a-person-cloned-agent-by-caller-with-drafts-as-the-shared-capability.md) - the same agent runs with owner read/write and full tools for the owner and draft-only with reduced tools for everyone else.
- [Crystallize the UI for Repeated Use Cases and Generate It for Novel Ones](../concepts/crystallize-the-ui-for-repeated-use-cases-and-generate-it-for-novel-ones.md) - the argument against generating every interface is user learning, not cost: a stable surface can be learned once and reused.
- [Replace Buy-Versus-Build With Arbitrary Customizability](../concepts/replace-buy-versus-build-with-arbitrary-customizability.md) - the decision axis is whether the system can be customized programmatically, which a bought system exposing MCP satisfies.
- [Staff Forward-Deployed Engineers Who Run Deals and Build the Deal Tooling](../concepts/staff-forward-deployed-engineers-who-run-deals-and-build-the-deal-tooling.md) - AI collapsed the solutions-engineer and internal-tooling-builder roles into one, with non-technical GTM staff trained as heavy tool users rather than as builders.

## Notes

- Exa is described as "a search engine for agents… this web MCP web tool that agents can access," powering Cursor and Cognition (00:40-00:58) — the same company whose neural-search argument is already in this wiki from an earlier talk.
- The product-versus-distribution debate is treated as a false choice with a named example: "is Glean going to succeed because they're really good at distribution, but what the heck is their product?" against "the product needs to be super good cuz agents shop for the product" (01:12-01:35).
- He dates his own interest to GPT-4 and Exa's mid-2023 launch: even then the model "could actually just automate entire parts of go-to-market," which is why the GTM stack was built agent-first from the beginning (04:53-05:16).
- The internal/external split he draws is the operative one for building the world model: internal is customers, colleagues, and product usage; external is companies, people, and news (04:01-04:30).
- The ICP categories shown are self-descriptive of Exa's market: model providers, AI coding platforms, go-to-market intelligence tools (06:03-06:08).
- "Embeddings over the internet" is offered as the technical summary of Exa, with "arbitrarily powerful semantic filtering and slicing and dicing of any type of data" as the capability it buys (06:41-06:53).
- The Salesforce decision is a rare explicit statement of what buying buys: "it's made a lot of amazing choices around what sales should look like, choices that we don't want to make ourselves" (12:58-13:04).
- On the "does everything need to be a chatbot" question, his answer is that both matter — "having crystallized UIs and then also arbitrarily powerful flexible chat agents are both important components of being agent first" (11:43-11:51).
- The non-technical GTM staff are explicitly not vibe coding the internal interfaces; the company invests in training sessions so "people really understand how to use these tools" (15:12-15:28).
- He calls the FDE arrangement "kind of a funky thing we have going on" (15:53-15:55) rather than a settled model, and gives the ratio (about eight or nine FDEs, ~115 people) only when pushed for it (18:20-18:31).
