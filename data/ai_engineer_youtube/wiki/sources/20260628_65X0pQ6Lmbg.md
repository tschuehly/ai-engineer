## Summary

Allen Pike (Forestwalk Labs) argues, following Andrej Karpathy, that the pinnacle of AI UX is voice-in, visuals-out: voice is the highest-bandwidth human *input* (more words per minute, more meaning per word — "okay" vs "okay"), while rich visuals are the preferred *output* now that models can generate HTML, interactive controls, diagrams, and illustrations via tool calling. The core engineering payoff of this asymmetry is latency: full voice-in/voice-out conversation demands a punishing ~200 ms response, but the visual response envelope is far more forgiving (~1 s on screen still feels seamless), so you can ship delightful responsive experiences today without novel 200 ms-slice architectures. Pike shares three concrete techniques for staying inside that envelope: use a latency-prioritized fast model (Haiku-class, not GPT-5 mini whose P95 hit 5-10 s despite being small and cheap) and hand heavier work off asynchronously to a larger thinking model that interleaves back; fire inference eagerly every 1-2 s as the user talks instead of waiting for end-of-speech silence; and run a stable prefix-caching regimen (keep ~90% of the context identical request-to-request, minimize output tokens) for up to 90% cheaper/faster turns.

## Notes

- Karpathy's argument (referenced): voice is the human-preferred input, visuals the preferred output. A third of the brain processes visual information; models can now generate rich HTML, interactive controls, and illustrations via tool calling, lifting the ceiling on the visuals-out half. 00:17-01:29
- Voice's input value: more words per minute than typing *and* more conveyed per word ("okay" vs "okay"); when something is important we jump on a call for high-bandwidth communication. Most people's prior voice experience (Siri, ChatGPT voice mode) was "slow and dumb," souring them on voice input. 01:35-04:00
- Forestwalk example: a voice agent lives in the team's calls. Pike mentioned a suspected Slack-integration bug, his co-founder confirmed it, he said "let's file that as a Linear issue," and the agent replied within a second that it had done so — taking action on intent without being interruptive. 04:10-05:00
- The tyranny of latency: since the '60s, ~100 ms feels instant; ~1 s is the limit before people lose their train of thought; full voice-in/voice-out conversation needs ~200 ms or less to feel conversive. Doing a network round-trip + STT + inference + network in 200 ms is a huge ask. 05:22-07:00
- Novel-architecture workaround (that you can avoid): Thinking Machines / Neolab demoed time-slicing into 200 ms chunks with a model doing continuous inference in/out at 200 ms slices — but switching to voice-in/visuals-out gives the forgiving visual envelope (something on screen within ~1 s feels seamless) without waiting for such architectures. 07:20-08:20
- Technique 1 — fast model on a latency-prioritized platform: small isn't enough. GPT-5 mini was cheaper but showed 5,000 ms latencies, 7,000 P95, sometimes 10,000 ms; Haiku is much better on P95. Use a Haiku-class (or small open-source) model with short context for the real-time turn, and hand heavier work off asynchronously to a larger thinking model whose results the fast model re-interleaves. 08:32-10:25
- Technique 2 — short inference intervals: don't burn budget waiting for a full second of silence to detect end-of-turn; send inference eagerly every 1-2 s as the person talks, even when unsure they've stopped, so on-screen output keeps pace. 10:30-11:35
- Technique 3 — stable caching regimen: lean into prefix caching (up to 90% cheaper/faster) by keeping the first ~90% of the context window identical from request to request and varying only the final ~10%, and minimize output tokens for fast, affordable turns. Applies to both long-running and frequently-running agents. 11:35-12:40
