## Summary

Jack Morris, a researcher at the startup Engram (launched the week before the talk), frames the problem his company works on and then walks the field's current answers to it, saying explicitly where each one stops. The frame is Terence Tao on breadth against depth: AI "knows like every single public mathematical topic" and can bridge gaps in the literature "in a way that no human even can know because it's read so much," but it lacks the depth of "a graduate student who spent 5 years practicing in one area that gets this… almost like subconscious intuition for the problem space" (01:14-02:07). Scaling compute on context is, in his words, "the pursuit of depth in AI" (02:21-02:28). The reason the depth is missing is structural rather than incidental: the three axes that produced the entire deep learning revolution — more data, more compute, bigger models — all run on public data, and so does the post-training layer bought from data-acquisition vendors, "still by definition creating publicly available data because it's something that the model could tell to a user" (05:05-06:04). Models therefore keep getting better at coding "in the way that is public on GitHub" and at math "in ways that are written in public textbooks," but "they're not getting more knowledge of you or your life or your work" (07:03-07:15).

Against your own corpus two of the three axes are gone — you cannot manufacture more of your own emails, and nobody is training from scratch on them — which leaves compute as the only axis you can still push (07:17-08:11). Morris states the problem formally as a box you are dropped into containing one pre-trained model θ and one unstructured dataset D (all the emails you have ever written, every meeting transcript your company has ever had), with the goal of producing a θ\* that "knows" D — and flags "know" as "a very load-bearing term… maybe that's where people get the leeway to propose new ideas" (09:10-10:27). He then walks four families. **Just train on it**: next-token prediction over 10K financial reports drives loss to ~0.0001 and the model "knows the data perfectly well," but generation collapses and it "can't answer any question unless the question is perfectly encoded in the data with its answer, which is like never the case in practice" (10:29-11:59). **KV compaction** — the same mechanism Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode use — compresses D into a small set of KVs, but "it only applies to things that are in context" and "misses… some of the magic that you can get from taking gradients" (12:01-13:10). **On-policy distillation**, whose trick is to "make the model think that the text is in context," works and is "a pretty good algorithm," but raises the question of what you distill, since "you can't really distill the raw documents" — the gap that self-study in the cartridges paper fills by generating question/answer pairs conditioned on D and training the model to behave as if it were seeing D in context (13:10-14:18). **Continued pre-training on synthetic data conditioned on D** is "pretty promising" but "you sort of overwrite some of the pre-training," it is "difficult to scale," and the blocker is that "you then have to post-train the model after doing this," while "a lot of people don't actually start with good pre-trained base models. They have post-trained models, which makes this hard" (14:19-15:38). Crafting unsupervised RL environments and running GRPO-style RL is the same shape with a different loss (15:40-15:58).

The talk's real contribution is the ceiling all four share, stated "basically for classical machine learning reasons": whatever you do, "you have to define the data set, and then you train on the data set, and eventually things saturate. So, even if it's like really hard, unless your model is under parameterized, eventually it will learn all the data" — "kind of like a data wall in the synthetic sense," after which "you have to do it again," and the open design question is what that second stage is (15:58-17:26). What none of the methods have is pre-training's scaling behavior, and the property Morris points at as the missing ingredient is AlphaGo's: it "makes its own training questions harder by getting better through training" (17:28-18:32). He reports this against his own results — when Engram started, "we generated curves that look just like this blue curve where no matter sort of how much data we generate or how much we train, we kind of do plateau because there's this almost like natural upper bound to how much you can learn in one go from D" — and says "it turns out that there are more sophisticated things you can do that make the training gradually harder, that make the model better over time," without describing them (18:03-18:32). One aside is separately useful: the data budget is less fixed than the formal statement makes it look, because a person studying a textbook "is not really… limited to the words of the textbook itself" — they find other textbooks, search the internet, and "even be proactive and talk to speakers of the language or people who know the thing that you're trying to learn" (08:13-09:05).

## Notes

- Self-description of scope, up front: "This isn't going to be like a super detail-oriented talk where I go through a lot of experiments we've been running or talk too much about what our models do. I just want to frame… the high-level problem" (00:24-00:39). Read the talk as a problem statement and a literature map, not as results.
- The breadth/depth frame, from Terence Tao: AI knows "every single public mathematical topic" and can "bridge gaps in the literature… in a way that no human even can know because it's read so much," but lacks the depth of a five-year graduate student's "subconscious intuition for the problem space" (01:14-02:07).
- Three failure classes are given as motivation, and they are different problems: knowledge after the training cutoff (the model "has no idea if Mexico won their game last night"); long-tail skills that are simply rare in public data — "models still are quite bad at writing AMD kernels. There are not that many good kernels written on AMD GPUs that are public"; and anything about you personally, "unless you happen to be famous enough to appear in the pre-training data" (02:36-03:57).
- The paradigm-level claim: "It's like the core problem with the current paradigm in AI that models cannot acquire new knowledge after training in a personalized way" (04:03-04:11).
- The naming problem is itself evidence of the field's stage: "people call it um sleep time compute, continual learning, neural memory, write time compute, note taking, dreaming, studying, machine studying. In classical AI, maybe it's called amortized inference." His own name is scaling compute on context, and the reason no name has won is that "the paradigm is like very early and hasn't been solidified the way, for example, pre-training or post-training have" (04:33-05:05).
- The three scaling axes: "we can train them on more data. We can train them for longer or add compute. Or we can make the models themselves bigger… really the entirety of the deep learning revolution comes from these three axes of scaling" (05:10-05:36).
- Models are "really good at Wikipedia. They know everything about Reddit, papers on arXiv, code on GitHub," and the post-training layer is "experts that are hired through data acquisition companies like Scale AI, Surge AI, and Mercor." The load-bearing sentence: "But, they're still by definition creating publicly available data because it's something that the model could tell to a user" (05:38-06:04).
- The METR time-horizon plot is cited only to attribute the trend: models "get better every month and can complete tasks that take a longer time. And this is purely an artifact of scaling" (06:12-06:23).
- The consequence for private domains: models are "getting better at… coding in the way that is public on GitHub… doing math in ways that are written in public textbooks, but they're not getting more knowledge of you or your life or your work" (07:03-07:15).
- Elimination of the other two axes: "we can't create new data. So, like the kind of data scaling axis is out the window. Um I think we also probably agree that we can't train a model from scratch on our data. So… you very likely want to start from a pre-trained model. Um, this leaves us with essentially one axis of scaling, which is compute" (07:26-08:11).
- The aside that qualifies the whole formulation: "the amount of data isn't really fixed. There are a lot of ways that you can get more data afterwards… if you're like studying a textbook or trying to learn a new language, it's not really like you're limited to the words of the textbook itself… You can find other textbooks, you can go on the internet and search for related things. You can even be proactive and talk to speakers of the language or people who know the thing that you're trying to learn. So, in practice I think the data access is very interesting and not actually fixed" (08:13-09:00). He then sets it aside for the idealized version: "how do you scale more compute given the same data?" (09:01-09:10)
- The formal statement: a box containing one pre-trained model — "maybe it's I don't know, GLM 5.2. Maybe you somehow hacked into Anthropic and stole the weights of Claude" — and "this unstructured data set D. So, maybe this is like all the emails you've ever written. It's all the transcripts from every meeting your company's ever had." The goal is a θ\* that knows D, and "the definition of know… is a very load-bearing term in this question… maybe that's where people get the leeway to propose new ideas" (09:17-10:21). "This is what every continual learning like startup is trying to do. This is more or less what we're doing at Engram" (10:21-10:27).
- Naive continued training, with the numbers: 10K financial reports, "you want it to be in the weights," "you can get to a loss of like 0.0001… and you can end up with a model that knows the data perfectly well. And then when you generate from it, it basically collapses" (10:50-11:24).
- Two separate objections to that approach: it is bounded — "there's some information in that just gets perfectly transferred into the model and then you no longer learn. So, this is like not an indefinite axis of scaling" — and independently, "it just frankly doesn't work… doesn't produce a model that has interesting generalization properties like normal models. Like, it can't answer any question unless the question is perfectly encoded in the data with its answer, which is like never the case in practice" (11:24-11:59).
- Why the next family exists: "we know models are really good when you paste stuff into context. Like, in-context learning is is magical" (12:03-12:16).
- KV compaction is anchored to shipping products — "similar to the way that, you know, Claude code or Codex or open code, what have you, does compaction" — and to "a very cute paper that has like a kind of greedy algorithm for approximating KV compaction." Its limits: "it only applies to things that are in context, but it also misses, I think, some of the magic that you can get from… taking gradients" (12:16-13:10).
- On-policy distillation is attributed to another speaker at the same event (first name only in the captions) and described mechanically: "you have text and you show it to the model and then you make the model think that the text is in context. That's more or less the trick of on policy distillation. The on policy part just means you kind of update the model throughout training" (13:15-13:41).
- The data question inside distillation: "what data do you actually do this with? You can't really distill the raw documents, so techniques like self-study from the cartridges paper… try to generate like question and answer pairs conditioned on D and then train the model to behave as if it is seeing D in context when it's answering questions. I think this is like close to the behavior you want" (13:44-14:12).
- Why simulating pre-training is attractive at all: "pre-training is amazing for knowledge acquisition. Like I can ask Claude what… result I got in a paper that I've written and it actually knows this, which is incredible" (14:35-14:49).
- Its three caveats: "you sort of overwrite some of the pre-training. I think it's difficult to scale… Maybe like one blocker is you then have to post-train the model after doing this. So, a lot of people don't actually start with good pre-trained base models. They have post-trained models, which makes this hard" (15:13-15:32).
- The RL variant: "you can craft unsupervised reinforcement learning environments and do RL. It's pretty similar to the previous suggestion, except instead of doing some type of distillation, you're just using like RL loss like GRPO or whatever" (15:41-15:58).
- The goal none of them meets, stated as a requirement: "the thing that we're really after is like to give the model more knowledge of D, or to get better depth of your domain. We want to be able to add compute arbitrarily in a way that makes the model better… I think none of the approaches I proposed do this" (16:06-16:23).
- The reason, and the wall: "whatever you do, you have to define the data set, and then you train on the data set, and eventually things saturate. So, even if it's like really hard, unless your model is under parameterized, eventually it will learn all the data. And this doesn't give… the beautiful scaling properties that we see out of pre-training. It's kind of like a data wall in in the synthetic sense, where when you create synthetic data from D and train on it, you eventually hit this upper bound where like you've learned all of the synthetic data. And then you have to do it again" (16:24-16:57).
- The open design question that follows: "a lot of the missing components here are… how do you do it again? Like what's this second stage of training look like?" — and any of the techniques can fill it ("the attention matching, or some type of self-study thing, or some continued pre-training"), but "eventually you will fit the data and you'll know some about D, but you won't know everything and you'll no longer have this property where you can add compute and give the model more depth" (16:57-17:26).
- The target property, named through AlphaGo: "AlphaGo makes its own training questions harder by getting better through training. And so… one thing that everyone is looking for is a technique that can make models better, which makes them train themselves better… you sort of generate data and then the model gets a bit better and then you generate better data um recursively" (17:35-18:05).
- Engram's own reported result, stated as a plateau rather than a win: "when we started the company, we we generated curves that look just like this blue curve where no matter sort of how much data we generate or how much we train, we kind of do plateau because there's this almost like natural upper bound to how much you can learn in one go from D. But, I think it turns out that there are more sophisticated things you can do that make the training gradually harder, that make the model better over time" (18:05-18:32).
- What success would look like: "It's like your emails, for example, the model is just generating a better and better internal model of who you are and what you do and what your goals are, what your value function looks like, and it can help you more and more" (18:38-18:53).
- Close: "that's like a summary of maybe what we think about when we think about doing research at Engram. It's a little bit abstract," followed by a hiring pitch (18:54-19:20).

## Provenance and Caveats

- **The speaker is a researcher at a company selling the solution to the problem he is describing**, and says so — Engram "launched last week," "this is more or less what we're doing at Engram," and the talk ends on job postings (00:19-00:24, 10:21-10:27, 19:08-19:20). The literature map is even-handed about competing approaches; the framing that private-corpus depth is *the* core problem of the current paradigm is his company's positioning.
- **No results are reported for any method, including his own.** The only Engram datapoint is a negative one — early curves plateaued — and the claim that "more sophisticated things" fix it is stated without description, mechanism, or measurement (18:05-18:32). Nothing in this talk supports a claim that any approach works better than another.
- **The failure descriptions are asserted from experience, not measured here.** "Loss of like 0.0001" and "it basically collapses" come with no dataset description, no model, no eval, and no baseline (10:50-11:24). The wiki's earlier note from the same speaker describes a related experiment with different numbers; see the Caption Artifacts note below on the report count.
- **The papers are pointed at, not summarized.** The cartridges paper (self-study), the KV-compaction paper, and the three continued-pre-training papers are shown on slides that the transcript does not name, so only the cartridges title and the self-study technique name are recoverable from audio. Anyone acting on this page should read those papers rather than trusting the one-line characterizations.
- **The talk was compressed under time pressure** — he says "I'm trying to go a bit faster," "I need to go faster," and "let's skip Andre" while cutting a slide (06:07-06:12, 15:36-15:41). At least one referenced item was skipped entirely and is unrecoverable.
- The METR trend is cited only as attribution for a well-known plot, with no new data.
