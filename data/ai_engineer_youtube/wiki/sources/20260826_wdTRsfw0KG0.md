## Summary

A nineteen-minute retrospective from someone who joined OpenAI "a little over four years ago" when it was "a couple million in revenue" with "a self-serve API… some developers who found it and swiped a credit card," and helped grow the enterprise side "to several billion" and the organization "to several hundred people" (00:01-00:52). She is now at Acrew Capital, so the talk is an operator's post-mortem delivered as investor advice. It is the wiki's seventh go-to-market source and the only one that looks at the *commercial motion* — packaging, sequencing, procurement friction, pricing, and when to add humans — rather than at the GTM data and agent stack. Almost every claim is framed as a mistake: "we did it all backwards."

**The central sequencing claim, with a worked failure.** ChatGPT launched at the end of 2022 "with no enterprise features whatsoever," and the inbound was immediate and specific: "Can I get SSO? Can I get an NDA? Can I get an invoice?" Approval to build an enterprise version came "9 months later" (01:49-02:25). The requirements sample was biased by who could reach her: "over that 9-month time, the companies that had been the loudest, the companies that had gotten to me, were the bigger companies, were the large enterprises. So, I thought, 'Okay, we should go out and build a really high-end product for all these enterprises'… We added every bell and whistle. We made it lightning fast. It was a very expensive product… We went way up market right out the gate" (02:25-02:59). Self-serve shipped "in January of 2024, so about 4 months after our enterprise version, and it just completely cannibalized our enterprise business. Uh it grew so much faster. It turns out most people just didn't want to talk to a salesperson," which "frustrated the sales reps cuz now they had to compete with self-serve" (02:59-03:37). The conclusion is an ordering rule that became policy: "We should have launched with self-serve first, listen to feedback from our customers and what they weren't getting from the self-serve product, and then figured out how to build a more expensive enterprise offering, and then hire the team to sell it… For every other product we launched at OpenAI, we made sure to do it self-serve first" (03:37-04:14).

**The generalization: build the machine before the team.** The default she argues against is "hire a bunch of sales people… RevOps… sales engineers… and then I'm going to figure out where the bottlenecks are and I'm going to add AI or automation to figure out how to scale this thing." Her inversion: "start with what you can automate… build the machine first before you build the team. Figure out what you can automate. Figure out where the bottlenecks are and what's broken and then add humans on top of that to get through those bottlenecks" (00:52-01:49). The enabling claim is tooling availability — "there's so many tools available now in 2026 for a modern go-to-market or sales organization that have never been available before which really turns the entire playbook on its head" — and the named tools are Clay (used at OpenAI, credited by name) and Nooks for "researching, sequencing, dialing," so that "your reps can focus on the qualified leads and the high value conversations" (04:14-04:31, 05:36-05:52).

**The inbound failure, which is the same story from the demand side.** "It was me and four sales reps, and we were getting, you know, 10,000 inbound a day. I mean, it was just the most staggering problem." Two regrets follow. First, under-capture: "I would have added way more fields to our sign-up form, like phone number, because eventually when we did catch up and we built all the automation… I didn't have a lot of information to go follow up with people. So, add a very in-depth sales form. It can be optional, but if people are willing to give you their phone number, you should get it, cuz at some point you might want to call them or have AI call them" (04:31-05:04). Second, silence: "Try to respond to everyone… at least just sent some email out saying, 'Hey, we hear you. We don't have our enterprise product ready yet. You're on our waitlist'… Just some sort of automated email campaign to make them feel like we cared. Because when we finally did launch our enterprise product 9 months later, almost every company said, 'Well, you never got back to me. And I went out and I bought Microsoft Copilot'" (05:04-05:36).

**Friction as the buying-side design problem.** "Make it easy on your buyers. Don't give them homework… Never say, 'Hey, go do these five things and then come back to me.' Or 'Go ask this person about this and come back to me.' You do as much of the work for them" — up to and including "hackathons and come in their office and do it with them in person" or "some sort of Zoom session and do it live with them" — because "whatever you do, don't send your buyer away to do something and wait for them to come back because you lose total control of the sales cycle and it makes everything take longer" (05:52-06:31). The general list: "fewer options is better on pricing. Make the paperwork easy and fast. Make the approval flow as manageable as possible" (06:31-06:48).

**Pilots.** "Avoid pilots at all cost… founders say, 'We are stuck in pilot hell. We've got all of these pilots going on. None of them are converting. They're so much work.' Or if they do convert… I sell the pilot. I invest really heavily in managing the pilot. And then I have to start all over again from scratch once I get a yes to move forward" (06:48-07:27). The reserve rule: "use pilots sparingly. They should be not the default, but the option for your most valuable, highest paying, biggest revenue opportunities." The leverage argument: "as soon as you give someone access to your product, you're giving away a lot of power and leverage in the deal cycle." Four named substitutes: "You can introduce them to another customer. You can do an eval on part of their data. You can do a demo with their custom data and you do it over Zoom rather than handing them access to it. You can give them an opt-out clause… sign a contract, you have 90 days to opt out if it doesn't meet your needs, and that shifts the onus of trying out the product and validating it onto them cuz the clock is ticking on them now." Plus a term-length trade: "our normal contracts are 3 years, but we'll do a 1-year POC with you" (07:27-08:15). She flags that opt-out clauses are a startup move: "It's not something you do when you're a giant company, but when you're a startup, you can get away with these things."

**Security as a stall point.** "Security is where deals tend to stall out and die over time. There's a lot you can automate now… trust portals where companies can come in and they can auto sign an NDA. They can self-serve for your pen test and security documentation. There's AI products that can auto fill out your security questionnaires." The behavioral instruction matters as much as the tooling: "most companies will tell you, 'Oh, I need to get on a call. I need to talk for 2 hours.' Push back on that. Push them towards your trust portal and then make them come to you and say, 'Here's what I couldn't find. Here's what wasn't available. Here's why I need a call'" (08:15-09:06).

**Pricing, called "building the Porsche before the Prius."** "We set it initially ChatGPT Enterprise was $60 per user per month. Now, we were really the first ones on the market, and we didn't know what pricing was going to be. So, we basically priced it according to how expensive it was for us to serve it. And then all the other products came out on the market, Copilot and Gemini and Anthropic at a much lower price point. And we realized we had set the price too high" (09:06-09:44). The fix and its mechanism: "we changed the model to basically a license fee, a base fee to use the product, and then move to usage based… the barrier to entry went down. So, we had so many more companies signing licenses… And then usage went up over time. Because when it was $60 per month, organizations would come in and say, 'well, I'm only buying this for a subset of my team or only for my developers.' … But once we lowered the threshold, it spread like wildfire" (09:44-10:19). She then moves the argument one step forward: "I know over time now seats are out. This has become common knowledge. We learned it the hard way. And I also know that now usage is also getting pushed back on as companies are seeing cost of usage skyrocket. There's easy ways around this. So, you could for example, cap… you can cap your spend per month. Most companies don't end up using that, but they like to know that it's there. You can cap certain employees' spend" (10:19-10:51).

**When to add humans, and which humans.** The trigger is a saturated machine plus an explicit ask: "you've gone as far as you can in your sales process and you're really having a lot of companies saying, 'I just need to talk to somebody' — whether it's I need a demo. I just need to look someone in the eye and build the trust here. And you're going to have that as you start to move up market naturally" (10:51-11:25). Attached is a warning about the same move: "avoid the pull upmarket as long as you can resist it because once you get a big customer, they'll come and they'll say, 'I can spend a million dollars, 10 million dollars with you.' And it's very tempting to want to orient your whole business around that. They will take so many of your legal, security, product, engineering, sales resources. So, you want to be really picky about who those first big enterprise partners are" (11:25-11:57). The hiring profile is "someone who is AI native, who really gets AI, who lives it, who can authentically sell it… you want them to practice what they preach" (11:57-12:20). And the counter-intuitive claim she names "the revenge of the steak dinner": "As more and more of this becomes automated — the automated outbound, the automated demo, the automated security checklist — more and more companies are craving in-person time with humans. So, conferences, conference booths work really well, dinners work really well. And they're, by the way, not that expensive to set up" (12:20-12:46). The residual capability argument: "You're not selling your tool. You're not selling your product. You're selling what that tool does for your champion or your buyer. And this is where I do think humans are still very helpful. They're very good at value selling, and I think AI does still have some ways to go on value selling" (14:08-14:29).

**Compensation, as an aside.** "We were notorious because we still have not given out comp plans… I do think OpenAI is finally about to put in commission… I would not necessarily say that is the right path for every company. I think OpenAI could get away with that because of the equity packages and the way the company was increasing in value, but in general, it's really hard to hire the best enterprise sellers in the market if you don't pay a variable." The exception: "those first few sales hires are usually wired a little bit differently. They're there to build the company. They're there for the equity… you can get away with it really for the first two or three sales hires," after which you need "more coin-operated enterprise sellers." Design rule: "Simpler is better, but have upside for out-performers" (12:46-14:08).

**Q&A.** *Reconciling self-serve-first with pilot-avoidance*: they are different objects. "PLG, so people coming and self-serving and just using your product is very different from a POC, which is usually a contracted period of time where they have access to your product and they have support and resources from your team and you're giving them services and support along the way… self-serve access to your product is totally fine and great and you can stage gate that or limit that with consumption, time box it, or feature gate it… When I talk about POCs, I'm specifically referring to more handheld services-oriented POCs that require a sales engineer or salesperson to supervise" (15:03-16:00). *Forward-deployed engineers*: "in an ideal world, we wouldn't need them. Eventually, hopefully we get continual learning and these models can just figure out the inner organization and immediately become smart." For now they are supply-constrained — "there's something like 55,000 job reqs for forward deployed engineers right now and about 5,000 people who know how to do that job" — and the compensating benefit is retention: "they make the product really sticky cuz once you've invested in that services element… your customer is unlikely to go anywhere soon because they feel like they've got a customized tool that works uniquely for them" (16:00-17:10). *First ten customers*: not the automated-outbound motion at all. "Your first 10 customers are going to be different. They're going to be really more design partners… they're going to need to be much more handheld… it's sort of a gift to a company to let them be your design partner. There's exclusivity to it, there's scarcity to it." The selection tactic: "go to a company that has a great logo, but work on their internal product or some sort of product that's not their main production, so that they're willing to take a little bit more risk with you and bypass some security processes, but then you get the logo on your site… And it opens the door to potentially sign a bigger agreement with that company over time." Ideally "they're relationships you already have, either companies you've worked with or your investors are introducing you… cuz you want these to be very friendly processes and experiments in the beginning" (17:10-18:45).

## Provenance and Limits

This is a single operator's recollection of one company's history, delivered as advice by someone now on the investing side, and nothing in it is measured with a stated method. The revenue arc ("a couple million" to "several billion"), the inbound volume ("10,000 inbound a day"), the cannibalization ("it grew so much faster"), and the price change outcome ("it spread like wildfire," "usage went up over time") are all directional recollections with no figures, windows, denominators, or comparison against a counterfactual. The forward-deployed-engineer supply figures — "something like 55,000 job reqs… and about 5,000 people who know how to do that job" — are prefaced with a hedge and given no source. Two claims are also stated as already-known rather than evidenced: "seats are out. This has become common knowledge," and that usage pricing is now itself under pushback.

The sequencing claim carries a survivorship problem the talk does not address. OpenAI's self-serve motion grew faster than its enterprise motion in a period of unique inbound demand for that specific product; a company without a consumer-scale brand may find self-serve produces nothing to learn from. Related, "we should have launched self-serve first" is a counterfactual asserted, not tested — the enterprise launch may have contributed to the awareness that made self-serve grow. The "$60 was too high" reading is confounded with competitive entry, since Copilot, Gemini, and Anthropic arriving lower is itself a sufficient explanation for a price reset independent of whether $60 was ever mispriced.

The pilot advice is asymmetric on purpose — it optimizes the seller's control of the deal cycle, and the buyer-side reasons for wanting a POC (validating in their environment, on their data, at their scale) are acknowledged as objections to negotiate around rather than as legitimate technical requirements. For an AI product specifically, where behavior on a customer's own data is the thing most in doubt, "do an eval on part of their data" is the substitute that preserves the buyer's actual question; the reference call and the Zoom demo do not. The talk does not draw that distinction. Similarly, the 90-day opt-out is described as something a startup "can get away with," which prices it as leverage rather than as a claim about product confidence.

The design-partner tactic — target a non-production internal workload at a company with a good logo "so that they're willing to bypass some security processes" — is stated plainly and worth reading as such: it is advice to route around a buyer's controls, and the logo it produces on your site attests to a workload that was chosen for being low-stakes.

Caption artifacts: the speaker's name is rendered "Alyssa" throughout but she spells it on stage as "A L I I S A"; "a bare fee" is a base fee; "the pull-up market" is the pull upmarket; "job wrecks" is job reqs; "Alyssa Roy" opens a question and is a mis-caption of a greeting. Left as heard: "Nooks" as the named dialing vendor, and the "Everett" shout-out, which refers to Everett Berry of Clay, a speaker at the same event.
