## Summary

Sean DuBois and Kwindla Kramer argue that realtime voice AI has to be designed from the network layer up: voice-to-voice latency near human conversational timing is the product constraint, WebRTC is the practical transport for low-latency audio/video streams, and WebRTC topology choices let builders connect local devices, cloud agents, and multiparty applications without rebuilding realtime media machinery.

## Notes

- Voice AI shares many multi-turn agent concerns with text agents, but the talk frames latency as the differentiator: around 500 ms feels natural in human conversation, while response latencies much above one second lead to poor completion, NPS, and hang-up behavior. 01:46-02:41
- The speakers define the key measure as voice-to-voice latency: the time from when the human stops talking until the first returned audio is heard. 02:33-02:41
- A real browser-to-cloud Pipecat voice app was shown at just under one second of voice latency; the talk notes this is usable but still involves tradeoffs among speed, quality, and cost. 02:47-03:13
- WebSockets are described as useful for server-to-server use cases, small structured data, and prototypes, but not as the right choice for realtime audio/video streams. 03:25-04:56
- The WebRTC argument rests on transport semantics: TCP's in-order delivery and retries can block on packet loss or jitter, while realtime media should often ignore late packets inside a tight latency budget. 04:58-06:20
- WebRTC also supplies media-specific machinery such as resampling, packetization, bandwidth estimation, stats, and observability that a WebSocket media path would otherwise have to rebuild. 06:44-07:25
- The Squabbert demo used a Raspberry Pi with MLX Whisper, Gemma 3, and a custom logic sampler, connected peer-to-peer over WebRTC to a laptop on the same local network. 10:51-12:38
- The same WebRTC substrate can support a local peer connection, a connection to a cloud AI server, or multiparty Pipecat-style applications that bring LLMs into meetings. 12:23-13:08
