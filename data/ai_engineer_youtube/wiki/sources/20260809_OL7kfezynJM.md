## Summary

Arjun Singh (Superconductor; previously GradeScope, and a Berkeley PhD program with co-founder Sergey) opens by naming what he thinks the conference is under-covering: "a lot of people are talking about putting the agents at the center of everything… but you don't see a lot of people talking about the people." The talk is six lessons from a year of aggressive internal agent adoption, and its through-line is that every one of them is downstream of a single infrastructure decision — running the codebase in an isolated cloud environment. The first lesson is to be **model and harness agnostic**, argued from three facts rather than from principle: "the best model and harness can change weekly," it can change "cuz the best one got taken away" (he reports switching their default to Fable when it launched, running it "for like the few days we had it," and switching back to Codex when it went away), open-weight models are now good enough that they are "really happy with GLM 5.2," and "the incentives of the people selling you tokens aren't really aligned with yours… you might be happy to pay for as many tokens as it takes, but you don't want to pay for more than that." The second lesson is to **turn every human interface into an agent and human interface**, with a precise statement of why the obvious move is insufficient: a Slack bot ("Claude has a Slack bot. Codex has a Slack bot. We have a Slack bot") only relocates the problem, taking the agent "from trapped on somebody's laptop to kind of trapped in Slack." What they wanted instead was "the same session from every relevant interface" — Slack, desktop and mobile app, GitHub — so that "the agent didn't forget what you did in one place in Slack when you go and talk to it from GitHub, it's the same session. It's got the same context." The payoff he demonstrates is a review one: seeing a teammate's ticket, "I can just pop in and say, 'Hey, why did you do it this way?'… I don't need to wait for Sergey to kind of get my notification on GitHub and respond to me. The answer to the question is almost certainly in this thread," and rather than read the thread, "I can just ask the agent." A session also carries its participant roster — "who's getting notified about this session, who's seen it" — which he calls "especially important when you have work triggered by non-technical people": if support filed the ticket, the roster answers "has this been vetted by an engineer or not?" Visibility itself is carried by **artifacts**: "it doesn't matter where the work started or where it's finishing, the agent can show you the work it's doing as screenshot or video or other. And you can see it from everywhere." The third lesson is to **turn every external signal into code that your team can quickly evaluate**, where a signal is a Slack conversation, customer or onboarding or sales call, internal meeting, Sentry alert, bug report, email, or feature request. His diagnosis of the status quo is that MCP already solved reading — "now your coding agent can check the email or check Notion" — but not selection: "how does it know what to work on? … it's still kind of stuck everywhere," so "some humans are involved in taking stuff from one place and telling it solve email number 48 or ticket number 6,000. But that's still a lot of coordination." Their answer ingests signals automatically, prioritizes, and acts, and the demo is a **meeting bot** invited into "Meet or Zoom or Teams" that sat in a Google Meet at their expo booth for four hours. Someone passing through said they wanted coding agents to have "clear criteria to evaluate whether it did a good job on the work before it tells me that it's done"; the bot opened a ticket and started working with nobody doing "anything manually," and on request produced a screenshot showing two new acceptance-criteria fields added to the product's own ticket form. He is deliberate about the bound: "Am I going to ship this one exactly how it is? Like, no, probably not. But it's a new idea, it's concrete, I can play with it," and the aggregate claim is that every onboarding, customer call, or team meeting yields "dozens of new ideas that are prototyped, but more importantly, at least a few shippable PRs with a very minimal intervention." A deduplication rule keeps it from flooding: "if it finds existing work, it'll link to it." The fourth lesson is the load-bearing one — **isolated cloud environments** — and he explicitly demotes the popular reason for it. Eliminating "lid anxiety" ("people running around the office with their laptops open while stuff is working or at the airport… tethered to their phone in their cars as they're driving home") matters to him personally, "but I don't think that's the most important reason." The important reason is least privilege applied to a machine nobody audits: "their laptops, unless you have like impeccable hygiene, probably have a bunch of stuff on it that you don't want the LLMs or agents to have access to," which leaves you "in one of two camps. You're either approving a bunch of stuff or you're hoping that your auto approval flow or your YOLO mode… is configured properly and your sandbox is configured properly and doesn't read a bunch of stuff on your laptop that it shouldn't have." The failure he names is not disobedience but eagerness — agents "are getting really resourceful. They're trying to please you and do what you said" — so "when you say, 'Hey, wipe this staging database,' and it finds a token on your laptop that it can use and it thinks it's working with staging, but actually it's production and now it just deleted everything." His hedge is honest: "I'm not trying to say this is happening constantly, but it still happens." Isolation is paired with a **configurable network sandbox** covering the other direction — "make sure they can't exfiltrate your code or your projects or your secrets or your content" — that names allowed and disallowed destinations, prompts on anything new ("maybe you're trying to integrate a new vendor and you need documentation"), and is scoped "on a per ticket basis or for the whole project." The organizational payoff is the part that is easy to miss: isolation "is the key for allowing your non-technical team members to trigger real work," because "your non-technical people don't have development environments set up on their computers." Support and growth people now "just go to Slack or the [app] and say, 'Hey, fix this.' They fix it. Screenshots are shown. Engineer gets it, gets merged," replacing "they'd have to put it in linear and linear would eventually pick it up and a PM would triage it." The historical blocker — sandbox setup being "really, really painful" — he considers largely dissolved: they ship an environment setup assistant, but "whether you use this or not… you can just get Claude Code or Codex to do this for you." The last lesson is to **benchmark agents on your own codebase**: select "pull requests that represent great engineering work — could be agent created, could be human created, could be a hybrid, doesn't matter," replay them across the harnesses you care about, and read off "a quality versus cost and time breakdown on your code base." The justification is stack mismatch: "SWE-bench is all in Python, we're Ruby on Rails… There's trends that do compare, but the results can be very, very different." Their own plots show Anthropic agents "consistently getting better, but not really any faster" and "clearly just so much more expensive for us," Codex and Cursor "pretty fast and quite good," and open models improving but "kind of slow" — with an explicit disclaimer, "this is for our code base again. I'm not trying to make any general claims here." The results moved a default ("they kind of matched our vibe check. We wanted to have hard data, too. We switched our default to Codex"), and he names a second payoff that is about attention rather than accuracy: the standing anxiety of hearing that MiniMax or GLM or Kimi K2 is better while never finding "the two hours to try it," then discovering it "didn't really work for us. So, I just wasted those two hours" — the benchmark "kind of eliminates that." The forward-looking version is a rebuttal to third-party task routers: "how does some third party know what to route for your code base?" Operating numbers for a "relatively small team": 99.9% of pull requests "heavily agent generated" with "everything's human reviewed," 1.5 billion tokens in the past month (the video description says 10.5 billion), 3,300 Claude Code runs at "$10,000 in tokens" of list value against a subscription plan so "we didn't spend $10,000 on it," and Codex running "four times as many sessions" and "cheaper overall," with "more and more… happening through GLM 5.2."

## Extracted Concepts

- [Replay Your Own Merged PRs as the Coding-Agent Benchmark](../concepts/replay-your-own-merged-prs-as-the-agent-benchmark.md) - selecting good PRs from your repository and plotting quality against cost and time is the eval a public benchmark on the wrong stack cannot give you.
- [Make One Agent Session Reachable From Every Interface](../concepts/make-one-agent-session-reachable-from-every-interface.md) - a per-surface bot relocates the agent's isolation instead of removing it; the shared object has to be the session and its context.
- [Turn Unfiled Conversation Into Concrete Prototypes](../concepts/turn-unfiled-conversation-into-concrete-prototypes.md) - the scarce step is not reading the signal, which MCP already solved, but selecting and acting on it before anyone files a ticket.
- [A Developer Laptop Is an Ambient-Credential Surface](../concepts/a-developer-laptop-is-an-ambient-credential-surface.md) - a resourceful agent finds credentials it was never handed, so the machine is the threat surface, not the request.
- [Environment Isolation Is What Lets Non-Engineers Trigger Real Work](../concepts/environment-isolation-is-what-lets-non-engineers-trigger-real-work.md) - the blocker on support and growth shipping fixes is a missing dev environment, not a missing interface.

## Provenance and Caveats

- A vendor talk by a founder about his own product, delivered in a ~19-minute slot with no Q&A. He flags this repeatedly and fairly — "I'll mention a couple of places where our product makes it easy for us, but whether you use us or not, I'm just going to leave things with you that I think are really important" — and twice recommends achieving the same result without the product ("you can just get Claude Code or Codex to do this for you"; "plenty of people are home rolling things").
- Almost nothing is measured with a baseline. "Dozens of new ideas that are prototyped… at least a few shippable PRs with a very minimal intervention" per call has no denominator, no definition of shippable, no before/after, and no count of how many of those PRs merged. The 4-hour expo-booth run is shown as a screen of created work, not as a hit rate; he does not say how many items were noise, how many duplicated existing tickets, or what the review cost of the batch was.
- The benchmark results are the strongest artifact in the talk and are also the least reproducible from it. No task count, no scoring rubric, no definition of "quality," no axis units, no error bars, no list of which harnesses and versions were plotted, and no description of how a replayed PR is judged against the original. He states the scope limit himself — "this is for our code base again. I'm not trying to make any general claims here" — so the *method* is the transferable part, not the rankings.
- The security argument rests on one hypothetical (the staging token that points at production) with the frequency explicitly unquantified: "I'm not trying to say this is happening constantly, but it still happens." No incident, no rate, no post-mortem.
- The network sandbox is described as a prompt-on-new-destination allowlist, which is the same interactive-approval pattern the talk criticizes two minutes earlier as one of the "two camps." Nothing is said about approval fatigue on this surface, and the control is destination-based, so it decides which hosts a run may reach and not what is sent to a host already allowed.
- The trust boundary is inverted rather than removed: the isolated cloud environment is the vendor's, and the talk offers no threat model for running an organization's code and credentials on a third party's capacity.
- Two figures conflict with the video description. The transcript says "1 and 1/2 billion tokens over the past month" where the description says 10.5 billion; and "3,300 Claude code runs that cost $10,000 in tokens daily" sits awkwardly against the same paragraph's monthly framing, with the description reading it as a total token value rather than a daily rate. Both are recorded as stated with the discrepancy left open; no claim on any concept page depends on resolving them.
- "99.9% of our pull requests are heavily agent generated" and "everything's human reviewed" are both self-reported for a small team whose size is never given, and no review-time or defect-rate figure accompanies either.
- Nothing is said about what happens when several people push a shared session in different directions, or how conflicting instructions from Slack, the app, and GitHub are reconciled — the multiplayer contention question the design implies.
- The live demo failed on venue Wi-Fi and was replaced with a recording from the previous day ("I wanted to show this live, but the Wi-Fi is not quite there," 05:44-05:50).
