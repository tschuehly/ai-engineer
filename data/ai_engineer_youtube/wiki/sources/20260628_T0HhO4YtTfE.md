## Summary

Apoorva Joshi (Staff AI Developer Advocate, MongoDB; data-scientist background in cybersecurity ML) argues that when AI writes the code, the hard part shifts to *deciding what to build* — the product spec, system design, and eval criteria — and most AI systems fail in production because of bad decisions made before any code is written. She presents a repeatable four-phase framework for designing an AI system idea-to-production — (1) product requirements, (2) system design, (3) evaluation and monitoring, (4) optimization before ship — and walks it end-to-end on one running example, a health-insurance claims-review assistant for medical reviewers at a fictional "MDB Health." The requirements phase quantifies a solution-agnostic business problem, gathers business/performance constraints, classifies the role of AI along three dimensions, and states a SMART success metric. The system-design phase works out a per-source data strategy (residence, update cadence, processing, retrieval technique), maps the request flow, and composes the *simplest* set of AI design patterns (RAG + control flow + human-in-the-loop here) rather than jumping to an agent. The eval phase defines input/output guardrails and a layered metric ladder that continues to run as production monitoring. The final phase optimizes accuracy, then cost/latency/reliability before shipping.

## Notes

- Framing: "vibe code it and ship it" works for low-stakes fun where you can eyeball the output, but is dangerous the moment something real depends on it. Quotes attributed to Anthropic/OpenAI talks over recent months: "specs are the new code," the art is in defining product requirements, system design, and eval criteria so your "AI coding buddies" build the right thing. (00:46-01:45)
- The framework's four phases: **product requirements** (what/for-whom/constraints) → **system design** (data, architecture, patterns) → **evaluation and monitoring** (does it work before and after ship) → **optimize** for cost, latency, and reliability, not just accuracy, before ship and as gaps appear in production. (01:49-02:27)
- Running example: a health-insurance claims-review system to help human medical reviewers, who historically cross-reference clinical guidelines, coverage policies, and patient history to decide if a treatment is covered. Goal: improve/simplify the reviewer's experience with AI. (02:42-03:36)
- **Quantify the business problem**: make it user-specific, state the current state, quantify the pain with baselines, and keep it *solution-agnostic* — it should not prescribe agent vs multi-agent vs anything else. Example: "Medical reviewers at MDB Health spend an average of 2 days processing claim-review requests — 4× the industry standard for non-urgent and 12× for urgent — delaying patient care." (03:45-05:06)
- **Gather business constraints early** as design inputs: regulatory/compliance, data-residency (patient data must stay in the approved cloud), procurement/vendor approval (only models available on the approved cloud), and where humans are mandatory (complex cases need a senior physician; any *denial* must be human-reviewed — cases you can't fully automate). Also gather **performance requirements**: latency needs, monthly LLM-inference spend ceiling, uptime SLAs. (05:10-06:26)
- **Classify the role of AI along three dimensions**: (1) critical vs complementary (here complementary — claim reviews already happen without AI, just slower); (2) reactive vs proactive (reactive — triggered by a submitted claim); (3) level of autonomy (here semi-autonomous at most, because constraints mandate human review in some cases). (06:31-07:23)
- **Define 1–2 SMART success metrics** aligned to the business problem: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound. Example: "Reduce average processing time for *urgent* claim-review requests from 2 days to 1 hour within 90 days of launch." (07:28-08:30)
- **Data strategy** — for each source ask what data is needed, whether you have access, where it resides, and whether it's consumable raw. Sources here: clinical guidelines and MDB Health coverage policies (assumed PDFs in Confluence) and patient claims history (in MongoDB, written as claims are processed). (08:35-09:44)
- **Data update cadence** — processing pipelines must run at the right frequency or the system reasons over stale data (dangerous in a high-stakes use case): clinical guidelines ~annually, coverage policies ~quarterly, patient claims history whenever a new claim is processed, and — because the success metric targets 1-hour urgent adjudication — refreshed ~hourly. (09:46-10:36)
- **Data processing per source** — long documents (guidelines, policies) get chunked, embedded, and metadata-extracted (procedure names, publication dates) for vector/hybrid search; the already-structured MongoDB claims history mainly needs PII removed before passing to an LLM. **Retrieval technique per source** — guidelines/policies suit vector search but contain medical terminology (diagnosis/procedure codes) vector search may miss, so use vector-search-with-metadata-prefiltering or hybrid search; patient history is an exact match on patient name/ID. (10:42-12:07)
- **System architecture — resist jumping to an agent**: it's tempting (hype) to jump to an agent or let a coding agent pick the architecture, but you risk an over-engineered system. Instead start with the simplest design, evaluate it, find gaps during evaluation, and iterate. First *map how a request flows*: claim + physician clinical notes received → retrieve relevant policies and patient history → pass all of it plus the claim and system prompts to the LLM → LLM recommends approve/reject → complex case routes to senior physician, a *deny* recommendation escalates to a human medical reviewer → final decision + reasoning logged to MongoDB. (12:12-14:03)
- **AI design-pattern catalog** presented as a menu: RAG (augment LLM knowledge with external sources); AI agents / multi-agent (full autonomy to complete tasks with tools); agentic systems generally; **control flows** (LLM does tasks inside a workflow whose step sequence is predetermined by design/code); **LLM-as-router** (limited autonomy — categorize incoming requests and route to downstream workflows); **human-in-the-loop** (LLM/agent acts but needs human intervention somewhere); **fine-tuning** (when failures are behavioral rather than data/orchestration, or you need superior domain-specific performance). (14:10-16:09)
- **Compose the simplest patterns that fit**: the claims system needs RAG (retrieve guidelines/policies), a control flow (pre-structured: recommend, then escalate in specific cases), arguably LLM-as-router (if an extra LLM call decides what a "complex case" is), and human-in-the-loop (reviewers are part of the flow) — the speaker keeps it to control flow for now. (16:18-17:27)
- **Design the UX and feedback loop** by asking: what's the input (a claim request form), the output (approve/deny verdict + explanation), where the system lives (embedded in MDB Health's website), what triggers it (a claim submission), the human's role (review the assessment, make the final call in some cases), how it explains itself (citations quoting the guidelines/policies that informed the assessment), and how users give feedback (override the verdict + log a reason; flag hallucinated or wrong citations). (17:30-19:35)
- **Stack decisions come after architecture and are constrained** by the requirements phase (e.g. models must be supported by the approved cloud); decisions include model choice, data-processing tooling, embedding model, vector DB, and orchestration framework — don't just let a coding agent recommend tools that may violate constraints. (19:37-20:49)
- **Evaluation vs monitoring**: evaluation is before ship, monitoring is after; you need both. (20:56-21:20)
- **Guardrails** matter because LLM systems are *probabilistic* and can produce unexpected/incorrect/harmful output (not a pre-LLM concern); you define the acceptable boundaries. **Input** guardrails detect invalid/irrelevant/harmful inputs (e.g. "write me a poem" is irrelevant → reject). **Output** guardrails detect invalid/incorrect/hallucinated/harmful output (e.g. a response with no citations is invalid here). (21:12-22:16)
- **Layered metric ladder** beyond guardrail compliance: input-guardrail compliance (claim-rejection rate — investigate if too high, but you can only investigate what you measured), output-guardrail compliance (missing-citation rate), **response quality** (faithfulness — is the approve/reject actually rooted in the retrieved guidelines/policies), at least one **domain/application-specific metric** (claim processing time — the North Star), and **system-health** metrics (avg token cost/usage, avg turns per conversation, cost per recommendation). (22:00-24:00)
- **Monitoring** in production keeps the offline metrics running and adds *implicit* indicators of success/health: how often a human overrides the AI verdict (want it low; rising above a threshold → investigate) and how long a human takes to review the AI recommendation (too long may mean verbose/confusing responses). (23:59-25:10)
- **Optimize before production** — accuracy looking good isn't enough; cost, latency, and reliability become non-negotiable, so expect more iterations between "accuracy is good" and shipping. Optimizing *accuracy* in LLM apps is really optimizing what lands in the context window: prompt engineering, reranking (order retrieved info by relevance), and memory/persistence (patient history in MongoDB is already a form of memory; consider what else to persist across sessions). *Cost/latency*: semantic caching (reuse decisions from similar past claims), batch processing (process claims in batches). *Reliability*: mostly API-failure handling, and specifically **structured outputs** to guarantee the response always carries the decision *and* the citations. (25:10-27:15)
- Takeaways: think deeply about product requirements before having AI generate code (the spec is the hard part now); identify business and performance constraints first because they shape every downstream architecture decision; design the simplest system that meets your needs, evaluate it, then iterate — the most common mistake is over-engineering before knowing what's actually failing, or not evaluating at all; build evaluation in from the start ("you can't improve what you can't measure"). Resource pointer: MongoDB's GenAI cookbook (retrieval techniques, agentic design patterns, eval/optimization examples). (27:17-28:40)
