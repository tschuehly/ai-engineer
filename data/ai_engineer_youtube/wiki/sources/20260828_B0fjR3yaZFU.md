## Summary

Varun Shenoy is a co-founder of Long Lake, a holding company that does not sell AI software but buys the businesses it deploys into — 35 services businesses across HOA and property management, architecture, and HR services, plus an announced $6.3 billion take-private of American Express Global Business Travel. That position is the talk's premise rather than its subject: "when the AI doesn't work, it's not their problem. We're not the vendor. It's our problem. We are the operator owners." He frames the whole problem as diffusion, on the electricity analogy — demoed at Edison's Pearl Street Station in the 1880s, but Ford's electrified moving assembly line arrives in 1924, because having the technology was never enough; you had to rip out the existing motors, bring in new equipment, and retrain everybody.

The engineering core is an autonomy ladder and one rung on it that nobody has climbed outside code. The ladder runs copilot (a RAG chatbot), synchronous agent (Claude Code, Codex, Claude Cowork; one to five minutes of tool calls), asynchronous agent (background work, external triggers, an async job queue), long-running agent (hours to months), and AI coworker. For code, the async rung is "largely solved": wrap the same coding agent in a sandbox, let it build and test, and have it hand back a PR. Two properties make that work and neither is portable. The substrate — "we've figured out what the async and forking mechanism for code is. You just spin up a bunch of sandboxes and do work" — has no equivalent for scoping a building or coordinating a roof repair. And the reception habit is code-specific: engineers "launch 10 jobs and be comfortable with the fact that job seven might finish before job three," whereas "people clean out their inbox one email by one email, not 10 emails at once." His two live questions follow from that: how to parallelize work that is traditionally serial, and whether to sidestep the model gap entirely by representing knowledge work as code, since "the models are trained on code, they want to write code, they're incredibly good at writing code."

The data argument is that "the most valuable tasks are not on the internet" — closing the books when receipts are missing, scoping a building from a blueprint, coordinating vendors for a broken roof — and that this knowledge sits in people's heads, in twenty-year-old software, and in one senior person who just knows how to do it. What owning the operation buys is not the corpus but the *label*: "there is a ground truth here. In the case of the roofing example, the question is, did the roof get repaired? Did the books get closed?" That produces evals "built and scored automatically," a weekly ratchet in which "our hill climbing benchmarks become a regression test," an implicit feedback signal in "the real diff between the data that the AI generated and what was ultimately submitted," and a post-training corpus that is "completely out of distribution for most frontier labs." Against the demo's smooth slope he sets what the work actually is: "there are hills and ravines. There's death by a thousand paper cuts… That's the entire job. The exceptions are the job."

The closing argument is organizational. Continual learning and enablement are usually owned by different teams — research and platform engineering on one side, growth or deployment or customer experience on the other, "usually pretty siloed" — and he treats them as one loop: "the agent only improves if people actually use it. And people only use the agent if it's worth adopting." The loop has no seed, which is the elephant: "everyone assumes the usage just shows up… It never does. Getting a 100-year-old firm to change its processes is hard. You could have the best AI coworker on Earth. And if the person who's closed the books for the last 20 years continues to do things the same way, nothing changes." His answer, borrowed from Jensen Huang's "extreme hardware software co-design," is extreme software–service co-design: meet people metaphorically, by embedding into Excel, the ERP, the 3D design software, or Outlook "so that the energy required for enablement is kept low," and physically, by getting on a plane, running a cotton-candy stand at their trade conference, and asking about the job while mountain biking. "You cannot co-design software with the services business over Zoom or over a support ticket."

## Provenance and Limits

Nothing in this talk is measured. There are no accuracy figures, adoption rates, eval scores, latency numbers, or before/after comparisons anywhere in the seventeen minutes. The flywheel, the internal post-training, and the automatically scored evals are described as underway rather than as results; no example of a passing or failing eval is shown, and the number of tasks under evaluation is never given.

The speaker's structural position is also his thesis. Long Lake's differentiator is that it owns the businesses, and the talk's two strongest claims — that the valuable ground truth is only visible to an operator, and that co-design is "only possible from being within under the same roof" — are exactly the claims that make that ownership necessary. Neither is tested against a remote-first or vendor-side deployment; both should be read as an argued operating model rather than as a comparison.

The async-services rung is explicitly unsolved, by his own account: he presents it as the frontier he wakes up thinking about, not as something Long Lake has built. The "represent knowledge work as code" idea is posed as a question with no mechanism, no example, and no result. The autonomy ladder is a slide taxonomy, not a measurement instrument, and the claim that you must climb it in order is supported by reasoning rather than by a failed attempt to skip rungs. The corporate figures ($3 billion raised, 35 businesses, the $6.3 billion take-private) are company statements relayed from slides.
