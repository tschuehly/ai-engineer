## Summary

Arturo Nunez, who spent almost ten years at Unity, diagnoses why a coding agent pointed at a conventional game engine reinvents the same camera-follow script every time: the API surface the agent must speak is the engine's implementation vocabulary (mesh, renderer, animator, rigid body, collider, audio source), not the game's design vocabulary, so an intent like "a camera that follows my character" has nothing to bind to and must be re-derived from primitives on every request. Nereu inverts the layering — everything in the world is an asset, and the user or the assistant attaches *intent* tags ("character," "animated," "double jump," "vehicle," "drivable") that built-in systems query, an Entity Component System / data-oriented-design idea applied to the agent's action surface. The assistant's whole job becomes adding and removing tags rather than writing code, which makes tag composition the only degree of freedom and produces free absurdities like tagging a building drivable and racing it. Two implementation details are the most transferable part of the talk: context is assembled with rendering's level-of-detail idea (assets near the user's current edit arrive with full tag values, farther ones collapse to a position and a type, and the hundred pieces of grass are simply omitted), and the 6,000-7,000-asset library was made searchable by screenshotting each 3D model and running a vision model over the render, because only filenames and geometry existed. He closes by scoping the assistant to getting the user unstuck rather than one-shotting a finished game, and by placing generated world models in a different category — "a different medium even if we call them video games" — bounded by 60 FPS at 4K plus simulated physics.

## Notes

- Opening demo, all by description and with no code: add a robot (the assistant searches assets "tagged as robot or the description are… a robot"), make it move with WASD and animate it, add buildings from a library of matching assets, add rain (which "will spawn a particle system that's set up or configured as… rain"), and add a camera that follows the character. 00:47-03:30. His framing of the vocabulary is that "these descriptions are pretty common if you know or if you've played games… it's language that people who play games kind of understand," so "you don't need to code or… learn about importing models." 01:47-02:06.
- Credentials and motive: "I worked at Unity, a game engine company for almost 10 years… I saw a lot of people building games and struggling with the same things over and over," then MongoDB, then helping a startup with version control of game assets. 04:08-04:49. The product goal is stated as enjoying the process over the end product, explicitly not a route to shipping commercial games. 05:00-05:32.
- Why current engines plus current agents do not compose: "we're just building a bridge between two worlds, and it's not optimal, and you still need to know kind of like what to ask in vocabulary of an engine, in the vocabulary of code. Otherwise… the LLM goes rogue and does stuff, and reinvents the wheel over and over." 06:55-07:28.
- The concrete symptom: "if I say, 'I want a camera that follows this character,' I've seen the demos, and the LLM reinvents the wheel every single time. Well, when the result is going to be essentially the same." 07:28-07:41.
- The diagnosis, stated as a layering error rather than a model deficiency: "by default, the context is on the game engine rather than on the game design part, and I think we should flip that idea." 07:41-07:50.
- The boilerplate that vocabulary imposes: to control a character in a current engine you need to import a mesh and then think about "a renderer, an animator, a rigid body, and collider for physics, an audio source, and then you add your movement logic and your game rules." Most of it "is just boilerplate that every single game out there has," yet "developers need to understand and read the descriptions of those components and what the hundreds of sliders do." 07:50-08:27.
- The inversion: "everything is just an asset. Everything has to be rendered on screen. Everything has physics most of the time… and you just add tags to describe the intent of those assets in your game. So, I want this to be a character, to be animated, to double jump." He grounds the vocabulary choice in tutorials: "the tutorial tells you, 'Oh, press A to jump and press A again while you're in the air to do a double jump.' That's the language that we should be using." 08:28-09:06. Event rules are described the same way — "when you collect a coin, I want you to increase the score." 09:06-09:16.
- The mechanism, named ATS (captioned "asset tax system," i.e. asset tag system): "comes from the idea from game development called the entity component system, data oriented design, which means we just describe objects with components or in this case with tags. And then there are systems that query for all the assets in the world and say… I'm going to move all the objects that have the vehicle, the player, and the drivable tag." 09:29-10:11.
- The composition consequence, shown on a slide: a building with no tags does not move, but "nothing prevents you from adding the vehicle and drivable tag to your building and then you have a building that you can put in a Mario Kart style of game." 10:14-10:36.
- The assistant's scope in-session: "the AI assistant essentially helps you get unstuck. Like if I don't know how to make the car move, I just ask it and it knows, understands what are the tools available, what are the tags available, and then it applies them to the asset that I'm talking about." 10:37-10:53.
- Prompt assembly inputs: the user query, context from the scene, extra context based on the *type* of assets in the game ("if you're using robots, if you're using something like medieval"), and a user-supplied description of the type of game being made. "And then the agent just performs calls and appends or removes the tags. All these tags and all these systems are built into the engine." 10:54-11:48.
- A deliberate omission: "we don't have a scripting system in there. That's on purpose. But it's just JavaScript and runs on the browser. So, whoever wants to extend, they can… But for most users that shouldn't be the case." 11:48-12:05.
- Where he says the real difficulty sits: "the challenging part is the composing games into these tags and into these systems. Because there are a lot of genres, there are a lot of ways to describe the same game, there are things like the mood of the game. Like I want to make a platformer but that feels like scary. Well, that also touches on things like the post-processing effects and things like the lighting." 12:09-12:45.
- How the engine itself is built: discuss the intent with Claude Code (captioned "Cloud Code"), consult a friend in the industry who specializes in the area at hand (e.g. lighting), bring that back in, and delete settings and features he has not seen users use "in order to simplify the engine." Then "as we build, we give the definition of the tools to the assistant. So, every time we add new tools, the assistant knows the tools that we're adding." Status is a closed alpha with users giving feedback. 12:45-14:04.
- Asset tagging: "I've used also vision models mostly to tag the assets that we have because it's like six or 7,000 assets. I could not manually tag them all… I just have the names and the 3D file. So, I took a screenshot and ran a vision model to describe those." The runtime assistant is otherwise "mostly an LLM." 14:04-14:47.
- The context problem: "if we feed the entire scene to the LLM, the context grows a lot. Let's say in this scene I think I had like 100 assets… But, most of them are like grass that we could ignore. It doesn't really make sense." 14:48-15:07.
- The borrowed solution, first stated in its rendering form: level of detail means a near object gets "a higher quality texture… material… model," while "something that's too far away from the camera, I'm just going to maybe just put a cube and the user won't be able to tell because it's so far away." 15:07-15:44.
- Applied to context: with the user clicking on the knight, "the things that are around it might have a higher priority, so we feed them information about the tags that they have and the values of the settings on those tags. Things that are nearby, we just say, 'Okay, there's something that's a player here at this position, but I'm not going to send you the entire context.'… And as a user keeps moving around and modifies things, then we update that and feed the assistant with more relevant information about the game." 15:44-16:33.
- The product position: "I don't want us to one-shot games that nobody is going to play and I don't see the point in that. There's in the industry, of course, the need for games that are one-shotted, but here the idea is that we allow people to make games and experience that and have fun and share those games with their families and friends." 16:33-17:12. The stated learning outcome is domain language: "that they learn along the way the language of making games, the language of game design, not necessarily the coding or programming" — with the aside that some users report understanding programming concepts anyway, "but that was not the initial goal." 17:11-17:33.
- On generated world models as the alternative branch: "there's another kind of branch of engines or tools… called world models, which are being generated on the fly… I think that's going to be a different medium even if we call them video games." The bar he names: "games… have to render 60 frames per second. And doing that at 4K resolutions in real time with a world model, I think it's still far away. And on top of that… simulating physics and stuff, it's very difficult, but I think it's exciting what's going on." 17:33-18:20.
