## Summary

Will Bond and Ameya Ketkar describe uReview, the automated code-review system Uber built in-house for thousands of engineers across hundreds of teams, twelve sites, and six language-specific monorepos. The motivating measurement is a single internal metric: time to first review on a pull request went from about three hours in 2024 to nine hours in 2026, alongside growth in both PR count and PR size, which makes review the bottleneck rather than authoring (00:51-01:22). They built instead of bought for three reasons — Uber runs Phabricator and "most of the solutions do not provide support for Phabricator"; they want the inner loop and the outer loop reviewed by one service so "our agents are getting the same code review, the same rules, everything applied as our humans do"; and with hundreds of teams they cannot centrally hold the rules or the knowledge, so the system has to plug into the existing ownership model rather than replicate it (01:22-02:25).

The architecture is a fan-out with a filtering waist. Three surfaces (GitHub, Phabricator, and the agent loop) feed one uReview service that takes review requests, ingests user feedback, and routes to a set of generators "tuned for different performance and cost avenues," with third-party review systems pluggable as comparison arms. Because several generators run over the same diff, comment volume is an emergent property, so a post-processing stage rates, categorizes, filters, and deduplicates before anything reaches an engineer (03:07-04:10).

The observability half is the instructive part. uReview started as a single prompt doing per-file logic checks, a simple thorough-review agent, and a dispatcher choosing between them, instrumented only with cost, an NPS survey, Google Forms, and Slack support — under which the quality-to-cost ratio was "all over the place" (04:33-05:11). They added classified sentiment on developer replies, then addressal rate (did the developer actually change the code), then a runtime profile of the agent trajectory — tool calls and thinking — which supplies the *why* behind an outcome metric (05:11-06:35). The premise driving all of it is stated flatly: "the model doesn't know that it's wrong. It always confidently says 100% sure" (06:35-06:46). Two responses follow: bake each team's style guide and anti-patterns into the agent, and add guardrails that "tell the agent what not to waste turns doing," because review has to finish inside a time span and wasted turns degrade the review (06:46-07:20).

Customization runs a ladder — single-file and multi-file reviewers, a general-purpose per-file logic-bug pass, a deep multi-file review carrying each monorepo's anti-pattern style guides, few-shot "AI linters" that gather context deterministically and then run rules over it, and finally custom team agents linked to a knowledge base and past PRs with a skill to do the review (07:37-08:41). Ketkar is blunt that the authoring was never the hard part: teams wrote skills by asking Claude to read their previous PR reviews and write one, while "the hard part was how to run these skills at scale with consistent quality and low cost" (09:43-10:12). Reported results: about 25,000 comments a week, 10% of comments drawing any feedback, 4% of PRs drawing negative feedback, a ~67% overall addressal rate, nearly three-quarters of high-severity issues addressed, and against their own naive first implementation, cost down 60% with "quality and accuracy up by around 70%" (10:12-11:02). Bond closes on the inner-versus-outer loop: inner-loop accuracy requirements are *higher*, because a low-quality comment sends an agent into rework it will happily perform, while nits that infuriate humans cost agents nothing — and rather than killing the outer loop, human review moves up a layer to architecture, domain expertise, and product thinking (12:14-14:34).

## Provenance and Limits

- Every number is self-reported by the team that built the system, from slides, with no methodology shown. There is no external baseline, no A/B test, and no published dataset.
- The headline improvement — "our costs were down by 60% and our quality and our accuracy was up by around 70%" — is measured against "a very naive implementation," which is their own earlier build (a single prompt plus one agent plus a dispatcher). It is a self-comparison against a starting point they chose, not a comparison against a vendor product or against human review. What "quality and accuracy" means is not defined, and no measurement instrument for it is described.
- Addressal rate is a proxy that the talk does not validate. A developer can address a comment that was wrong, and can ignore a comment that was right; nothing in the talk reports precision, false-positive rate, or a human-labelled sample against which the 67% could be interpreted.
- The two engagement figures have different denominators, stated back to back: "10% of them" (comments) get some feedback and "only 4% of the PRs" get some negative feedback. Neither can be converted into the other, and the talk does not reconcile them.
- The motivating metric — three hours to nine hours for first review — is presented without stating what share of the increase is attributable to agent-authored code as opposed to headcount growth, reorganization, or the Phabricator-to-GitHub migration in progress. The causal attribution is implied, not shown.
- The talk reports no change in time to first review after uReview shipped, which is the metric the system was built to move. Comments, addressal, and cost are reported; the bottleneck metric is not.
- The inner-loop claims — that low-quality comments send agents into backwards rework, and that agents will happily fix a hundred nits — are stated as observations from operating the system, with no rate, incidence, or cost figure attached.
- The build-versus-buy reasoning is specific to Uber's constraints (Phabricator, six monorepos, an existing ownership model) and is not offered as general advice; the third-party comparison arms exist precisely because they do not claim their system is better than what is available.
