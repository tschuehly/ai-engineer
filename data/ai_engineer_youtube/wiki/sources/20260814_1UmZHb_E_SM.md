## Summary

Patricija Žemaitytė (product manager at Oxylabs, a web-intelligence platform and proxy provider founded in 2015) tells three delivery stories from the layer that feeds models live public web data, and each one carries a reusable engineering lesson. A video API request with a two-week deadline and a floor of 5 petabytes per month turned out to be a request for a pipeline, then compounded — downloader, transcripts, subtitles (the client's "transcripts" ask was actually subtitles, which is why every request failed at first), language search, metadata, an internal glue library — into a product suite in about three months. A sub-second search API for AI systems could not be reached by tuning the 4-second general scraper, because the general scraper deliberately collects ads, widgets, rich results, and AI overviews while an AI-facing API needs only organic results, top stories, and news; the rebuild hit ~650 ms P90 in under two weeks and then got blocked live on the client demo call, which is her clearest statement of the talk's theme — "there is a difference between a system that works in development, a system that works in a test, and a system that actually survives reality." The second rebuild reached 550 ms average by hunting seconds across layouts, parsers, sessions, and proxies with browsers as an unavoidable slow dependency. The third story is scale: 10,000 → 60,000 requests per second in under two months, where a "request" is an end-to-end scraping job, the bottleneck surfaced in load testing rather than an outage, and the hard part was producing organic-looking traffic and trusting the numbers, since telemetry at that volume becomes part of the load it measures.

## Notes

- Framing: most talks start with models, "this one starts somewhere less glamorous with infrastructure that decides whether those models get fresh, usable, real-time data at all." Public web data is theoretically available to everyone, but connecting models, agents, and databases to it requires an infrastructure layer. Training still matters, but "training alone is no longer enough" — models need live search and real external data to stay useful (00:01-01:22).
- The video API story: a sales trip to San Francisco produced "there is a demand for video API for AI training," with a two-week deadline and a floor of at least 5 petabytes per month, at a point when they had built nothing like it. Delivered on time as version one: a new dedicated scraper with new logic, new storage integrations, and a new delivery flow (02:33-04:03).
- The definition mismatch: transcript support was built, all client requests failed, and the cause was not a defect — the client needed subtitles, not transcripts. Then came search across languages, then metadata and channel information, then an internal library gluing it together (04:03-05:34).
- Roughly three months produced the full video API suite (downloaders, transcripts, subtitles, channel information). "It's 2026. Client already gathered 30 petabytes of data, and we're still waiting for a payment" (05:34-06:10).
- SERP's changing role: search data used to serve analytics, SEO, monitoring, and market intelligence; now it feeds retrieval pipelines, grounds answers, powers assistants, and lets agents "interact with live information instead of stale training memory." She cites Google's grounding documentation as explicitly positioning Google Search as a way to connect models to current public knowledge — "the model layer is increasingly expected to work with live retrieval layer around it" (06:21-07:25).
- Latency history: a 2024 client asked for sub-second SERP delivery against a ~4-second average baseline; they built it, the client never tested it, "the market wasn't ready," and it was shelved. A 2025 client asked for zero data retention, sub-second latency (under 800 ms), geolocation and query parameters, and a testable solution in under two weeks (07:25-09:10).
- Why the old scraper could not be tuned into the new one: the regular scraper is "built to retrieve as much information as possible" — ads, widgets, rich results, AI-generated results, different layouts — while the fast search API "focuses on the things that actually matters only for AI systems," mostly organic results, top stories, and news, cutting away the heavy layout (08:00-08:34).
- The rebuild reached ~650 ms P90 in under two weeks, then the live block on the client call forced a restart; the second iteration depended on browsers, which are "amazing" but "slow, expensive, complex, and deeply incompatible with dreams about low latency" (09:10-10:49).
- The time hunt: no magic trick, just reviewing layouts, parsers, sessions, and proxies for every place a second could be cut — "this is how systems becomes fast, not by giant breakthroughs… but by small decision that adds up." Current state: 550 ms average, and scale from 400 million to almost 6 billion daily requests, which changes cost, observability, and failure domains (10:49-12:06).
- The product-boundary claim: "in AI era, speed is not just performance. Speed actually defines what product can exist" — 4 seconds is a slow pipeline, sub-second is "something that can sit and interact in your AI workflows" (12:06-12:26).
- The unblocker (a scraper with proxy integration) went from ~10,000 to 60,000 requests per second in under two months. That number is misleading if read as simple HTTP: one request is the end-to-end scraping job — routing, rendering, proxy handling, browser execution, parsing, retries, normalization, delivery. "Even adding up additional 2,000 servers doesn't solve the problem. You need an architecture… central components that actually are reliable… observability that still tells you the truth… and testing that resembles reality enough to matter" (12:33-13:47).
- Load testing was where the bottleneck appeared, not an outage. Synthetic traffic was relatively easy; "organic data testing" — traffic that behaves enough like real client usage to be informative — was the hard part. They hit a wall around 20,000 rps: "there is no question if the system is actually working. It is working. The question becomes, do we actually know that it can go further? And that uncertainty was the real bottleneck" (13:43-14:44).
- Observability at scale: collecting logs is hard, processing them harder, and "the telemetry itself becomes a part of the load and a part of the complexity." Resolution was gradual scaling plus accepting that "the real testing is going to be with production traffic," which went fine (14:44-15:31).
- Internally the effort was "Project 60" (60,000 rps); before it finished, demand moved and it became "Project 150." "Scale is never a finish line… when you reach one target number, the next one will appear." The transcript's spoken figure for the new target is garbled; the video description states Project 60 is already Project 150 (15:31-16:10).
- Closing position: the company is "not just a proxy provider" — the larger job is reaching the open web, collecting data reliably, dealing with anti-bot systems, handling browsers when needed, and structuring and delivering data so AI companies can plug it in. "The targets change, layouts change, detection changes, market itself changes, client needs changes. So, this is not a build-once business. This is an adapt-forever business," and the closing thesis is that the next generation of AI "will not be powered by better models. It will be powered by better infrastructure around it" (16:10-18:22).
