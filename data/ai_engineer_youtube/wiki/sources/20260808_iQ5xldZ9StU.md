## Summary

Idan Gazit leads GitHub Next, the lab that built Copilot, and tours two prototypes along the two axes he thinks survive whatever the technology does next: automation and collaboration. The automation half is Agentic Workflows, in public preview at the time of the talk. He wrote roughly three lines of plain English — "every day I want you to check if there's a new release, look at the change log, look at the docs, come up with a plan for the upgrade, and then create a PR" — and Copilot expanded it into a full playbook that carried his personal site from Astro 5 to Astro 7, two majors at once, fixed the code that broke, verified the build, and flagged the manual steps it could not take. The workflow *is* a Markdown document: "the markdown is the source code. The YAML is like a compiled artifact. You never look at it."

The guardrails are the part he asks the audience to remember. Prompting an agent to behave is not a guardrail, because the prompt is the same channel an attacker writes to: "if you're prompting the guardrails at the agent, you're effectively letting the fox loose in the henhouse. It's not actually a guardrail." Permissions, allowed tools, reachable network destinations and permitted writes are declared deterministically in the workflow's YAML front matter instead. Two of those declarations are unusual. `pull-request: single` caps the *output* — "I don't want the agent to get prompt injected to create 500 pull requests. That would be a denial of service" — and the workflow is explicitly allowed to do nothing at all, "because in a world where I have lots of automations, the last thing I want is noise. I don't want the agents denial of servicing me." Secrets stay outside the agent's jail entirely and are brokered by a warden the agent has to ask; a secret an agent can see should be treated as already compromised.

The class of work this unlocks is named by contrast: "until now the only automations that we had were heuristics like make sure there's a semicolon at the end of every line," and now the automatable set includes anything needing "some amount of basic judgment and intelligence." The canonical example is not his own: the Home Assistant project's first Agentic Workflow reads every submitted issue, walks the Python stack trace to decide whether the bug is in first-party or third-party code, and closes the issue if it is not theirs — "not possible before AI, not possible with heuristics, but is possible now." The shipped starter library includes an issue triager, a CI doctor, a repo-assist swarm, and daily team and repo status, which he pitches at product managers as much as engineers: "that's how you actually get industrial scale." His bet is that unattended automation will be "a bigger category than interactive AI, because automations that run in the background while you sleep, that's the ballgame."

The collaboration half is ACE, a second GitHub Next prototype the wiki already covers from Maggie Appleton's earlier talk. Gazit adds a selection criterion for what a shared human-agent surface is actually for: "anything that's in code, any fact that's in code, the agents can figure out by reading the code. What's left are the things that are not in code" — the political considerations, the VP's favourite colour, the infrastructure deal that quietly picks Azure over GCP or AWS. Every session is a cloud micro-VM holding a branch, ACE deliberately resembles a chat app, and the payoff of putting the conversation and the agent in one place is that a decision reached in the thread does not have to be restated: "I don't want to turn around and now emit those instructions again. Instead, I just want to be like, yo, ACE, do it." He notes that models are good at recovering the final state from a conversation that changed its mind twice, "so I don't need to work for the robots," and projects development toward editing a shared plan document and telling the agent to "make the document true."

He closes on a study he does not name: a longitudinal study of about a hundred developers over thousands of hours, in which hands-on-keyboard typing is roughly 5% of the job — "and that's what all the tools have been helping us to do so far." The remaining 95% (where the system is, how it works today, what other people think should change about it) is the target he sets for both prototypes.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Bound What an Unattended Automation May Emit, Including Emitting Nothing](../concepts/bound-what-an-unattended-automation-may-emit.md) - safe outputs cap the write side by kind and by count, and staying silent is an explicitly granted permission.
- [The Markdown Workflow Is the Source; the YAML Is a Compiled Artifact](../concepts/the-markdown-workflow-is-source-the-yaml-is-a-compiled-artifact.md) - a three-line message compiles into a playbook plus a capability manifest, and you change behavior by editing the English.
- [Tell the Agent Only What Is Not Recoverable From the Code](../concepts/tell-the-agent-only-what-is-not-recoverable-from-the-code.md) - the agent's own code-reading competence defines the residue humans must supply, and that residue is what a shared surface carries.
- [Automate the Chores That Needed Judgment, Not the Ones That Needed Rules](../concepts/automate-the-chores-that-needed-judgment-not-rules.md) - the newly automatable work is what a heuristic could never decide but a human decides routinely from evidence already in the repo.

## Provenance and Limits

- This is a vendor lab demo by the person who leads the lab. Both artifacts are prototypes or previews of GitHub products, and every capability claim is a live demo or an assertion, not a measurement.
- Nothing in the talk is quantified. There is no success rate for the upgrade workflow across runs, no false-PR rate, no measure of how often the "do nothing" branch fires, no cost figure, and no data on how the starter workflows perform in the repositories that adopted them. The Astro 5→7 run is a single demonstration on the speaker's own personal website, a small codebase with a build he can eyeball via preview deploy.
- The security architecture is described, not demonstrated or audited. The warden/broker, the staged writes, and the safe-outputs enforcement are asserted properties of the runtime; no threat model, no red-team result, and no discussion of what happens when the compiler itself is the injection target.
- The closing 5% statistic is given with no author, publication, year, or link — only "a longitudinal study conducted on like 100 developers over thousands of hours." Treat it as a framing device rather than a citable finding.
- The Home Assistant example is second-hand: it is reported about an external open-source project, with no outcome data on triage accuracy or wrongly closed issues.
- The ACE material overlaps heavily with [Maggie Appleton's earlier GitHub Next talk](20260426_ClWD8OEYgp8.md) on the same prototype. Where the two agree, this source is a later status report by the team's lead rather than an independent confirmation.
