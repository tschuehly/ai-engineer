## Summary

Yu Su — professor at Ohio State and COO of NeoCognition — gives an explicitly conceptual talk ("it won't be too technical") that separates two things he says the field keeps collapsing together. **Intelligence** is "the capacity to reason through unfamiliar problems from available context," which frontier models keep getting better at and where "every episode is more or less independent from each other." **Expertise** is "accumulated and situated competence" — acting reliably, efficiently, and with judgment in a particular domain. His account of why coding agents work while everything else stays brittle is a modern Moravec's paradox: code is "already a language-native world," symbolic, structured, with tests standing in for rewards, while the rest of digital work is "millions of these micro worlds," each with "its unique local physics," too heterogeneous for any monolithic model to "compress into one static representation."

The slide he calls "maybe the most important figure in this talk" plots raw intelligence against expertise and finds them "largely orthogonal." Scale intelligence alone and you get "the world's smartest novice" — brilliant at whatever it is handed, accumulating nothing, "brute forcing its way at every problem." Operationally, "intelligence tends to expand our search" (spin up a hundred parallel attempts) while "expertise will actually try to compress the search space" because the shortcuts are already learned; intelligence solves a problem *through* the context it was given, whereas expertise *brings the right context* in the first place. Continual learning is the bridge, and he defines it precisely — "adaptive compression of experience into reusable structures for future behavior" — with each of the four terms an open design axis. The provocation he closes on is **unbounded expertise from bounded intelligence**: if continual learning gets good enough past some threshold of raw capability, "we don't need a stronger intelligence anymore," and the thing worth scaling stops being the model.

Provenance and limits: this is a position talk with no measurements, no benchmark, and no system results. The only figure quoted is secondhand and illustrative (Anthropic revenue). The speaker is COO of a company whose stated focus is "agents and continual learning," and the closing call to action — scale expertise, every company builds its own learning loop — is that company's positioning. The open questions he leaves (how to define and measure expertise, how to reconcile reliability with plasticity, how to synergize parametric and non-parametric learning) are stated as unsolved.

## Notes

- Speaker and affiliations (00:20-00:34): professor at Ohio State, and COO at NeoCognition, "and we focus on agents and continual learning." The talk is framed as conceptual: "it will be mainly a conceptual one. But I think it's a very important conceptual distinction."
- The two motivating puzzles (00:57-01:19): "why we are so successful at the coding agents, but they're so terrible at anything else," and why "the current agents are so token inefficient… to the degree that every company right now is like coming out and try to curb their [token spend]."
- Agents are not new (01:25-02:09): 1960s-80s expert systems and logical agents, 2010s deep-RL neural agents — each captured "some very limited facets of human intelligence," whether logical reasoning, single-modality perception, or decision-making.
- What multimodal language agents changed (02:09-02:51): "for the first time, we have a neural model that is able to encode multi-sensory inputs into a unified neural representation that is also conducive to symbolic reasoning and communication… that was a trait unique to humans." He credits this with improving "expressiveness, reasonability, and adaptivity" and calls it "a new evolutionary stage of machine intelligence."
- Coding as first mass market (02:51-03:26): illustrated with Anthropic's revenue curve — "in just under 2 years, their revenue has grown 400 times to 40 billion. I think the newest number is maybe 60 billion annualized run[rate]" — "largely driven by coding and coding-related productivity capabilities." Secondhand and used only as illustration; no source given.
- Why code was ideal (03:26-03:49): "code is already a language-native world. Everything is already represented symbolically and recorded in a very structured way. And you get your rewards, you get your tests all in place in symbolic ways."
- Leaving the privileged world of code (03:49-04:42): "Well, not so well." Enterprise and personal deployments hit "quite brittle and silly errors." He invokes Andrew Ng's line that "it's not going to be the year of agents, it's going to be the decade of agents because they cannot do computer use, they don't have continual learning," and adds that while Ng's view may have shifted with coding agents, "the difficulties with computer use, with continual learning [are] still largely the same right now."
- The paradox (04:42-05:25): "How can something be so sm[art] but also so brittle at the same time?" His thesis is "a modern version of the Moravec's paradox" — "hard things are easy, easy things are hard." We excel at symbolic reasoning tasks "like coding and math, which were considered crown jewel of intelligence earlier," yet "still struggle with this everyday digital work because they really require quite different set of cognitive competencies."
- Microworlds (05:25-06:24): "modern society is really not just one unified world. It's millions of these micro worlds… every domain, every profession is different, every company is different. Even if you're using the same software, every company configure it differently." Each has "its unique local physics, like different structures, constraints, affordances, and dynamics that you have to learn." The conclusion: "it's just like too heterogeneous and dynamic for any monolithic model to try to compress it into one static representation. So, agents must continually learn on the job to acquire what I call specialized expertise for each specific microworld."
- Intelligence, defined (06:33-07:01): "the capacity to reason through unfamiliar problems from available context. This is what the frontier models are increasingly good at. You give it the problem statement, the context, the tools, and it can reason through this even if it's a [thing] done for the first time… Every episode is more or less independent from each other here."
- Expertise, defined (07:01-07:19): "accumulated and situated competence… the ability to act reliably, efficiently, and with judgment to achieve [reproducibly superior] performance in a particular domain." (The captions garble the middle of this phrase; see Caption Artifacts.)
- Experts see differently (07:25-08:02): the cognitive-science claim is that "experts don't just know more facts. They actually see the world differently." Pattern recognition example: an expert reading "a gigantic bug report… can immediately locate the most plausible places where things could go wrong."
- Deep structure (08:02-08:22): "When you are scheduling a meeting, you know that it's not just like finding the shared slot on everyone's calendar — [it] is actually a constraint optimization problem over everyone's authority, the priorities, the urgency, and everything."
- Conditionality and judgment (08:22-08:53): "experts don't just operate with a set of rules, a set of facts. We know that every single thing is conditional. Every rule has like the preconditions where it applies, but then we also know when we can bend the rules when exceptions happen." And: judgment and taste means knowing "what's high quality and, very importantly, when to stop, when it's good enough."
- World model (08:53-09:20): "experts effectively have built a world model of their environments… a generalized notion of world model that captures how that microworld works, and that becomes the basis for all of our perception, reasoning, decision-making, and judgment."
- Context selection (09:20-09:53): "intelligence is about, hey, when we have the context, how to solve the problem through the context. But expertise actually will bring you the right context. Given any problem, we know what context [to] bring in are important for this problem and bring it in to solve the problem."
- Expand versus compress (09:53-10:27): "every problem solving is a search problem. So intelligence tends to brute force it — try to spin up like 100 different parallel ways to try to solve the problem. Well, expertise will actually try to compress the search space because expertise has learned the essential shortcuts for the problem space. So that whenever you have a problem, you know the most plausible ways to solve it."
- Continual learning as the bridge (10:27-10:53): "continual learning is the important bridge from intelligence to expertise," followed immediately by a complaint that the term "is such a confusing term" — he notes an earlier speaker (captioned "Jack") gave "like 10 different names" for it.
- The definition (10:53-11:08): "continual learning is **adaptive compression of experience into reusable structures for future behavior**."
- The four elements, each an open axis (11:08-12:41): **experience** — "episodes of experience, or… semantic facts or procedures or feedback from human or in environments"; **compression** — "we embed them into vectors, or we index them into some symbolic structure, we distill them into model parameters, or do some kind of a reinforcement learning," and it must be *adaptive*, since "what you have compressed so far should largely influence how you compress further"; **structure** — "parameters like adapters of your language models, or vectors, graphs, or skills, or even [world] models"; **use** — "just to recall these facts, or… for prediction of future states… for better planning, or even for the control like actuation layer of the agent, or as a value function for potential states." The cross-product is his explanation for why the field reads as confusing.
- The orthogonality figure (12:41-13:10): "this is maybe the most important figure in this talk… if we put raw intelligence as the x-axis and expertise as the y-axis, I think we'll find that they are largely orthogonal to each other."
- World's smartest novice (13:01-13:26): with no continual learning, "all you do is scaling your model to get better raw intelligence, then what we will get is what I call the world's smartest novice. Like super smart, it can try to attack at any problem given to it, but it doesn't accumulate expertise, so it ends up as just like brute forcing its way at every problem."
- The learning algorithm sets the slope (13:26-14:06): "different continual learning algorithms will essentially set the slope of your learning curve here. If you have a sloppy CL algorithm, maybe some kind of simple in-context learning, then with increasing intelligence, your expertise will increase like a little bit. But if you have a really strong continual learning algorithm, then the expertise will increase rapidly." Stated caveat: "this is assuming like a given time horizon and the experience horizon."
- Unbounded expertise from bounded intelligence (14:06-15:14): "what if we can come up with a continual learning algorithm such that once the raw intelligence has [crossed] a certain threshold, we don't need a stronger intelligence anymore… continual learning will bring us like unbounded expertise once we have like a reasonable level of intelligence." He names this "the escape [velocity]" case (caption ambiguity) and draws the consequence: "do we need to continually train these larger and larger models? Or like, these models… maybe they're already good enough. What we're missing is just like better continual learning algorithms."
- The overarching open question (15:14-15:35): "given any domain or environment, how can an agent continue to learn to specialize and reach expert-level competency?"
- Measurement is unsolved (15:35-15:46): "How do you even define and measure expertise? And this is probably environment-specific."
- Reliability versus plasticity (15:46-16:32): "we want these agents to be both reliable and plastic. But they are inherently conflicting with each other. Reliable systems or stable systems, they resist the change. But the plastic systems likes change. So how do we reconcile that?" His grounds for optimism: "we do have a living existence proof, which is us ourselves, humans, that we are incredibly plastic, but also manage to be dependable most of the time."
- Parametric and non-parametric (16:32-16:49): "when we talk about learning, largely they are like this two forms of learning, parametric or non-parametric… my belief here is that both are really needed for this type of continual learning to actually work. But how do we synergize the two?"
- Specialization as a data opportunity (16:49-17:29): "even though we are focusing on specialization, I think there is a great potential for specialization to actually lead to like better generalization. We have exhausted the public data for training LLMs, but the next stage of training, the next internet-scale data opportunity is actually in all of these different private worlds. If we can make these specialized agent work, they can learn in situ and channel back the learning to the general model."
- Call to action (17:29-18:38): "let's start scaling expertise. This will be a new dimension for us to scale because intelligence is already becoming abundance. The frontier models — they are probably smarter than average humans. But expertise is still scarce." The world he wants: "everyone can have their personal health care, personal financial advisor, and personal tutors," and "every company can build their own learning loop." He cites Satya Nadella from "two weeks ago" on enabling "this human AI learning loop at each company that turns into institutional memory," and adds that companies should build "their own [moats]" and "still be in charge of their means of production."
- The economic close (18:38-19:11): "with abundance of expertise we will actually see more types of work become possible, because right now there are still a lot of opportunities that are locked up because the friction is just so high to make them economically viable. But with abundance of expertise… we will be able to lower the friction and make many of the new type of work across the threshold of worth doing."

## Provenance and Caveats

- The speaker is COO of NeoCognition, a company he states is focused on "agents and continual learning," and the talk closes with "this is the future we're building towards [at] NeoCognition." The call to action is that company's positioning, and no NeoCognition system, result, or product detail is presented.
- Nothing in the talk is measured. There is no benchmark, no ablation, no system evaluation, and no comparison against alternatives. The intelligence/expertise axes, the orthogonality claim, and the learning-curve slopes are drawn as a conceptual diagram, not plotted from data.
- The one quantitative claim — Anthropic's revenue growing "400 times to 40 billion," with "maybe 60 billion annualized" as a newer figure — is secondhand, unsourced, and used only to establish that coding is the first mass market. It should not be carried as a measurement.
- The Moravec's-paradox framing, the expert-cognition claims ("experts don't just know more facts. They actually see the world differently"), and the reliability/plasticity tension are attributed to cognitive science in general terms; no specific study is cited.
- The claim that "we have exhausted the public data for training LLMs" is asserted, not evidenced, and is contested elsewhere in the field. It is recorded here as the speaker's premise for the private-worlds argument.
