## Summary

Anuj Iravane leads AI at Anterior, a clinician-led company running AI for health plans across prior authorization, payment integrity, and HEDIS measures — work he summarizes as "policy-guided decision-making over highly unstructured data." The data that work depends on is scanned fax bundles of medical records, and Anterior's contracts forbid retaining, reusing, deriving from, redacting, or anonymizing it, so nothing survives into a dataset. Their answer is to generate the eval data instead, by running the inference workflow backwards: sample a label, sample a reasoning trace from the policy (which Anterior already models as a symbolic decision tree), then synthesize the record that would have produced it. A coarse-to-fine pipeline layers patient invariants into a journey of provider encounters and fans out into documents, with a consistency eval catching contradictions between documents generated in parallel and a round-trip check confirming the record still yields the sampled label — so labels are correct by construction and ground-truthing disappears. Clinicians own the pipeline because every stage is a skill on a generic internal agent harness. Roughly 90% of Anterior's datasets are now synthetic, and in a blind review clinicians separated synthetic from real only about 60% of the time.

## Notes

- Anterior is described as a clinician-led AI company for health plans, backed by Sequoia and NEA, building production agents for prior authorization, payment integrity, and HEDIS measures; all of it reduces to "policy-guided decision-making over highly unstructured data," 00:16-00:59.
- The input is scanned fax bundles of medical records — "around 70% of medical communication still happens via fax" — carrying bad handwriting, tables, checkboxes, key-value pairs, and images, drawn from "a very long tail of rare … cases with … nuanced scenarios," and modelling an entire clinical trajectory where "every single person's journey is very different," 00:59-01:53.
- His framing of the data: "an observation through a very fuzzy lens over an entire person's lifespan," 01:53-02:02.
- The accuracy premise: in healthcare "the baselines for accuracy are just exceptionally high. 95% is not good enough," which is why Anterior "invest[s] very deeply in datasets and evals," with these unstructured records as the staple source, 02:02-02:24.
- The constraint: the records are PHI, "we can't retain it, we can't reuse it, we can't even derive information from it," and "most of our contracts prohibit us." Redaction, anonymization, and derivative copies are "a strict no-no, completely off the table," so "nothing really survives in any sort of dataset that we want to persist," 02:29-02:54.
- The problem statement: "what do you do when the dataset you most need is also the data you're least allowed to keep," 02:54-03:00.
- The bet is synthetic generation, with frontier labs cited as precedent for generating synthetic data "for continued pre-training, for RL, for computer use, for agents," 03:07-03:26. He is positive on LLMs as a synthetic-data tool and notes prior work inside and outside healthcare, 03:36-03:52.
- One-shotting fails: it is "really hard to generate diverse realistic looking synthetic records," worse at scale, and records "are over 300 pages long" — "you wouldn't ask an LLM to write a novel for you in one shot," 03:52-04:31.
- Two stated causes of mode collapse: little exposure to this data source in the pre-training corpus, and pre-training/post-training objectives that "are not incentivized for creativity or diversity … they're incentivized to be helpful systems," 04:32-04:50.
- The forward task is unstructured data plus a policy, executed as a reasoning trace, arriving at an outcome that is the label, 05:00-05:18. The reversal: "start by sampling a random label, figuring out a reasoning trace for that label, and then trying to generate data backwards from that," which conditions generation on diverse inputs and "circumvent[s] the diversity problem," 05:21-05:48.
- Policies are concrete: the example is a CPAP device policy for a medical-necessity review workflow, enumerating patient conditions under which the device is approved or rejected, 05:53-06:24. Anterior "spend[s] a lot of time and energy in trying to model these policies explicitly as decision trees" using a symbolic representation, which "helps us achieve a better accuracy and consistency score when executing them in LLM-based workflow," 06:28-06:44.
- The payoff of the symbolic form is sampling: it gives "a way to kind of deterministically sample different reasoning traces for a given outcome," 06:44-07:00, and that distribution "is a much more uniform and effective prior distribution than what you'd normally get from an LLM," 07:00-07:18.
- Coverage argument: a 200-case customer sample scoring 95% "doesn't really tell you about … your performance … in those rare edge cases that are not in that data set," and edge cases will always sit outside the distribution because the data is so highly variant, 07:19-07:52. He notes others in the space sample scenarios from symbolic causal state representations for the same reason, 07:53-08:05.
- Pipeline, coarse to fine: patient invariants (biological sex, birth date, blood group), 08:10-08:33; invariants plus the reasoning trace produce "an ordered list of events and provider[s] that a patient might have had … we call this the patient journey," in natural language, 08:33-08:52; because in the real world documentation "is really generated" only during provider encounters, "we model exactly that," 08:58-09:11; a document plan per encounter, then a fan-out that generates and hydrates the documents using the plan and the preceding patient history, 09:12-09:25.
- The layering is what keeps "the different prompt payloads in the pipeline very token efficient … from both input and output perspective," and lets the pipeline scale to longer patient journeys "without overloading the context windows," 09:26-09:50.
- A refinement loop at the end uses a set of evals to feed back into specific parts of the generated documents; one is "an LLM based check for consistency between all documents … no contradictions or inaccuracies or conflicting information," which matters because the documents come from "a parallel fan out process" that generates them independently, 09:55-10:24.
- Round-trip check: because the run started from labels, the generated record can be compared back against the original task inputs and outputs "to ensure that our data is actually in sync and by default get the correct labels by construction," which means you "basically skip the … expensive ground truth[ing] process," 10:26-11:00.
- Generation stays in plain text and markdown. Rendering to PDF is possible but "we don't really see much value" because "we have state of the art PDF parsers today … available to everyone" that convert complex PDFs back to markdown, so "all of this synthetic generation [and] evaluation happens in the text domain," 11:04-11:31.
- On authority: "who am I to know what a good medical record looks like?" — "you really don't. Like no AI engineer would," so "you want your domain experts to be the ones telling you what's good," and it "is of great value to empower your domain experts to own your whole data pipeline," 11:34-12:07.
- Mechanism one is human-in-the-loop steering: "at any point, a clinician can steer the generation process to make a medical record in the way they want it," and clinicians commonly look at production cases, take ideas from them, and steer the pipeline to produce similar cases — so failure cases can be modelled "beforehand or even after … you see them in production," 12:15-12:58.
- Mechanism two is ownership of the logic: "we let our clinicians also own the whole logic of the pipeline … by modeling the whole pipeline as a skills-based workflow running on a generic agent harness that we built internally," where the patient journey, document generation, document enrichment, and the evals "are skills that run on our agent harness," 12:59-13:22.
- The concrete case: a clinician adding a new document type for a customer's intake forms "could easily just make a new skill file for it, attach it to the pipeline, and … there wouldn't be any engineering changes required," 13:29-13:46. Generalized: "skills are really an amazing interface between AI engineers and domain experts, especially in vertical AI," reused across other internal and production workflows, 13:47-14:02.
- Results: synthetic data is used "only … for evaluation at the moment"; "roughly 90% of our data sets are already made of synthetic data," supporting high production accuracy across many customer deployments, 14:09-14:29. In a blind review, clinicians "were only able to distinguish synthetic from real about 60% of the time" — he calls it "room for improvement, but … it's close," 14:29-14:45.
- The operational consequence he is most excited about: datasets "are created just in time for these customer deployments," so "you don't need to depend on … waiting for data from your customer" and can model edge cases, simulate them, and test workflows before going live, 14:53-15:18.
- Four stated takeaways: reverse your inference workflow; sample diversity "from an appropriate distribution for your use case"; "emulate the process in which data was actually generated"; and give domain experts the keys, because they "will help you drive towards a recursive self-improvement and not the AI engineers," 15:24-16:11.
- Scope beyond healthcare: "you don't need a PHI problem for this" — the approach applies whenever the data you need is "ephemeral, sensitive, or even expensive to label," 16:16-16:26.
