## Summary

Batra takes a popular three-step argument — agents will drive most action on the web, the web will therefore become agent-friendly, and it will do so by publishing APIs — grants the first two, and spends the talk killing the third. His evidence is a tour of what ordinary websites actually look like when you go looking for one fact: a restaurant menu that is text (easy), a PDF behind a button (medium), or a gallery of JPEG photographs of a printed menu that ChatGPT struggles to OCR (hard); and a US school district's purchasing page that is a portal (easy), a scanned PDF of solicitations (harder), or a Freedom of Information Act request answered by scanning your own email onto Google Drive with PDFs attached (boss level). "These are the people you're telling me will give you an MCP server." The scale argument behind it: roughly 200 million active sites out of about a billion total, run by institutions where "there are still places that are faxing each other," and where you will never get the unfettered access a coding agent would need to generate an endpoint even once that is technically easy. Reading the HTML instead does not rescue you, because much of what a page shows was never written down: an NBA box score arrives in a later asynchronous JSON fetch and is absent from the initial HTML, and a product page's "sold out" is not text at all but a `quantity` of zero in a fetched JSON object plus a rendering script that grays the option out and makes it unclickable. The generalization is that the browser is a rendering engine — "think of it as a game engine" — so asking to read the source and predict the pixels is "an exact inversion of that process," and since the web was built for human eyes, pixels are the source of truth and machines will need vision. He calls this "the bitter lesson for web agents": per-site scaffolding does not generalize to the long tail, and the general solution is the one the web was designed for. Yutori's Navigator model started (November of the prior year) as pure screenshot-in / clicks-and-scrolls-out, and the next version also writes JavaScript on demand inside the page — the browser executes code but "has a sort of formal verification system built in," namely the screenshot, so the model can check on screen whether the code did what it intended. On Mind2Web with human evaluation of trajectories, Navigator 1.5 sits at 97% (8 of 300 trajectories wrong) and he says the benchmark should be retired. Against frontier models on browser-use benchmarks, accuracy is "within statistical noise" but the smaller-footprint model wins the axes he cares about: latency per step and roughly $0.80 versus $2.30 cost per task at 20-30 steps. His closing prediction is deliberately anticlimactic — the web will not be reinvented; hundreds of browsers will pretend to be humans behind a task endpoint that returns structured output for less than a penny, "and at some point you will say, yeah, that's an API."

## Notes

### The argument, and the step that is wrong (00:12-01:40)

- The claim he is arguing against, in three steps: "AI agents will be the drivers of actions on the web, not humans… more agents clicking buttons, reading things, buying things for us than human eyeballs"; because "today the web is extremely hostile to automated traffic," the web will be *agentified*; and that will happen "with APIs that agents will call via some set of protocols and the set of protocols grows over time." (00:12-01:12)
- The protocol proliferation is named as a symptom: "it was initially supposed to be MCP servers, then WebMCP, and for payments there are 20 different competing protocols… every single company wants to introduce their own protocol." (00:57-01:12)
- He accepts the first two steps and rejects the third: "this last bit that suddenly the web will provide you APIs for accessing things is just delusional… computer-use agents and computer-use models will agentify the web, not APIs, and more specifically the long tail of the web. The head of the distribution, the most popular websites perhaps, will give you the API, but the long tail will not." (01:12-01:47)

### The flight-booking example is the wrong example (01:47-02:35)

- He volunteers the strongest objection to his own field first, using his own example rather than someone else's: a demo that finds business-class flights by clicking buttons on `flights.google.com` "is bizarre. Why would you possibly do it this way? Aren't you aware that there are already aggregators? You send them a variable, they will send you a JSON. Your LLMs can do tool calling." (01:55-02:26)
- The point of the button-clicking, where an API exists, "is to generate this behind the scenes" — i.e. to produce the structured result, not to be the production path. (02:26-02:35)

### Restaurant menus on easy, medium, and hard mode (02:35-05:27)

- The imagined endpoint: a restaurant menu API "that accepts a query… where I can ask for give me your menu items that are gluten-free. I want to rid you of the delusion that such an endpoint exists." (02:35-03:14)
- Easy mode: a page with text and prices, in German. "No API, but at least it's text… models can speak languages. Maybe this is easily scrapable." (03:23-03:42)
- Medium mode: the menu button leads to a PDF. "If your agent has to do this, it needs a PDF reader mode. Fine… I will dump this into ChatGPT and it'll do it." (03:42-04:14)
- Hard mode: a page of photographs of the owners and the location, with the menu reachable only through a Spanish phrase he reads aloud and translates as "our menu," leading to "a gallery of individual… JPEGs embedded in a gallery which contain the [printed] menu items. You download this and you put it into ChatGPT and it's struggling doing OCR on this thing." (04:14-05:14)

### School district procurement, up to a FOIA request (05:27-07:38)

- The enterprise version of the same question: "there's about 15 to 20,000 school districts in the US," and a vendor wants to ask whether a given district is procuring laptops right now — "I want to say RFP status open and topic laptop, and you think this will exist." (05:28-06:01)
- Easy mode is a district portal. Harder mode is a menu path (finance → purchasing → solicitations) ending in a scanned PDF with no text layer: "this is how they tell you about what they have purchased." (06:01-06:47)
- Boss level: "in order to find information, you have to file a request for access of information under Freedom of Information Act. And then… they scan your email that you sent to them, put it on a Google Drive, and then attach PDFs associated with your request. These are the people you're telling me will give you an MCP server." (06:47-07:25)

### 200 million active sites that change slowly (07:38-08:33)

- Scale: "the active websites are somewhere [around] 200 million. The total number of websites are sitting in a billion. Infrastructure changes very slowly." (07:38-08:05)
- The access argument, which is separate from the capability argument: even granting that a coding agent could generate the endpoint — "even if that technological problem is solved, which I agree seems like on a horizon it should eventually be solved" — "you're not going to get unfettered access to these institutions, and these institutions change very slowly. There are still places that are faxing each other." (08:05-08:33)

### Why reading the HTML does not rescue it (08:33-10:14)

- The fallback he expects the audience to reach for: "there's not going to be APIs available, but I have coding agents. Why don't I just throw them at the HTML? There is, after all, if the browser is doing it, it's a piece of code." He grants it is a fair point and says he thought the same two years earlier. (08:33-08:56)
- The counterexample: asking for the final score of a Timberwolves-Nets game on `nba.com`. "You and I can see the score 125 versus 109… behind the scenes, when you actually load the page and read it… there's an empty placeholder initially, and you wait a few hundred milliseconds to a few seconds depending on your network connection, and your browser makes an asynchronous call later to fetch the information… If you just read the HTML when you load it, the answer is not in the HTML, and so your chatbot doesn't have access to that either." (09:01-10:00)
- The obvious patch, and its limit: "you go fine, these are just details, I just have to add some waits and sleeps." (10:00-10:14)

### "Sold out" is not text, it is a rendered zero (10:14-11:33)

- The second example: is the 25mm osmium cube in stock? A dropdown shows three variants grayed out and one selectable, "even though it doesn't actually say in stock — like there's no text there that says in stock, but you understand that grayed out means sold out. And sometimes the sold out won't actually be there as a text. Sometimes it'll just be unclickable, grayed out." (10:14-10:38)
- The HTML does not contain the answer either: "there is an option selector. It actually doesn't say any of the things that I'm seeing on screen. It doesn't say sold out. It doesn't say available." (10:38-10:51)
- The mechanism: "behind the scenes the browser makes a call, gets a JSON object which is the variable which is product count, that contains a variable called quantity. That quantity is 10 sometimes, zero sometimes… and there's a different rendering script that anytime there's zero grays it out and makes it unclickable." (10:51-11:21)
- The generalization: "this information that you are seeing on screen is not written somewhere as pure text. It is calculated. It is rendered." (11:21-11:33)

### The browser is a rendering engine; pixels are the truth (11:33-12:53)

- "For people who work in the browser industry, they understand this. But… people who are coming from an AI background like me, we didn't always understand this. The browser is a rendering engine. You are seeing pixels on screen. Think of it as a game engine." (11:33-11:52)
- The inversion argument: "you're asking, can I not read the source code of the game and predict exactly what the pixels are going to be? Well, yes, eventually. But right now you're asking for an exact inversion of that process." (11:52-12:08)
- "Fundamentally the web was built for human eyes. Pixels are the source of the truth because the consumers of the websites are humans… machines will need vision to operate those things." (12:08-12:24)
- The bitter-lesson framing: "in a way this is the bitter lesson for web agents — the more you end up writing scaffolds around existing websites, it doesn't actually generalize to the long tail of the web. The thing that generalizes is the thing that it was designed for, which is the most general solution: just pixels in." (12:24-12:53)

### Navigator: screenshot in, clicks and code out (12:53-15:51)

- Navigator's first version shipped "in November last year" and "acted purely like a human: screenshot in, button clicks and scrolls out. That is not where you should settle on, but it is a general solution." (12:53-13:10)
- The task that shows why the general surface matters: given an e-commerce site and a discount code with constraints described in natural language (specific product, date window, minimum cart threshold), decide whether the code is valid. "There is no API for this. The way to do it is just like a human can: you go to that website, you find the product that is described, you add it to cart, you apply the discount code, and you check whether the claim of 22% off was met or not." The trajectory runs "20, 30, 40 steps depending on the sophistication of the task." (13:10-14:03)
- The capability claim is deliberately bounded: "if you can do it on your browser, this model can accomplish it in principle. In practice, of course, there are accuracy gaps… whereas in a lot of earlier cases, even in principle that task may not be solvable." (14:03-14:16)
- Vision is a floor, not a ceiling: "just because for the long tail you need to have a capability does not mean that is the only way you should do it." The next model version writes JavaScript on demand — a Chrome extension screenshot shows an action `execute js` filling multiple form fields at once "because it wrote a little bit of a function. It can read the code when necessary. It can write code when necessary." (14:16-14:57)
- Why the browser is a good place to run agent-written code: "the browser after all is an engine that can execute code, but it has a sort of formal verification system built in. It is seeing the screenshot — that is the source of the truth. So it knows whether it succeeded or not." The rule he states: "click buttons when you have to, write code when you have to, and look at the result through pixels because that is the source of truth." (14:57-15:23)
- Parallelism as the payoff of being a machine: "you can have an orchestrator that is launching multiple navigators in parallel, each with a cloud sandbox instance… so you can accomplish things that would be superhuman because no human would be able to parallelize over that many instances." (15:23-15:51)

### Are computer-use models actually good enough (15:51-17:20)

- He names the counter-perception: "there's actually a perception online that it's not clear whether progress on computer use has been fast, and there are questions about why has progress been slow. That's not the reality I'm seeing and that's not the reality that the numbers back up." (15:51-16:05)
- The measurement: Mind2Web, plotted as release date against performance, where performance is *human* evaluation — "a human went in, looked at the trajectory, decided whether it was correct or not." Navigator 1.5 "is sitting at 97%. Eight trajectories out of 300 are incorrect at this point of time. You should just retire the benchmark, build something harder." Tasks involve "about 30 to 50 steps of interaction." He volunteers that "no benchmark is perfect. The point isn't that this is the right solution." (16:05-16:56)

### Latency and cost per task (17:20-18:28)

- The next objection, which he partly concedes: "aren't these things slow, because after all you're looking at a screen, you're clicking a button, there are lots of buttons to click, and these things are expensive if you're running them for hundreds of things. That claim I think is largely true. There is some truth to it. But I think people forget how much you can optimize these things out." (17:13-17:34)
- The comparison, against Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.5 on a couple of browser-use benchmarks: "we're slightly better, but I think that's within statistical noise in terms of accuracy. That improvement I wouldn't beat the drum on. What I would emphasize is latency per step and cost per task." (17:34-17:57)
- The mechanism and the number: "this is a smaller footprint model. That's why it's a lot faster than some of the trillion-parameter-plus models. And there are corresponding cost savings. So if you have… something like 20, 30 steps of interaction, you're looking at 80 cents per task versus $2.30. And that makes a big difference. And so the models are getting cheaper, and you can launch them at scale." (17:57-18:24)

### Another layer of mess that we will call an API (18:28-20:35)

- What he considers uncontestable: agents as the primary drivers of action, "because the underlying intelligence of the models is becoming larger and larger… there is a certain gain of productivity and efficiency." (18:28-18:55)
- What he considers fantasy: "this hypothesis that suddenly overnight 30 years of infrastructure that was built layer upon layer for human consumption will in — what, two, five, 10 years — be reinvented." (18:55-19:12)
- What people actually want is a task endpoint, not an API: "ultimately you want an endpoint that some higher-level entity can go to and say I want you to do X. There is some task — maybe I give you that task description in natural language, maybe I have some programmatic description with parameters — and there's some endpoint, I should be able to do it." (19:12-19:38)
- The prediction: "just extrapolate this trend. Accuracies are getting higher, benchmarks are falling, latencies are getting smaller, costs are falling… we will just pile on another layer of mess on top of the mess that the web is, which is to say you will issue a task, behind the scenes there will be hundreds of browsers that are pretending and clicking buttons just like humans would, and they will give you a result in a structured format. It will cost less than a penny. It will run in your browser. Sometimes it will run in less than 100 milliseconds. And at some point you will say, yeah, that's an API. Like, why do I care?" (19:38-20:33)
