## Summary

Applied Compute frames enterprise reinforcement learning as a way to specialize models for private business benchmarks, then focuses on the systems problem of making those RL runs fast, cheap, and predictably timed. The talk contrasts synchronous RL, where long-tail samples leave GPUs idle, with asynchronous pipeline RL, where sampling and training workers run concurrently but must control policy staleness; it then shows how response-length distributions, forward-pass latency curves, training throughput, KV-cache limits, and GPU allocation simulations can choose efficient layouts before expensive runs are launched.

## Notes

- Applied Compute describes its enterprise goal as moving AI beyond productivity into quantitative ROI, then deploying specialized systems with a data flywheel so they improve through use. (00:43-01:13)
- RL is presented as a mechanism for making out-of-distribution enterprise tasks become in-distribution for current models, analogous to how lab RL optimizes public benchmarks but aimed at private benchmarks. (01:16-01:43)
- The simplified RL loop samples many reasoning trajectories per problem, grades the answers, reinforces correct traces, and discourages incorrect behavior. (01:44-02:53)
- Enterprise RL runs need to be fast, cheap, and low variance in duration because customer delivery and unit economics depend on reliable turnaround, not only average speed. (02:56-04:03)
- In synchronous RL, sampling and training happen in lock step, so step time is dictated by the slowest sample; in their arithmetic experiment, 99% of samples finished around 40 seconds while the final 1% took another roughly 80 seconds. (04:06-05:23)
- Pipeline RL dedicates some GPUs to sampling and some to training, keeps sampling workers running, queues completed samples for trainers, and propagates in-flight weight updates back to samplers. (05:27-06:24)
- In-flight updates mean one generated sample may contain tokens produced by multiple policy versions, creating stale tokens; more tolerated staleness improves utilization but raises importance-ratio variance and can destabilize learning. (06:27-08:37)
- Their layout model uses GPU budget, training batch size, sampling latency per GPU, response-length distribution, KV-cache limits, and per-GPU training throughput to balance production and consumption rates under a maximum staleness constraint. (09:00-18:58)
- Sweeping candidate layouts lets the team estimate efficient GPU allocation before expensive runs; their simulated asynchronous layout yielded roughly a 60% speedup over a synchronous baseline under the stated assumptions. (18:58-19:57)
