## Summary

Sangwu Lee describes how Krea trained its Krea 2 image foundation model, whose medium variant they open-sourced, from a research rather than infrastructure perspective. The talk's thesis is that once the architecture is locked — a latent diffusion transformer, in their case — data is what determines model quality, and most of the work is data curation. The specific curation techniques are the durable content: a captioning pipeline that runs OCR before a vision-language model, filtering out images whose important properties the captioner reliably fails to mention, refusing AI-generated images entirely because their aesthetic is sticky, ordering deduplication and classification by cost per sample so a billion-image sweep is affordable, repurposing sparse autoencoders as an unsupervised tagger for watermarks and border artifacts, and checking world-knowledge coverage against PageRank-ranked Wikipedia concepts — around 30-40 in-house filters in total. The product framing behind all of it is a deliberate position against the big production models: those trade stylistic diversity for consistency ("mode collapse"), and Krea trades the other way for creative users who do not yet know what they want. The training pipeline itself is borrowed wholesale from LLM practice (resolution curriculum, mid-training, SFT, preference optimization, GRPO-style RL with reward servers, plus a trained prompt expander).

## Notes

- Lee introduces himself as working on research for Krea 2; a colleague was scheduled to cover training infrastructure separately, so this talk is deliberately data- and method-oriented rather than systems-oriented (00:13-00:45). The medium variant of the otherwise closed model was open-sourced, and stylistic diversity is named as the focus (00:56-01:13).
- The positioning argument: production-grade models from the big labs — captioned as "Chat GPT-2" (i.e. OpenAI's then-current production image model, not the 2019 language model) and "Nano Banana Pro" — optimize for slow but reliable generation, taking "up to like a minute or like two" to return something with "hardly barely any flaws." The price is that "in order to get like good consistency, they have like kind of like significantly mode collapse their models" (01:47-02:24).
- The mechanism, stated as a rule of thumb: "if you're trying to render a person, the easiest and most reliable way to like render a person is render the most boring average person that exists and then like put it in a center frame." His standing example is the prompt "burning skull," where outputs are all fine and nearly identical (02:24-02:48).
- Krea's opposite bet is fast generation plus knobs, aimed at a user who does not have a fixed target: for "a poster or like a birthday card" the production models are "excellent solution," but "sometimes like when you're like a creative studio, you don't quite know what you want yet and want to like slightly explore what kind of like visuals you want to make" (02:48-03:23).
- Diffusion recap and architecture: add noise to an image, train the model to remove it, then denoise from random noise at generation time (03:27-04:10). Production diffusion models, "at least the open-source ones that are very competitive, including ours," are latent diffusion — an autoencoder spatially compresses before generation and decompresses after — because diffusion transformers are quadratic in sequence length, so "if you try to like model every single pixel, that's very expensive" (04:12-05:16).
- The thesis, repeated deliberately: "really like data is like quite everything that goes into the model. Like typically, you lock in your architecture and then… a lot of work just goes into like just feeding the model like what it wants… I mean, this sounds stupid, but I cannot iterate this more" (05:16-05:45).
- Because the goal is stylistic diversity, standard quality filtering is itself a hazard: "some people like think… low-resolution CRT videos are like a bad image, but some people like that kind of like aesthetics, so making sure that we have like good coverage and don't just rely on like very standard like aesthetic scores or like image quality scores to… oversample… what are conventionally considered like good images" (05:57-06:31).
- Their bad-data taxonomy: duplicates and overrepresented concepts (handled by deduplication and clustering-based rebalancing); images whose captions the VLM captioner consistently gets wrong, "which leads to certain biases"; and images inappropriate for the current training resolution, such as "20[-]100 like characters on a 256 by 256 pixels" image, "too hard for the model to like learn at least at like low resolution stages" (06:38-07:30).
- On AI-generated images: "we tried very hard to like remove any AI images like at all because it does like provide you a shortcut to… get you like a good model, but synthetic data is like so sticky to the model that once you like start training on AI image data, sure your model's good, but you kind of lose the point because then you're going to get like very like ChatGPT or like Nano [Banana] aesthetic." He adds that he can personally tell when a model has been distilled on those models, and "as a researcher it always slightly hurts my ego if all I'm doing is distillation" (07:30-08:15).
- Captioning pipeline, in order: OCR first, "because text rendering is quite important… we make sure that we first extract out all the text that are like visible in the image"; then optional metadata (for example, that the subject is a famous person); then a second pass with a vision-language model for a detailed caption; then rewriting that caption into "whatever like JSON prompts or like other formats you would like to… feed the model" (08:17-09:03).
- The bad-data example he shows is a painting photographed hanging on a white wall. "It doesn't look that bad," but the captioner would say "this is a painting of blah blah blah… but it would not mention the fact that it's framed on a wall… and have a white background." The downstream effect: "when you try to generate a painting of whatever, it'll be always hanged on a wall, on a white wall," which is not what the user asked for. The remedy is to design a filter and throw that class of image out, "or at least undersample it" (09:03-10:00).
- Deduplication is cost-ordered because "we need to use like… anywhere from like 2 to like 10 billion images. That's a lot of images to run like filters on." First pass is pHash or MD5 for exact and near-exact duplicates; "once we get to like a smaller like size, that's when we bring in… embedding-based deduplication method[s], SSCD, like SigLip, to do… semantic deduplication or… remove near duplicates" (10:01-10:37).
- Filter construction follows the same economics. Prompt a large vision-language model with the judgment you want ("does this look like a AI image or not"), then "distill this… decision… to like a very small like SigLip classifier. And then… you have like a very like cheap classifier that is somewhat like reliable… typically if you run a classifier over like a billion images, you do need things to be like SigLip-sized" (10:37-11:24).
- He cites the LLM-side precedent explicitly: Essential Web used "a big LLM to like come up with like some kind of taxonomy or like classifiers to like judge whether this text data is like good or not… and then distill that down to like… 500… million parameter model so that you can actually run this over like a pre-training level corpus. Otherwise, it'll be somewhat expensive and frankly inefficient use of your GPUs" (11:24-11:53).
- Sparse autoencoders, from his own prior SAE research on CLIP-style vision models, are used as "a unsupervised tagging system": feed an image to the SAE and it activates sparse features such as "horse, black and white, blur[ry]… image." Those features become off-the-shelf filter or oversampling criteria — his named targets are "signatures or like watermarks or like some kind of like border artifacts" (11:55-13:18).
- World-knowledge coverage is checked with a technique from "the original clip paper": compute PageRank over every Wikipedia article/concept; concepts in the top percentile of PageRank are "probably something quite important for the model to know"; take those keywords and "run like… standard like plain text search or like embedding search just to make sure that like these kind of concepts are in our dataset" (13:19-14:11). He is candid that "frankly, I don't know how much this has helped, but this is one of the things we did" (13:26-13:31), and the exploration surfaced a Wikipedia article for "Barack Obama the horse" that he does not believe made it into the dataset (14:11-14:34).
- Totals: "around like 30 to 40… custom in-house classifier[s], like different heuristics and like filters," and the closing repeat of the thesis — you already know you are training a transformer, so "data is like what really determines the quality of your model" (14:34-15:05).
- The training pipeline is described as LLM-inspired (captioned "Barry LM inspired"): low- to high-resolution pre-training, mid-training, supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, reinforcement learning, plus a separately trained prompt expander (15:06-15:31).
- Resolution curriculum rationale: low resolution is "where the model actually learns like text-to-image capabilities… it needs to know like how a horse looks like," then scaling resolution up teaches "structure, detail, like these kind of things that can be learned at high resolution later." Krea 2 trained from 256 to 1K resolution (15:31-16:02).
- Mid-training and SFT are explicitly the diffusion analogue of instruction tuning: a pre-trained diffusion model is "just basically auto complete" the way a pre-trained LLM is, so Krea curates "illustration, graphic design, photography, cinematics… kind of like data you want you have in mind for your downstream use case" at mid-train and SFT scale "to kind of mold your distribution" (16:02-16:40).
- Preference optimization uses collected A/B preference pairs, the same shape as ChatGPT's "do you like this over this," and is "where we get a little bit more opinionated about… the kind of model we want to train" (16:40-17:06). RL is "a GRPO inspired method where… model[s] generate images and then we send it to the reward servers," used to teach "how to like improve text rendering… have like better anatomy structure" (17:06-17:35).
- The prompt expander is described as "almost essential… for production grade diffusion models": a small LLM that takes the user prompt and outputs a long detailed prompt, "because typically longer detail prompt[s]… are more in distribution with your model's… training data[,] that tends to like make better images" (17:35-17:54).
- Current work: "multi-expert on[-policy] distillation" — train experts specialized in capabilities such as photography and text rendering, then "merge all of these capabilities into a single student" that matches each expert where the expert is strong (17:54-18:28).
- What actually mattered for iteration speed, in his own ranking: infrastructure to iterate fast; data, because "methods can change every time[,] code is something you can change very easily but… data is like eternal… that's going to be valuable no matter what the hot new training paradigm is"; simplicity and scalability, preferring "methods that have like low number of hyper parameters that we need to tune"; efficiency; and stealing from LLM research "so that I can just reuse their kernels and… research and… literature" (18:30-19:15).
- Closing observation on architecture drift: the field started with encoder-decoder transformers and is inverting — "we have a prompt expander which is a[n] autoregressive decoder and then diffusion which is the encoder" — which he notes "starting to look a little bit like DALL-E 2," where a model generates conditioning that is then fed to a diffusion model (19:16-19:53).
- Directions he is interested in: simplifying the stack to "get rid of VAEs and then text encoders and then just train a single clean transformer" (19:55-20:11); and, because vision-language models are far better than at the time of the original latent-diffusion paper, richer textual conditioning — bounding boxes ("before it was expensive to generate, but now you can perfectly generate good bounding box for every image and you can condition the image model on this," which he attributes to work at Ideogram) and scene-graph-to-image generation (traced to Fei-Fei Li's lab, 2017). He frames image generation as "really like a proxy for… VLM… progress" (20:11-21:05).
- Caption artifacts worth recording: "Create 2" throughout is Krea 2; "Nano banner" is Nano Banana Pro; "Barry LM inspired" is an LLM-inspired pipeline; "masses" is methods; "BLM" is VLM; "all the regressive" is autoregressive; "on positive distillation" is on-policy distillation; the speaker's name is captioned "Sangwha."
