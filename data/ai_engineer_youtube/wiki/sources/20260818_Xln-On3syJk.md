## Summary

Keegan McCallum, founder of uRun ("a new kind of inference provider focused around interactive media"), argues that the interesting axis in generative video stopped being quality. Quality has an obvious lineage (Will Smith eating spaghetti in 2023, Sora in 2024, Sora 2, Seedance this year), but the axis he cares about is efficiency and long-horizon generation, where at least 40 models with real-time or long-horizon capability shipped this year. The number he anchors on is economic rather than perceptual: real-time output now lands at roughly *last year's* frontier quality, so $10 buys about three hours of continuously generated video and $50 buys about fifteen — set against a room full of raised hands for burning that much on coding tokens inside a single hour. Helios, the model uRun serves, is a distill of an open 14-billion-parameter video model; put a real-time clip next to one that took minutes and the slow one still has better motion, at roughly a hundred times the cost. What the cheap axis unlocks is interaction shape rather than better clips: a webcam magic mirror that shows you a haircut you are considering, a visual medium for people who do not think in text, and content creation that stops being a slot machine of prompts and keyframes at ~$10 a minute — you steer in under a second while the model is generating, then render the shot you found at full fidelity. The last two minutes are the engineering thesis: "the models are here and the frontier is really in how we serve them," meaning globally placed GPUs and user-to-GPU routing, WebRTC with ICE and TURN, several models wired into one continuous streaming pipeline, and frame-by-frame synchronization with user controls. uRun packages that as a drop-in React component over a programmable Python runtime, plus a CLI and MCP server because "in 2026 we don't just need platforms, we need software factories and ways for agents to interact with these."
