## Summary

Imad Touil, a distinguished engineer at QuantumBlack, opens with three show-of-hands questions whose answers collapse in sequence: who has built a skill (most of the room), who shares them with their team (fewer), who governs and maintains them across the organization (a handful). The talk is an argument for closing that third gap. His route to it is an elimination: a workflow contains four things, and of those, hooks only "trigger on events," MCP servers are overwhelmingly consumed rather than authored ("tell me like who actually build a lot of MCPs? We just use MCP tools that is actually provided by the tool that we used to use before… so we don't really own"), and sub agents exist "just to minimize the context window." What is left is skills, and so "at the end of the day you will find all of your know-how is actually at the skills level. And if you don't have the right structure of your skills, then you're not really having a deterministic workflow" (05:53-06:52).

The design prescription is borrowed wholesale from the microservices era: skills should be reusable, modular, discoverable, portable across both workflows and harnesses ("if I'm having a skill on Claude Code and I want to move it to Cursor, it's going to just work"), specialized rather than "one skill like a monolith," composable so they do not duplicate and conflict at run time, consistent and deterministic, and cost-efficient — the last through progressive disclosure, "the right skills, the right amount of skills in the right time to solve the right problem," which is what reduces token usage. The unit this yields is know-how that is "executable, portable and cheap" (08:25-09:57). The worked example is regulation: a data-retention-policy skill composed with disclosure standards, GDPR rules, and fill-in templates, pulled automatically at run time by a regulatory disclosure review workflow, producing a deterministic outcome plus a stored audit report plus identified improvements that loop back into the codebase (09:57-11:20).

The warning is that ungoverned skills are a recognizable new class of technical debt. Teams on the same stack build the same skills repeatedly without sharing them. Quality decays unless skills are validated "not against your task but also against the latest models that comes." Nobody knows who owns one, and "if you don't have an owner then no one will be able to maintain, scale those skills." Composability "is not something that comes by default" — it needs a governance forum and a domain-driven carve, the same way service boundaries did. And there is a security edge, because "skills actually does have scripts because that is the deterministic part of it," so pulling a public skill without a checking pipeline is a supply-chain decision, alongside access control for skills that carry sensitive business logic (11:21-13:13). The fix is a three-rung adoption ladder — individual, team, then a centralized platform — where the platform owes a searchable catalog with metadata, an MCP plugged into that catalog and a CLI to pull skills into an IDE or sandbox, inter-skill dependencies, versioning and lifecycle so a harness pulls the current release, access control, and evaluation and observability (13:13-14:50).

He is blunt about where the tooling argument ends: "all of this is actually play around a governance, and this is where technology stop solving the problem… now who's going to govern this?" — answered with named humans, architects and engineering leads and infra leads and cyber leads owning domains (14:47-15:17). A synthetic simulation of fifteen teams over six months illustrates the ungoverned case, and its most portable observation is the shape of the cost: a missing skill does not appear as a gap, it appears as spend, because without a regulation skill "someone is vibe coding back and forth and trying to figure out exactly how to steer the agent to implement it properly… that is burning more tokens from one side cost-wise, but also the productivity is spending more time rather than giving in one shot the right answer" (15:32-17:15). He closes forward: the same treatment should extend from skills to whole workflows; skills registries are arriving from the internal-developer-portal vendors; skill evaluation is still an open question with a cheap interim answer (statically check the skill against Anthropic's published best practices, because "if the skill is not invoked properly, if the skill is not structured properly, there's a high chance that it's not going to be high quality"); and auto-evolving skills, "the next hype," change nothing about the argument — "if you automatically start this machine, the impact will be way more than it is today, because what I shared earlier is going to be just maintaining auto-evolving skills without that governance in place that actually put the guardrails" (17:53-20:03).

## Provenance and Limits

- **Nothing in this talk is measured.** There is no deployment, no A/B test, no before-and-after from a real organization, and no named client. Every claim is either a design principle asserted from experience or an output of a simulation the speaker wrote himself.
- **The fifteen-team simulation is synthetic, and it is the only quantitative-looking artifact in the talk.** He says so plainly — "what I created, I created a simulation… I have um just a random teams. I have 15 teams created, um five to 12 like per team" — and the metrics (skills per engineer contribution, average skills utilization as pulls per day, duplication ratio across teams, quality and security ratio) are parameters he assigned. Running it "across six months" produces the divergence between governed and ungoverned that the talk predicts; it does not test it. The governed side is also hedged in-talk: "this is reality, isn't going to be perfect um as we expect."
- **The skills-bench comparison is relayed from a slide with no numbers.** The claim is that latest models running the same task with skills produced an outcome "clearly higher" than without. The benchmark name is auto-captioned and one of the two named domains is not recoverable (see Caption Artifacts). No figures, no model names, no task list, no error bars.
- **The recommendation is also the speaker's business.** QuantumBlack is McKinsey's AI arm, and the prescribed end state — a centralized platform plus a standing governance body of architects, infrastructure leads, and security leads carving domains — is an organizational transformation of exactly the kind that firm sells. This does not make the argument wrong; the microservices analogy it rests on is a real historical precedent. It does mean the talk's central move (technology stops solving it, so you need governance) resolves toward consulting work, and should be read with that in mind.
- **The adoption timeline is stated relative to the talk date and is approximate.** "Just eight months ago Anthropic published the first article about skills… two month later we had this standard, an open standard that is adopted, and a lot of agent harnesses starts adopting this new standard… around February this year we have seen most of the actually agents adopted this." From a 2026-08-28 talk that places the first article around December 2025 and broad harness adoption around February 2026. The skill-count "snapshot that I did across some public GitHub repos, some public skills registries" is shown as a chart with no method or absolute numbers given in speech.
- **The portability claim is asserted, not demonstrated.** "If I'm having a skill on Claude Code and I want to move it to Cursor, it's going to just work" is offered as a property of the open standard. No cross-harness test is shown, and the talk does not discuss what breaks when a skill's scripts assume a particular sandbox, tool set, or permission model.
- **The four-component elimination is a claim about typical practice, not a survey.** "Who actually build a lot of MCPs? We just use MCP tools" is an inference from a room's reaction, and it is contingent — an organization that does author its own MCP servers has a second place for know-how to live, which would weaken the argument that skills are the residual.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Skills Are the Residual Where Organizational Know-How Lands](../concepts/skills-are-the-residual-where-organizational-know-how-lands.md) - the elimination across the four workflow components — hooks fire on events, MCP servers are consumed not authored, sub agents manage the context window — that leaves skills as the only place org-specific knowledge can accumulate.
- [A Missing Skill Is Billed as Tokens, Not Recorded as a Gap](../concepts/a-missing-skill-is-billed-as-tokens-not-recorded-as-a-gap.md) - an unwritten skill has no defect report; it shows up as steering iterations and token spend distributed across engineers, which makes cost-per-outcome the leading indicator of a library gap.
- [Skill Composability Is Decided Before Authoring, Not in the Registry](../concepts/skill-composability-is-decided-before-authoring-not-in-the-registry.md) - a catalog can make skills findable but cannot make independently written skills compose, so the domain carve and the named human owners are prerequisites rather than later cleanup.
- [Auto-Evolving Skills Multiply Whatever Governance You Already Have](../concepts/auto-evolving-skills-multiply-whatever-governance-you-already-have.md) - a self-improving skill loop writes into the same library, so it accelerates whichever regime is in place; the governance question is upstream of the loop, not solved by it.
