## Summary

Johan Lajili (Poolside AI, a company training its own foundation model and coding agents) explains the split between people who say AI writes all their code and people who say it produces "absolute garbage" not as greenfield-vs-brownfield but as a **feedback-loop** difference: on greenfield the agent's intuition is correct, while on brownfield ("there be dragons") it hits dead ends, unused code, and parts it never looked at — and the differentiator is whether the agent can *see* what it did. A blindfolded agent's "it's all working perfectly" really means "to the best of what you gave me, that sounds like it should work," which is why skeptics see a liar and enthusiasts iterate. His fix is to give the agent eyes with a bespoke internal CLI ("Spoolside") that lets the agent test Poolside's product — which is a VS Code extension, not a web page, so generic screenshot/snapshot tooling needs an extra step: take screenshots, produce a token-compressed "snapshot" of the UI, extract logs from front-end and back-end services, restart services, and issue stackable high-level commands (open a menu, navigate a page, send a message to the agent, wait for the reply, upload an image). The payoff is trust: when the agent can reproduce a bug before fixing it, you can stop verifying by hand and start running it overnight and multiplying agents; "until it reproduces the bug, I don't trust you." He generalizes this to a role shift — the engineer's new job is to make the AI able to self-serve on the product (build tools, improve the codebase, improve knowledge bases; ship it as a CLI, a skill, or an MCP, varying by product and problem), and we all become "AIX engineers." The oxygen-mask analogy anchors the investment argument: put the mask on the AI first (make it self-served) before you build features, because even though it slows you now it pays off the moment you multiply agents and run them over time — velocity without verification just compounds errors. Q&A adds that he prefers ephemeral human-like testing over brittle automated tests, finds tools by refusing to hand-correct the AI (make it realize the problem itself), runs a retrospective loop asking the AI to review its own logs for "stinks" (e.g. `sleep(15)` everywhere signals a missing wait-for), and tailors the representation to the product (an ASCII view of a Unity 3D world; easy logins for a multi-permission app).

## Notes

- Greenfield vs brownfield is really the feedback loop: on greenfield "the agent's intuition is correct… it has very good intuition," while on brownfield "there be dragons" — dead ends, unused code, "things it is not aware of in different parts of the code that it hasn't even looked at" — and "the big difference between those two is the feedback loop." 01:58-02:46
- Brownfield success is achievable, so greenfield/brownfield is not the real divide: "we've seen people using AI in legacy applications with good success. I have myself." 00:42-00:51
- A blindfolded agent's claim is hollow: "I've implemented the new auth flow and it's all working perfectly" really means "to the best of what you have given me, that sounds like it should work… Maybe the agent was able to verify its work. Maybe it wasn't." 02:46-03:12
- The user split is a verification gap, not a personality: enthusiasts see it's not working and say "agent, can you try again? Check those logs," while skeptics "just give up." 03:26-03:42
- The fix is a bespoke tool that gives the agent eyes — an internal CLI ("Spoolside", "not something you're going to find on GitHub") that lets the AI test the product; because "our application is not a web page, it's an extension within VS code," generic screenshot/snapshot tooling "takes an extra step to get there." 03:45-04:23
- Capabilities exposed: take screenshots, take a "very token-compressed version of what's going on on a web page" (a snapshot), extract logs from front-end and back-end services, restart services, and stackable high-level commands — "can you access a specific menu, can you go to this page, can you send a message to the agent, wait for it to reply, send another message, upload an image." 04:00-04:53
- Reproduction is the trust gate: "if it's working on a bug, it can actually reproduce the bug before it starts working on that. The agents are very eager… 'you just need to add this code.' But until it reproduces the bug, I don't trust you." 04:57-05:22
- Trust unlocks autonomy at scale: without it "I'm going to have to go and verify it myself and I'm wasting time and I cannot then take that agent and start running it overnight… This is a first step to trust." 05:29-05:45
- Build your own, in whatever form fits: "the point of this is not Spoolside… It's to build your own… You can implement this as a CLI, as a skill, as an MCP. In my case it's a CLI cuz I like things simple… it's going to be different from people to people and problem to problem." 05:43-06:23
- The role shift: "we're going to have to focus less on the product and more on trying to make the AI work on the product… That can be those tools, improving the code base so it's easier to work on, improving knowledge bases… we're all going to become AIX engineers." 05:51-07:02
- The oxygen-mask investment argument: "you put your mask on yourself before you put your mask on your children. It's the same with AI — put the mask on the AI, make sure it's self-served before you try to work on features. Even if it slows you down right now, it's an investment that pays off as soon as you start multiplying agents and running things over time." 07:06-07:29
- Velocity is a trap without verification: focus on "making sure that velocity is not a trap, that you're not going to multiply errors or compound errors." 06:38-06:50
- Q&A — prefers ephemeral human-like testing: automated tests "can be sometimes a bit too rigid and hard to predict and hard to work over time," so he likes "something that mimics the way I would test it, like a human would go and test the application." 07:58-08:26
- Q&A — how he finds the tools: rather than telling the AI "the button is a bit to the left," "what I want is the AI to realize that by itself," so on each problem he takes a step back and thinks about how to make the AI realize it. 08:30-08:50
- Q&A — retrospective log-review loop: after doing something many times, "look back over your logs, ask an AI, did you notice any issues, any stink?" e.g. "calling sleep(15) everywhere is a stink" that should be a wait-for; also anything you keep doing over and over (running storybooks). 08:50-09:17
- Q&A — tailor the representation to the product: for "a game in Unity, do you want an ASCII representation of your 3D world for your AI?"; for something with many permissions and logins, make it easy for the AI to take those. 09:17-09:38
