## Summary

A twenty-minute talk from the lead of Ramp's product and sales-led-growth engineering teams, and the wiki's sixth go-to-market source. It covers much of the same ground as the Notion and Clay accounts — an internal customer data platform, durable execution on Temporal, embedded unstructured data, per-account background agents — but the organizing question is different. Where Liu asks what the system must *know, decide, act on, and learn*, and Berry asks what must be *true of the data* before any of it works, Vaziri asks why a good idea takes months to reach the field, and answers that the missing layer is distribution: getting one described intent to land simultaneously in outbound, ads, web, and in-app, without a human persuading each channel owner to adopt a playbook.

**The goal, stated as an interface.** "What we're building towards is the ability to just describe a motion" — playbooks, experiments, evergreen campaigns — "and how those get distributed across the channels through which you actually execute your go-to-market, whether it's outbound or ads or web or whatever… we want the ability to kind of describe this and automate that output" (00:29-00:59). The running example is deliberately small: "I'm a golfer. Suppose I want to offer golfers at East Coast construction companies an incentive to try Ramp" — spin up the audience, spin up the incentive ("some Pro V1 golf balls"), "create outbound sequences, generate the copy, generate creative for paid ads and for web, maybe show some in-app notifications for your customers, and do all of that seamlessly by just describing the intent, right? And probably more than just this one sentence" (02:00-02:39). Late in the talk he notes in passing that the play "actually like… works really well" (16:02-16:10).

**The diagnosis: the bottleneck is everything after the idea.** "There's a ton of great ideas… everybody across product and data and engineering and go-to-market have really good ideas for things that they want to do. And the bottleneck is kind of like everything after that, right? How do you go pull an audience to go and target? How do you go and convince a bunch of people to abide by whatever strategy that you've come up with or playbooks or enablement materials" — and the stated aim is "to reduce that coordination cost" (01:00-01:31). Three named challenges follow. Data: "the necessary data for this was just messy, inconsistent across systems… everybody's operating off of a different source of truth, and that makes it effectively impossible to go and distribute some coordinated action across these different go-to-market teams and channels" (02:41-03:04). Busywork: "reps were just buried in busywork… our sales teams are in back-to-back-to-back-to-back meetings all day. They're outbounding, they're selling, and the operational burden of doing everything in between sales was just really high, which made really scaling out experimentation and creativity challenging" (03:04-03:33). Coordination: "if you're like, 'I have this idea. I'm going to go write this proposal, this enablement material. I'm going to go try to convince a bunch of people to go and use all of this.' That's just a really challenging thing to do on any pace that's not on the order of months" (03:33-03:52).

**The sequencing.** Solve ingestion, consistency, and data quality — "which is just, you know, on the road map every quarter" — then "build those vertical efficiency and growth levers, saving people time in managing operations and execution, as well as… improve conversion rates, make people more performant"; then "extend this horizontally… Teams have very common workflows at some level. Everybody wants to outbound, everybody has meetings. How can we go take the patterns that we build for one team and start to just mirror it to others?"; and only then the current frontier, "this distribution and coordination problem… how can you go and execute across multiple channels simultaneously through just the description of intent?" (03:55-04:53).

**Why the agents are hard.** "Go-to-market agents are complicated… your agents have to understand pretty much the entirety of your company, how you go to market, why products are useful, how to segment your buyers, your prospects, your customers" across the full range "from people who have never heard about you and you have no information on them and they have no information on you, all the way to customers who are actively using your products who have a totally different set of problems" (04:59-05:32).

**The customer data platform.** "If you're looking at this and you're like, 'that looks like a CDP,' yeah, you're right. We effectively went and built an internal customer data platform at Ramp" (05:44-05:55). Inputs are "CRM data, product data, enrichment data, web data, buying signals" — both "things that are internally modeled like… we think that this customer has a high propensity to attach to procurement or treasury" and "external signals like funding announcements" — plus "interaction data… emails, meetings, calls, page views" (05:55-06:28). Real-time events "like emails, you can go and pipe them onto a Kafka topic, consume them, and then funnel them back" into "a Postgres database that backs all of this," which "enables us to maintain transactional guarantees, referential integrity between the entities that exist… between your CRM, between your product, between third parties, and attribute everything to the right level of detail, which we found to be a pretty important problem, as well as all the associated metadata around capturing like where did this come from? When did it come in?" (06:28-07:09). Unstructured data is embedded, because "so much sales data is just inherently unstructured — you have call transcripts, you have emails, you have notes, and the ability to search across that is really valuable" (07:09-07:24). Two batch paths feed the same layer: "a set of online batch jobs, which are really just calling a lot of APIs," sized by market — "Ramp's addressable market is pretty much the entire US and now expanding internationally. So being able to pre-compute, pre-process, pre-ingest all this enrichment data about who we can sell to and who we're already selling to is really important" (07:24-07:49) — and an offline path with "DBT, Snowflake, pulling everything into our warehouse, doing a lot of offline batch compute, and then piping that in via reverse ETL back into the same layer" (07:50-08:05).

**Solve for one team first, then scale horizontally.** "You have a very overlapping set of problems… Everybody wants to do automated outbound. Everybody wants to prepare for meetings. Whereas certain teams may have problems or things that they do that are isolated to them, like QBR generation" (08:07-08:34). The worked vertical is pre-meeting briefs for account managers, who "manage the customer relationships that exist, trying to ensure that customers are using Ramp as best as possible" (08:37-08:55). The brief assembles "what are we talking about? Who are we meeting with? What is the AM trying to do? What are the product usage information? What are the account vitals? What's the agenda that we want to tackle?" alongside the customer's side — "do they have open tickets that they're trying to address? Did they email us saying that there is a specific thing they're trying to talk about?" — so the AM "can go in prepared and manage the operational piece of just being in back-to-back-to-back meetings all day" (08:55-09:33).

**The sneaky-hard part of the vertical is entity hydration.** "If we're trying to generate a pre-meeting brief, we need to know what these meetings are, so we can pipe in meeting events, do some hydration, map things like attendee emails, meeting titles back to the accounts that we're meeting with. This is like a sneaky hard problem at Ramp because you have the same emails that can work on behalf of multiple businesses, so it's kind of like a fuzzy match, and we can go and persist that, so that way every downstream consumer of 'hey, I care about this meeting' doesn't have to go and recompute this from the ground up" (09:35-10:11).

**Durable execution.** "We've also built a system around durable execution that's pretty agnostic to the trigger that comes in. Everything is represented as a durable thread built around Temporal, representing each tool call and model call as an activity. That way, if a worker goes out for some reason, it can resume execution from where it left off, pulling together all the state that had accumulated at that point in time, instead of starting back from the beginning of the thread and trying to reprocess everything, which would be very inefficient and slow" (10:12-10:48). Two capabilities are called out as coming with it: "config-scoped tool calls — different agents are going to have access to different sets of tools, which give them access to different information, different integrations, and different skills that might be necessary to actually perform the work," and "human-in-the-loop tooling to just pause execution, get input, resume" (10:49-11:13).

**The unstructured half.** "Unstructured information is probably the most valuable thing you're sitting on within your warehouse or your notes or wherever you store this today." Real-time inputs are meeting transcripts and emails; batch jobs pull "enablement materials, product knowledge, playbooks," chunking, embedding, and storing them in Turbopuffer, so agents can "search like what do I care about? What am I trying to answer right now?" using "some combination of vector search, attribute search, keyword search in order to pull information scoped to a specific account, for example, without having to pull in the full raw corpus into agent context, which would also be very inefficient, very expensive" (11:16-12:09).

**The skill library, and why it exists.** "We've gone and built a skill library to allow people to customize their agents. Getting back to the meeting brief example, different people have different formats that they care about. They have different information that they care about, and allowing them to represent that in text, giving that to the agent to pull it together, has been very valuable for getting adoption" (12:11-12:32). Composed, the result is "an operational background agent… every night we're going to go and generate these things, fan out a set of agents that are going to go and compute per account meeting prep," using "that online CDP in Postgres I mentioned, the vector database, meeting prep skills that we own at the system level, as well as custom instructions that users are providing themselves" (12:33-13:00).

**Extending the blocks.** "The goal is for these foundations to speed up the next thing… We want to be able to generate things like post-meeting follow-ups and things like automatic CRM updates, which can pull in the transcript and say, 'Hey, we discussed this potential expansion opportunity. Let me go and pre-fill all the information needed to create that opportunity, get a thumbs up from my rep, and just make it happen'" (13:02-13:28). Horizontal extension "is mainly an exercise of creating specific skills, data integrations, and just data ingestion itself, where we can say, 'Okay, email, call transcript embeddings, custom instructions, generalizable, but if we're building this for AEs, we're handling pre-sales opportunities, we need to focus more on third-party data instead of a bunch of product data that we have already, and that needs to be incorporated into our customer data platform. The skills need to reference a different set of information that we have on the people that we're trying to sell to'" (13:29-14:09).

**GT MCP: the same tools, exposed to employees.** "We've built this in a way where employees have access to the same tools and skills that are being used for the background agents that we're creating. We set up what we call our GT MCP, and this is basically just a window into the same exact tools that we've set up for these background agents, so that way the things that we build are just kind of automatically federated out to people who want to go and build their own agents. They want to go chat with the information that we're setting up, and build their own automations" (14:11-14:40). "And they're building a ton of them" — the slide is "a glimpse into some of the analytics that we've done taking the reasoning generated by the MCP tool calls… that are being executed remotely" (14:41-14:56). The loop closes as a roadmap: "this compounds because when people go and build their own thing and they go and connect to our MCP, they're basically telling us, 'Here is a problem that I have. Here's how I'm trying to solve this problem.' And we can go and work with them to be like, 'Okay, we can just go and productionize this, distribute this to everybody who probably has similar problems.' And they give us the prompts and the skills and the, you know, even applications that they're vibe coding, to just really simplify our ability to go and productionize these use cases" (14:57-15:28).

**Back to the golf balls.** "The point that I'm trying to convey by talking about all these specific things that we're doing is that these vertical builds that we're creating are the foundation of multi-team, multi-channel distribution." Given "a playbook — here's how you sell procurement, here's how you sell to construction — or wacky experiment ideas," the system must "take in that corpus of information of things that people are trying to do and federate that out through the background agents that are actually creating these artifacts that people are using to operationalize go-to-market and execute" (15:39-16:27). Concretely, the intent funnels into Ramp Revenue, "the internal application that we have built," and then into the vertical solutions: "for SDRs, we want to create an audience of here are the golfers that we want to send things to. We can generate personalized copy and sequences that they can send. Maybe we want to create web landing pages and spin up the images and the creative that will point these email sequences to. And we can do all of that through just the description of like here's my intent. Get the people who own these channels to review them and sign off" (16:29-17:10). The stated payoff is "ship faster, ship safer, scale our teams, become more efficient," with campaigns executed "across multiple channels with consistent audience targeting"; agents "can hold context on multiple things that are like options… and balance the traditional multi-armed bandit problem of exploring new possibilities versus being safe and going into just known returns"; and "we can build in guardrails as well to manage compliance rules, rules of engagement, and being context aware, making sure we're not doing the same thing over and over again" (17:20-18:11).

**Q&A: what a smaller company should do.** "I would go and find the very specific use cases that you can build automation around and just solve really specific problems that exist first. Like 3 years ago, there was two of us and we were building automated outbound… we're just trying to figure out how can we go and use GPT 3.5 and put personalized copy into some sequences and pull data from wherever to generate that. And by doing these things and solving these problems, you get a really good understanding of how this works, how it could extend to other teams… The reality is that you can't spend a year going and building some really complicated system architecture that is perfect. So you have to piece together the vertical solutions and then stick them together" (18:33-19:36).

## Provenance and Limits

- **Nothing in the talk is measured.** There is no adoption figure, no accuracy figure, no latency figure, no cost figure, no headcount, no volume of accounts, meetings, or agent runs, and no before/after on the coordination timescale the talk exists to compress. "Months" is the only number attached to the problem, and it is an estimate offered in passing (03:49-03:51).
- **The one outcome claim is an aside.** "Offering Pro V1s to golfers, which is actually like… it works really well" (16:05-16:10) has no metric, denominator, comparison, or channel attached, and the speaker introduces the whole example as a hypothetical ("suppose I want to offer golfers…", 02:02) — so it is unclear whether the campaign was run through the described orchestration layer or at all.
- **The orchestration layer — the talk's title and thesis — is the part that is not built.** Every worked example (pre-meeting briefs, the CDP, the durable threads, the skill library, GT MCP) is a vertical; the multi-channel fan-out from a described intent is stated in the future tense ("what we're building towards", 00:31; "the goal is to funnel this into Ramp Revenue", 16:32). The multi-armed bandit balancing and the compliance/rules-of-engagement guardrails are named as capabilities the design allows, with no implementation described.
- **Adoption is asserted, not shown.** The skill library "has been very valuable for getting adoption" (12:30-12:32) and employees are "building a ton" of their own automations (14:41-14:43) are both unquantified; the analytics slide over MCP tool-call reasoning is described but its contents are not read out.
- **No evaluation, review, or quality control is described for any generated artifact.** There is no eval, judge, rubric, tracing claim, or error rate for the pre-meeting brief, the extracted opportunity fields, the generated copy, or the generated creative. The only quality control named anywhere is human sign-off by channel owners (17:06-17:10) and a rep's "thumbs up" on a pre-filled CRM opportunity (13:25-13:27).
- **Entity hydration is named as hard and left unsolved on the page.** "The same emails that can work on behalf of multiple businesses, so it's kind of like a fuzzy match" (09:56-10:01) states the problem and the persistence strategy; no matching method, confidence threshold, error rate, or handling of a wrong match is given, and a wrong meeting-to-account mapping silently mis-briefs a rep in front of a customer.
- **The security and permissions surface is one clause.** "Config-scoped tool calls" (10:52) is the only access-control mechanism mentioned, and it is described as an out-of-the-box capability rather than a policy. Exposing the background agents' entire tool surface to employees over MCP (14:20-14:27) is presented purely as a distribution win, with no mention of what a user may read, what the tools may write, or how scoping differs between a background agent and a person's own agent.
- **Vendors are named as in use, not evaluated:** Temporal, Kafka, Postgres, Snowflake, dbt, Turbopuffer, and GPT-3.5 (historically). No selection criteria, cost, or alternative is discussed for any of them, and the reverse-ETL tool is unnamed.
- **The advice is drawn from one company's shape.** Ramp's addressable market being "pretty much the entire US" (07:34-07:36) is what makes pre-computed enrichment across the whole market affordable and necessary; the same batch-everything decision does not obviously transfer to a business with a small named-account list.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "Armon" is Arman; "Turbo Buffer" is Turbopuffer; "DBT" is dbt; "AMs are like count account account managers" is a disfluency for account managers; "a worker goes out" is a worker going down; "which gives, uh, or which use some set of tools" is a self-correction. Left as heard: "Madhu" as the host's name, "Ramp Revenue" as the internal application name, "Pro V1" as the golf ball, and "GT MCP" as the internal MCP server's name.

## Notes

- The talk's structure is unusual for the go-to-market cluster: it opens on the end state (describe a motion, get multi-channel execution), spends fifteen minutes on foundations that are built, and returns to the end state as something the foundations make possible. Read it as an argument about *sequencing*, not as a system description (00:29-00:59, 15:39-16:27).
- The three bottlenecks are worth keeping distinct because they take different fixes: inconsistent data is an engineering problem, rep busywork is an automation problem, and coordination cost is a distribution problem. Only the third is what the title is about, and it is the one still open (02:41-03:52).
- "Everybody's operating off of a different source of truth… makes it effectively impossible to go and distribute some coordinated action" is the load-bearing link between the data layer and the distribution layer: consistency is not sought for its own sake but because a coordinated multi-channel action cannot be defined over disagreeing audiences (02:53-03:04).
- The CDP admission is deliberate deflation — "if you're looking at this and you're like, 'that looks like a CDP,' yeah, you're right" (05:44-05:48). The novelty claimed is not the architecture but that it is internal, agent-addressable, and holds embedded unstructured data alongside the structured entities.
- Choosing Postgres over a key-value store is the sharpest architectural contrast in the go-to-market cluster: Notion serves a denormalized profile with no joins for millisecond agent reads, while Ramp keeps transactional guarantees and referential integrity across CRM, product, and third-party entities. The two designs optimize opposite properties and neither source acknowledges the other's tradeoff (06:38-06:58).
- Provenance is built into the row, not bolted on: "all the associated metadata around capturing like where did this come from? When did it come in?" (07:05-07:09) — the same discipline Liu describes as ownership and timestamps on modeled entities.
- Two batch paths feed one layer — online jobs that "are really just calling a lot of APIs" for enrichment, and an offline dbt/Snowflake path returning via reverse ETL (07:24-08:05). The serving layer is the convergence point for both, which is what lets an agent read one place.
- The horizontal-extension analysis is the most transferable paragraph in the talk: email and call-transcript embeddings and custom instructions generalize, while the data sources and the skills do not — moving a meeting-brief build from account managers to account executives means swapping product data for third-party data and rewriting the skills against it (13:29-14:09).
- Durable-thread granularity is stated precisely: each tool call *and* each model call is an activity, and resumption rehydrates accumulated state rather than replaying the thread (10:22-10:48). This matches the wiki's Temporal material from Colvin and Davis and adds a go-to-market workload to it.
- Config-scoped tool calls and pause/resume human-in-the-loop are described as out-of-the-box properties of the durable-execution layer rather than as things built on top (10:49-11:13), which is an argument for adopting the substrate rather than assembling the capabilities.
- The retrieval design is scoped rather than global: hybrid vector, attribute, and keyword search "to pull information scoped to a specific account… without having to pull in the full raw corpus into agent context" (11:52-12:09). Attribute filtering is what makes the account scope enforceable rather than hoped for.
- The skill library's stated purpose is adoption, not capability: people wanted their own brief format, and giving them one is what got them to use the brief (12:11-12:32). This sits directly opposite Cloudflare's central curation alias, which exists to *prevent* skill proliferation.
- "An operational background agent" composes four inputs — the Postgres CDP, the vector store, system-owned skills, and user custom instructions — and runs nightly per account (12:33-13:00). The scheduling discipline is a fan-out over the account list, not a wake on an event.
- The write path is described as pre-fill plus thumbs-up: the agent parses the transcript, pre-fills every field needed to create the CRM opportunity, and the rep approves (13:14-13:28). This is the same read-first, gated-write pattern the rest of the cluster reports.
- GT MCP inverts the usual internal-platform relationship: instead of the platform team collecting requirements, employees reveal both the problem and their attempted solution by connecting, and hand over "the prompts and the skills and the even applications that they're vibe coding" (14:57-15:28).
- Analytics are run over "the reasoning generated by the MCP tool calls" (14:47-14:52) — the model's own explanation text as telemetry, distinct from counting tool invocations.
- The final approval design is per-channel rather than per-action: one intent produces audience, copy, sequences, landing pages, and creative, and "the people who own these channels review them and sign off" (17:06-17:10), which puts the gate on the person accountable for the surface rather than on a generic reviewer.
- The Q&A answer contradicts the talk's own architecture-first appearance: "you can't spend a year going and building some really complicated system architecture that is perfect. So you have to piece together the vertical solutions and then stick them together" (19:26-19:36), with the two-person GPT-3.5 outbound project three years earlier as the origin story (18:56-19:14).
