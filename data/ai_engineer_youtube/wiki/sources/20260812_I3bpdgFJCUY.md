## Summary

Ben Holmes (developer relations lead at Warp) demonstrates an end-to-end pipeline that turns a disorganized personal notes folder into a browsable, agent-maintained wiki, and the sequencing is the argument: capture first, structure later, always by machine. He opens on the audience's own problem — a show of hands for "an Apple Notes folder that's a complete disorganized mess" gets most of the room — and locates the bottleneck not in organization but in capture. Speech runs about 200 words per minute; "unless you're an absolute Olympic typist, it will be faster than any other method that you have," so he dictates every note, on desktop or phone, and deliberately keeps it sloppy: "don't worry if you're being a little bit scrappy, a little bit rambly… get down as many thoughts in the moment as possible." The reason is downstream supply, not comfort: "if you want to get to a point where you can actually have LLMs generate wikis, visualizations, etc., you need a lot of raw data. You need a lot of raw materials." He names two local, on-device dictation tools that remove the subscription — Handy (open source) and VoiceInk (captioned "Voice Inc.", a ~$20 lifetime fee) — the latter giving both a hotkey and a mobile app.

Structure then arrives in two agent-run passes over the same Markdown files. An `enrich note` skill stamps each file with an enrichment timestamp "so if we ask the agent to do another pass it remembers that some other agent did it in the past," which is what makes the batch run resumable — the automation "will go ahead and find any notes that weren't enriched in the past… look for anything that's not tagged yet." It assigns tags from a fixed reference folder rather than inventing them, with an explicit instruction "to be reluctant to add new tags because Claude loves to get creative" and a narrow escape hatch for genuine new patterns. It researches the source on the web (any agent's web search, or "exa is a great little tool that you can add into your harness") to recover the podcast or article URL. And it writes backlinks into the bottom of each note found by key-term search over the other files — no embeddings, just "some file calls, find related notes using key term search." The result is navigable on its own: reading notes on *The Left Hand of Darkness* taken across weeks link to each other, so "I'm not hunting around where did I save in my Apple Notes the last time I wrote about this," and the effect is that "it kind of invents a Wikipedia rabbit hole of your own thoughts." A second pass generates a wiki above the notes — an entity layer of people, concepts, organizations, and sources scoped to a stated focus area — an idea he credits to a gist by Andrej Karpathy (captioned "Andre Carpathy"), findable by searching "his name and then wiki." He shows two: a "latest in AI news" wiki (entries for a Ralph-loop rabbit hole, for the jazz musician Adam Neely on AI and the music industry) and one over a Bible-in-a-Year podcast, where the payoff is a per-character entry for names that "sound very similar and can be hard to pronounce." The work version is a people section of everyone you met with, backlinked to the meetings and to the raw source notes. His own justification for generating rather than writing it: "all I have time to do is generate the raw ingredients, not connecting it all together myself."

The last third moves the whole loop off the human's hands and off the human's laptop. Generation is slow — "it can take time for an agent to go generate these things" — which is the argument for a schedule rather than an on-demand prompt: run the enrich and wiki skills daily or weekly, "maybe while we sleep." He draws the local/cloud line explicitly: the Codex app's automations "spin up tasks to run on your machine every day, but it means your laptop has to be cracked open when it runs because it's a local automation." His cloud version stands up a sandbox every day (Docker-backed if needed), syncs the Markdown down with the Obsidian headless CLI, runs `enrich note` across everything not yet enriched, and syncs back up. He prefers the Obsidian CLI to the obvious alternative — "you could also just do like a git clone… put all of your notes in a GitHub folder so that way a cloud agent could pull it down" — because it "avoids having to like push and pull your notes. No one has time for that." The prompt is exactly that shape: "I gave you the Obsidian CLI in your environment. Use that, pull down that wiki, go ahead and update it, [special instructions for my own setup], and then sync it back up." The payoff he names is a review artifact: "I wake up to a perfectly fresh wiki that I can review. It's like the daily paper, but it's your own." Finally he goes past Markdown: he asked an agent to build, in HTML and Tailwind, a clickable force-graph over every note — "this is not a tool that you have to install… I told an agent, build this for me" — restyled it into a star constellation on request, and added a GitHub-style habit chart for note-writing frequency. Its use is diagnostic rather than navigational: clusters show books, startup founding, AI and engineering, faith and scripture, and "it's useful just to get an idea of what you're actually interested in and where you have gaps in your thinking."

Vendor provenance travels with all of this: the speaker is Warp's DevRel lead, the harness in every demo is Warp ("totally unbiased. I'm a really big fan of warp"), the scheduler is Warp's own oz.dev / warp.dev/os, and the notes app is his open-source Hubble (hub.md), pitched as "an Apple notes that's agent accessible." Nothing in the talk is measured — there are no benchmarks, error rates, or costs, and no discussion of what the enrichment pass gets wrong.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Stamp Processing State in the Artifact to Make Agent Passes Resumable](../concepts/stamp-processing-state-in-the-artifact-to-make-agent-passes-resumable.md) - An enrichment timestamp in each note is the whole checkpoint store for a repeated batch pass.
- [Materialize Backlinks at Ingest With Key-Term Search](../concepts/materialize-backlinks-at-ingest-with-key-term-search.md) - Links written into the note at enrichment time replace query-time similarity search with browsable structure.
- [Generate an Entity Wiki Over Your Own Notes](../concepts/generate-an-entity-wiki-over-your-own-notes.md) - A people/concepts/organizations/sources layer scoped to a stated focus area, from Karpathy's LLM-wiki gist.

### Provenance and Caveats

- Vendor talk. The speaker is Warp's DevRel lead; the harness in every demo is Warp ("totally unbiased. I'm a really big fan of warp. Can you believe it?"), the scheduler is Warp's oz.dev, and the notes app is his own open-source Hubble/hub.md. The pipeline shape is separable from those products — he explicitly says any Markdown viewer and any harness works, and offers git clone as the sync alternative — but every specific tool recommendation except Handy, VoiceInk, and Exa is first-party.
- Nothing is measured. There are no error rates, costs, latencies, or comparisons: no report of how often the tagger drifts, how good the key-term backlinks are relative to any alternative, whether the wiki entries are accurate, or what a regenerated wiki does to entries you edited by hand. Treat everything here as a demonstrated build, not a validated one.
- Scale is unstated. The corpus is one person's personal notes; no note count is given, and nothing in the talk addresses what happens when the enrichment pass or the wiki generation no longer fits in an agent's budget.
- The 200 wpm figure is hedged by the speaker himself ("I believe, is the average") and is a speech-rate claim, not a measurement of his own capture throughput.
