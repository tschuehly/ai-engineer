## Summary

Kanish Manuja, a principal engineer at Twilio, builds a sixteen-minute operations talk out of one string: "something went wrong. Please try again." His claim is that the message is not laziness but the visible cost of a design decision — "behind that simple message is actually a system that is very complex that serves you that message despite the model providers being down" (00:20-00:48). An LLM gateway is "an entry point or a middleware between your apps and the model providers behind them," doing "routing, authentication, fallback, rate limits, all kinds of governance" (00:50-01:05), and "right at the heart of the gateway is a fight between four things. It's availability, latency, your guardrails and costs. In case of a degradation, you cannot maximize all four. You need to pick what you want" (01:08-01:24). The talk has two audiences and two asks: gateway *users* should decide which of the four they will give up, and gateway *designers* should "provide those levers to your callers and customers" rather than deciding on their behalf (01:24-01:43).

The availability section is the one that argues against ordinary engineering reflexes. A single provider means "their ceiling is your ceiling. Their outage is your outage" (01:46-01:57), and the normal answer — retry with exponential backoff and jitter, then trip a circuit breaker — is wrong here for three separate reasons: "retrying an LLM API eats into your latency budget really fast"; "tripping over a circuit breaker when you have another perfectly fine model provider to route to doesn't make sense"; and because the calls are slow and expensive, "blind retries just multiply your cost and your tail latencies" (02:03-02:58). The replacement is a *per-request fallback* — try provider A, then provider B in sequence — with parallel dual-fire available "only if you're highly highly obsessed with latencies because that's just going to double your cost" (03:00-03:27). Circuit breaking survives in a demoted role: a primary that has been failing for a while comes out of the load balancer into a cool-down and is retried "after a few minutes have passed" (03:28-03:52). Manuja flags one design choice inside that: whether failure counts live in memory per instance or in shared infrastructure across the fleet. "If you want quick failovers, then fleetwide helps," while local counters mean "whenever you change your deployment size your configuration and your expectations change" (03:53-04:35).

Two gotchas hang off the clean diagram. First, "fallbacks are not transparent": the industry is converging on an OpenAI-compatible format but "there are still nuances" in "tool calling schemas, token limits, stop reasons and what have you," so cross-provider fallback needs a normalization layer and real testing (04:35-05:07). Second — and this is the talk's sharpest single observation — streaming forecloses the fallback entirely. "Nobody wants to wait for 30 seconds to have a wall of text appear in front of them," but "you trade away your levers… once you have decided to go with provider A, you have to continue going with provider A. You cannot mid-stream change the providers. Whatever has been sent to the client, it's done. And that's where the something went wrong message… It's not because of laziness. It's by design" (05:08-05:55). He closes availability on a provisioning asymmetry teams get backwards: they test and provision the primary well, but "your throughputs or your capacity or your headroom should be even higher for the second provider… because that's your last line of defense. If that goes down, your application goes down" (05:55-06:29).

Latency gets the opposite framing from availability: "availability failures are right in your face. They fail. You get alarmed. You get paged. But high latencies can be the quiet ones" (06:30-06:52). A gateway runs mixed workloads — embeddings and classification under a second, chat around three seconds, reasoning far longer (06:54-07:15) — so an aggregate service latency number "doesn't make sense. It's a lie. You should be tracking your P99 per model per route, not a gateway wide number" (07:15-07:40). Timeouts get the same per-model-class, per-route treatment, and their absence is named as "the number one root cause of your silent outage. If you don't have a timeout, your gateway thinks your request is being happily served while it is not" (07:41-08:02). The line he leaves the section on: "a reasoning model's normal is actually a chat model's outage" (08:02-08:16). Reasoning and router models are "the slide that has given me the most scars" — nondeterministic, "you cannot set the temperature to zero in many cases," and "the same prompt can take somewhere from 2 seconds to 60 seconds and we've seen that in production where P99 suddenly popped to 60 seconds for no good reason" (08:17-08:56). There is "no magical solution," only two partial ones: fix the reasoning level per route, and treat router models — which "hide that abstraction behind you… they pick which models to run" — as something to pin down, making "requests as deterministic as possible with an undeterministic system" (08:56-09:22). Separately, hedging: fire a second request once the primary has consumed, say, P90 of the latency budget, to cut the P99 tail (09:24-09:43).

Guardrails — prompt-injection defence, PII filters, toxicity filters, "keeping the LLMs to stop swearing at your customers" — are treated as dependencies rather than as policy. "Guardrails are just like another service that can go down that can be unreliable and that's where you need to choose do you fail open or do you fail close," which is "the trade-off between availability and security" (09:45-10:40). There is "no universal answer" — a toxicity filter that is down may be survivable — and the decision rule he offers is "the default choice should be the worst case that you can live with" (10:40-11:00). Three mitigations follow. A time budget: "your request should never be bound by your guardrail timing. It should always be the LLM that is the rate determining step," enforced with timeouts on the guardrail itself (11:03-11:38). Fallbacks for guardrails specifically — "secondary provider, secondary checks, cache decisions" — because they are "critical services too" (11:38-12:02). And placement, in three positions: a pre-hook on the input is "probably the safest… but it does add serial latency"; parallel is his favourite but "streaming wouldn't work well here… so if you're specially producing structured output, please don't stream them… run these guardrails concurrently"; post-hooks are "best for output monitoring, auditing your outputs" (12:04-12:47).

The last two sections turn on the gateway itself. It is "another dependency in the request path" (12:58-13:16), so segregate API keys "per route, per use case, to the most granular thing that you can imagine," because "having a noisy tenant can be one of the biggest problems here" (13:24-13:48); and make sure the gateway supports load shedding, rehearsed in runbooks and game days, because "when you have a retry storm, it becomes really hard to just scale out. You cannot simply scale out services that is under a retry storm" — web-server internal queues must be bounded, and traffic prioritization keeps the most important use cases served under load (13:49-14:31). The closing argument is that a company-wide central gateway "is a single point of failure," and that most teams asking for one have misdiagnosed their own requirement: "it's not the central gateway that they want. They want centralized governance." Those separate — "do not try to centralize your traffic, but you can have plugins, you can have custom code that can centralize your governance," with governance meaning cost tracking and rate-limit management. One team can own it; "I wouldn't recommend deploying it as a single deployment for the entire company even though it's distributed" (14:34-15:42).

## Provenance and Limits

- Nothing in the talk is measured. There is no availability figure, no latency distribution, no cost comparison, no incident count, and no before-and-after for any recommendation. Every claim is stated as practitioner experience.
- The only production observation with a number attached is the reasoning-model tail — "the same prompt can take somewhere from 2 seconds to 60 seconds," and a P99 that "suddenly popped to 60 seconds for no good reason" (08:17-08:56). The cause is never identified, so it is a symptom report rather than a finding.
- "The number one root cause of your silent outage" (missing timeouts, 07:41-08:02) is offered without a sample, a period, or an organization. It is a ranking asserted from memory.
- The recommendations are not evaluated against each other or against alternatives. Per-request fallback is argued from three reasons why retries and breakers are wrong, not from a comparison of outcomes; hedging is proposed with no cost figure for the duplicate requests it generates; parallel dual-fire is priced only as "double your cost."
- Several prescriptions are stated at a level of abstraction that hides the hard part. "Fix the reasoning level per route" is the entire answer to reasoning-model latency variance, with no method for choosing the level. The normalization layer for cross-provider differences is named but not specified. Load shedding and traffic prioritization are recommended as gateway features to check for, not as designs.
- The talk is generic about vendors — no gateway product, model provider, or guardrail vendor is named anywhere, including Twilio's own stack. Nothing can be traced to a specific implementation, which makes the advice portable but unverifiable.
- The four-way tradeoff (availability, latency, guardrails, cost) is asserted as a framing device rather than derived, and the talk does not work through a case where two of the four are traded against each other explicitly. The one worked example is fail-open versus fail-closed, which is availability against security.
- The closing argument against a central gateway is the least supported claim in the talk. "In most scenarios, it's not the central gateway that they want. They want centralized governance" is an observation about teams he has spoken to, with no count; and the plugin/custom-code path to decentralized enforcement is asserted, not shown. The operational cost of the decentralized alternative — many deployments to upgrade, config drift, inconsistent policy versions — is not discussed at all.
- Two audience show-of-hands moments (00:18, 07:15) are used rhetorically; no counts are reported.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "Kanesh Manuja" → Kanish Manuja (00:13, per the video description); "LM gateway" → LLM gateway and "LMS" → LLMs throughout; "the most scarse" → the most scars (08:19); "your gateway thinks you're hap your request is being happily served" → happily served (07:54); "centralize government governance" → centralize governance (15:05); "undeterministic" is the speaker's own word for nondeterministic and is quoted as spoken (08:36-09:20). Left unresolved: the fragment at 05:08-05:16 ("Another thing is streaming / it."), where the slide text is lost.

## Extracted Concepts

- [An LLM Gateway Cannot Maximize Availability, Latency, Guardrails, and Cost at Once](../concepts/an-llm-gateway-cannot-maximize-availability-latency-guardrails-and-cost.md) - the talk's frame: degradation forces you to give one of the four up, and the gateway's job is to expose that choice as a lever rather than make it for the caller.
- [Streaming Forecloses the Provider Fallback](../concepts/streaming-forecloses-the-provider-fallback.md) - once tokens are on the wire you are committed to that provider, which is why "something went wrong, please try again" exists at all.
- [Prefer Per-Request Fallback to Retries and Circuit Breakers for LLM Calls](../concepts/prefer-per-request-fallback-to-retries-and-circuit-breakers-for-llm-calls.md) - retrying a slow expensive call spends the latency budget and multiplies spend, and opening a breaker is the wrong move when a healthy second provider is available.
- [Your Fallback Provider Is Under-Tested and Under-Provisioned](../concepts/your-fallback-provider-is-under-tested-and-under-provisioned.md) - the last line of defence needs more headroom than the primary and a normalization layer, because OpenAI-compatible is not the same as identical.
- [Track Latency and Timeouts Per Model Class Per Route](../concepts/track-latency-and-timeouts-per-model-class-per-route.md) - a gateway-wide P99 over mixed workloads is a lie, and a missing timeout is the named number-one cause of silent outages.
- [Treat Guardrails as a Failable Dependency With Its Own Time Budget](../concepts/treat-guardrails-as-a-failable-dependency-with-a-time-budget.md) - guardrails go down like any service, so decide fail-open versus fail-closed in advance, bound their time, and choose their placement knowingly.
- [Decentralize the Gateway, Centralize the Governance](../concepts/decentralize-the-gateway-centralize-the-governance.md) - most teams asking for one company-wide gateway want consistent cost tracking and rate limits, which does not require routing all traffic through one deployment.
