## Summary

Jared Joselowitz is a research engineer on the science team at Ufonia, a UK healthcare company, where he builds the safety and evaluation stack behind **Dora** — a voice agent that phones patients for post-op follow-ups and pre-op checks (~200,000 real clinical calls across 20 UK hospitals, contracted to reach a million patients in two years, plus a 2026 US launch live in two clinics with six more signed across four states). Because Dora asks about symptoms and gives advice, it is a regulated medical device. The talk's engineering argument is that the industry's default de-risking loop — ship to 5%, watch the dashboard, roll back — silently assumes you can afford to be wrong for an instant, and that assumption fails when the user is a patient: 5% is thousands of people, a spoken call cannot be un-said, and a dashboard turning red means someone was already harmed. Ufonia's replacement borrows the self-driving playbook: enumerate hazards with clinicians first, then run them in a simulation framework (**Matrix**) where one LLM plays the patient (**PatBot**) and a second LLM judges every dialogue against expected behaviors and hazard scenarios. Both models were validated rather than assumed — a patient-and-public-involvement study found real patients picked the *simulated* patient as more realistic in three of four sets, and the judge, checked against 10 clinicians from 10 specialties on 240 cases, matched or beat them at F1 0.96 with near-perfect sensitivity. Prompts are then optimized automatically with GEPA against a clinician-defined **cost matrix** rather than hand-tuned against flat accuracy. Simulation is explicitly necessary but not sufficient: it "earns the right" to a staged, clinician-supervised rollout where autonomy is granted in proportion to accumulated evidence, and every dataset, pinned prompt, and judge verdict traces back to the exact hazard it addresses — "you don't ship the model, you ship the evidence."

## Notes

- Role and remit: research engineer on Ufonia's science team, which "built the safety and evaluation stack behind Dora"; the job is "proving that the product is safe before a patient ever actually hears it." (00:33-00:55)
- Three safety nets that disappear when you ship to patients: you cannot A/B test, because "randomizing patients into a worse variant is unethical and often illegal"; you cannot undo a call — "once Dora says it, it's been said and there is no rollback"; and the model card will not save you — a vendor's "92% on some benchmark" is "not a defense at a post incident review." (00:58-01:30)
- Dora is a voice AI agent that phones patients for real clinical conversations such as post-op follow-ups and pre-op checks — calls a clinician would otherwise make. It "doesn't replace doctors" but takes a hugely time-consuming task off their plate. (01:35-01:53)
- Reported scale: ~200,000 real clinical calls across 20 UK hospitals, contracted to scale to a million patients in the next two years; US product released this year, live in two clinics with six more signed across four states. (01:55-02:12)
- The demo shows Dora handling a post-cataract-surgery follow-up: probing whether blurred vision is at distance or up close, when it started, reassuring that "blurry vision should improve rapidly in the first few days after surgery," and answering an unprompted question about swimming with "you should avoid swimming for a month after surgery." (02:22-03:18)
- Because it asks about symptoms, gives advice, and answers questions, Dora "is what's called a medical device and therefore it has to of course be regulated." Regulation reduces to three questions — what does your software do, what could go wrong, and how do you ensure that it doesn't — and while that is "quite tractable" for normal software, "for a model that talks to actual patients, what could go wrong is quite huge." (03:24-03:50)
- Start from harm, not from features. Worked examples of hazards: Dora misses a red-flag symptom such as sudden vision loss or severe pain; a patient asks a medical question and Dora hallucinates an answer; the patient is distressed and Dora ignores it and carries on. There are "20, 30, 40" documented hazards and "we have to ensure that none of them actually happen in real life." (03:53-04:22)
- The normal playbook — ship to a small percentage, watch the dashboard, roll back if it breaks, iterate — is described as good, reactive, fast, and "how the industry usually de-risks a launch," but it carries a hidden assumption: "it only works because you can afford to be wrong for an instance." (04:25-04:51)
- Why the assumption fails here: 5% "could be hundreds if not thousands of patients that have got unproven changes and undue care"; you cannot roll back because "the call has already happened, the person has already been harmed"; and "the dashboards going red means that a patient was actually hurt." The conclusion: "the reactive loop is actually gone now. So how do you iterate at all when you can't touch a patient until you're sure?" (04:57-05:26)
- The borrowed playbook is self-driving: "they didn't just drive around crashing into walls and say we won't do that again and then doing another RL loop" — they "put millions of miles of simulations first" before any passengers. Simulation "is the only real ethical option we can go with. You can't run all the hazards I just mentioned on real people as a first grasp." (05:28-06:08)
- **Matrix** is their simulation framework for clinical history taking; a paper is on arXiv. "At its core, Matrix recreates a real clinical conversation but with no real patient in it." (06:10-06:31)
- **PatBot** is the LLM playing the patient. A simulated patient is used rather than a hired actor because "hired actors don't scale" — iterating fast and simulating many things at once while updating the system "would just be too slow." (06:31-06:52)
- PatBot is conditioned on the scenario under test — the scenario "defines exactly what the patient should try and do when talking to our agent," e.g. asking whether the agent is a human or an AI — and the whole run happens "under a very specific clinical use case context," so scenarios are "grounded in real clinical workflows and not abstract situations." (06:54-07:23)
- First validation of the simulator is a pure script-adherence check: "if we told PatBot to do something, does PatBot do it? Yes or no?" This "helped us filter out a lot of maybe weaker models that didn't listen to instructions properly," but instruction-following alone "does not make a realistic patient." (07:25-07:57)
- Second validation is a PPI (patient and public involvement) study: real patients were shown two conversations side by side — one between a real doctor and a real patient, one between Dora and PatBot inside Matrix — and asked to identify which patient was real. Across four conversation sets, "in three out of the four, the majority of people actually thought that the simulated patient was more realistic." (08:00-08:55)
- The more important finding was that "there is no single realistic patient" — some people are verbose with "a lot of ums and ahs," some are terse — so the goal is to simulate "people with very diverse personas" rather than one canonical patient. (08:56-09:19)
- Judging thousands of simulated dialogues by hand does not scale, and the engineers "aren't clinicians, so [we can't] actually know if an actual hazard has really occurred." Hence an LLM judge (referred to in the talk as `BevJudge`) that takes the simulated dialogue, a set of expected behaviors, and the hazardous scenarios agreed with clinicians, and returns pass/fail plus a reason — "a structured output of which hazards were triggered and what actually went wrong in that scenario." (09:25-10:02)
- Judge validation: a 240-example corpus with ground truth for whether a hazard existed; 10 clinicians from 10 clinical specialties labeled it, and so did the judge. The judge came out "at least on par, if not slightly better, than the real expert clinicians" — the top model at the time of the paper (a year earlier) was Gemini 2.5 Pro at F1 0.96. (10:04-10:36)
- Sensitivity mattered more than F1: the judge "achieved almost perfect sensitivity," and in healthcare "you would rather overcall hazards that aren't there than undercall hazards that are there." The expert-level automated judge "is actually what makes this whole process scalable." (10:38-11:03)
- Grading is not improving: "a pile of pass/fails tells you where Dora breaks and where it's not safe, but doesn't actually make the product better." (11:05-11:18)
- Prompt brittleness is why hand-tuning was abandoned: "formatting changes alone have been seen to swing benchmarks by 76 percentage points," and "reordering few-shot examples flips a model from near random, so near 50%, to near state-of-the-art on some benchmarks." Hand-tuning is "very subjective, it's not reproducible, and very importantly, it's extremely time-consuming." (11:33-11:55)
- They use **GEPA** (spoken "Jeppa," genetic Pareto), "from the same people who made DSPy." The loop: define a metric for what good is; run your data through; it reports which examples failed; "you get a very strong LLM to reflect on the failures and update the prompt automatically"; repeat, keeping "a pareto frontier of the best prompts until your budget has been exhausted." (11:56-12:42)
- Payoff: manual prompt engineering "would take in the order of hours to days," optimization takes "between 30 minutes and an hour"; it is reproducible, with "a very clear audit trail and clear feedback loop," and the remaining work becomes "purely a data science problem" — the data, the feature engineering, and defining the metric together with clinicians. (12:43-13:15)
- The metric is not a flat accuracy score or a dataset average — "you give it a cost matrix." Catching a present red flag is good; missing it "could be catastrophic"; over-calling a red flag that is not there "is just mildly annoying to the patient — they may need to answer a couple extra questions, but it's not a catastrophic harm situation." (13:20-13:58)
- So the feedback metric gives "a higher reward for finding the red flags and a lower reward for missing them," and a clinician who wants to optimize accuracy or some other metric only has to "recompile the prompt" to get a new optimized one. (13:59-14:26)
- The replacement for the reactive loop, as a flywheel: real call data + synthetic edge cases that may not occur naturally (rare symptoms, mis-transcriptions) → an optimized prompt via GEPA or another optimizer → Matrix as a **simulation safety gate**, where anything failing sends you back to redo the data, relabel, or gather more → a gated deploy. "Every single deployment and every new call produces more call data, so your system is consistently improving." (14:28-15:18)
- Simulation is necessary but not sufficient: "however realistic you think they are, of course they are not real patients," and "passing every test in simulation doesn't prove that Dora actually helps someone in real life. It only earns the right to actually try carefully." Simulation is the inner loop — "fast, free, you can do thousands of runs before anyone actually real is exposed" — and "real patients are the outer loop, and that's where the only real proof is." (15:22-15:53)
- The crossing is staged, and "each stage earns the right for the next": simulation → a round of user testing → supervised clinical evaluation grounded in real patients (voice actors are possible, but real patients are most realistic) with "clinicians at every step in the loop" → monitored deployments. Critically, "how much autonomy you allow the system to do depends on your evidence. As the system gets more evidence, you can give it more independence." (15:54-16:36)
- The real deliverable is traceability: "every call, every data set, and every pinned prompt, every judge verdict traces back to the exact hazard that it addresses… you don't ship the model, you ship the evidence when trying to regulate." (16:37-16:52)
- Portable takeaways offered to non-healthcare stacks: define exactly what harm means for your product; "manufacture your rare but dangerous cases — don't wait for them just to happen naturally"; "make your evaluation metric your real cost function to optimize"; and "pin your prompt versions and keep the traces." (16:52-17:12)
- New modalities bring new hazards but not a new framework. Voice adds backchanneling and interruptions "which breaks a lot of the text" assumptions: an agent mid-safety-advice ("you must avoid bright lights") can be cut off by an out-of-scope question, and "weak models usually just forget about the safety advice and just answer the next question." Matrix captures these. (17:14-18:14)
- "The failure modes [change]. The framework doesn't." The method is unchanged: black-box the system, write down the new hazards, and simulate and judge them exactly as over text — "voice is just a new module in the same safety case," and Matrix is already used in the voice space. (18:15-18:44)
