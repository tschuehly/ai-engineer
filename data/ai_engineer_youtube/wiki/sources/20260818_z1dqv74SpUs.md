## Summary

Sidney Primas, CTO and founder of LemonSlice, describes how a real-time photorealistic avatar is built and served: train a video diffusion transformer, make it interactive by training with an attention mask that forbids attending to the future, then make it real time by collapsing roughly 30 denoising steps to one. The talk is unusually specific about what that costs. Because the model can only look backward, each generated block conditions on blocks that already contain error, so error accumulates over a session — and the sessions are long, with a Teddy Roosevelt avatar generating continuously for eight hours with no reset and a second deployment planned for sixteen. Two other findings are the reason to keep the talk. The audio encoder, not the video model, is what limits expressiveness, because most audio encoders are trained on audiobooks and audiobooks are monotone by construction. And the "model harness" — the thread and queue orchestration across GPU and CPU that keeps video from stuttering through interrupts — is where Primas now expects most of the durable productization value to sit. LemonSlice sells the visual layer as an API over a customer's own LLM and voice, reports serving cost roughly at parity with a voice model, and forecasts a single end-to-end "EQ layer" model fed by a separate "IQ" model within two to three years.

## Notes

- Framing and goal. LemonSlice's stated mission is "to break the Avatar Turing test," meaning an avatar "that is indistinguishable from a human on a video call." Photorealism is assumed; the named long tail is "getting the emotions right… the object interactions right… the micro-expressions right, and even figuring out an internal state for these avatars so that they feel like a real human being" (00:19-01:11).
- Deployment evidence. A Microsoft partnership put a Teddy Roosevelt avatar in a replica Oval Office inside his presidential library, where visitors "walk in and… have a full interaction with Teddy Roosevelt in real time." Primas is explicit that this is "definitely not Turing test solved yet, but like a big milestone." President Trump attended the opening, was scheduled for "a quick minute, one interaction," and "actually stayed for 10 minutes," leaving and returning (01:41-03:59). The avatar is described as "full body… It has hands. It does actions. It does movement," all in real time (04:01-04:11).
- Why a visual layer at all. The argument is attention rather than fidelity: "humans struggle to focus and pay attention… a lot of people spend a bunch of time reading. And even voice it's hard to really lock in and understand what's happening… we're biologically wired to best understand things when there's a visual component as well." The bet: "in the long term… most interactions between AI and humans will have a visual layer. And we're building that visual layer" (04:23-05:00).
- Architecture bet. "What we do is we take these world models and we focus them on humans… even though it's harder to get the initial model working, it's harder to train the model, it's harder to deploy the model. Once you have a model, you get all of these nice emergent properties… full body movement… object interactions… movements in the scene, all the way down to the micro micro expressions as well and emotions. And all of those kind of don't come for free, but come more for free than when you use the other approaches that other people use" (05:02-06:05). He deflates the term himself: "people can call this a world model, but it's basically a video model that understands the physics of the world" (10:04-10:10).
- Product surface from the demo. Any single image becomes an avatar — "you can have photorealistic, you also can have Pixar, you also can have cartoons. It just literally takes a single image." Within the same video call and "the same kind of inference setup, we can easily change clothes, we can change the scene she's in." Physics is visible in the output: "there's physics on the earrings. They move. The water moves" (06:55-08:05).
- Commercial shape. "We're mostly the API layer. So, we provide an API. People bring their own LLM. People bring their own usually like voices. And then we're the visual layer on top of it." Named uses are language learning and AI sales calls, generalized as: "basically, anything today where you have a voice agent, you now also kind of a video agent if it's on your screen or on your computer" (08:14-08:47).
- Training the base model. "We get started with training our own video DiT model. The big thing here for us that really matters is the audio. The audio turns out to be very important for getting emotions right and the facial expressions right… So, we really focus on getting great audio data that we can train on. And then actually also getting the encoders very right. Like most audio encoders today are trained on basically audiobooks, which is very monotone, very simple, don't have a lot of emotions. So, if you want to have a very expressive model, you can't use those audio encoders and you actually have to spend a lot of time getting the audio embeddings right so that the video model is super expressive" (09:06-09:59).
- Making it interactive. "Usually video models are bidirectional, so they can look into the past, but they actually also can look into the future… and they basically generate videos all at the same time by looking at all of the latents that are being generated. For us, you can't do that. You only can look into the past… we basically train a model with an attention mask so that the model can only look into the past. So, when you do inference, it never can see the future because the future doesn't exist because you haven't given it those inputs yet. So, for example, it doesn't see audio in the future, it only sees what has been said in the past" (10:17-11:07).
- Making it real time. "Usually you spend like a bunch of steps denoising these video models. So, like let's say 30 steps… And what we need to do is go from like 30 steps, bring it out to one step. So you basically just in a single step go from like pure noise to a pure video" (11:11-11:44).
- Error accumulation, named as the hard part. "Since you only can look backwards, you're looking actually backwards at videos that you've previously generated, but each video block you generate has some error in it. So now you're looking in the past, you're looking at the error, you're adding more error to it, and then the error compounds over time" (12:08-12:40). He calls it "a problem anybody in real-time video generation and world models is familiar with" and illustrates it with a degrading clip (11:46-12:14).
- Session length is the design parameter. "Ideally these video models are endless. Like the Teddy avatar is generating continuously non-stop frame by frame for 8 hours straight with like no reset throughout the entire process. We have another one that's going to be generating for 16 hours straight. So it's a very hard problem to not have any error accumulation over long periods of time" (12:40-13:02). LemonSlice claims "a new way to solve this problem that is different to the best of our knowledge than what everybody else does today," which Primas will not describe; the claimed result is long videos with "no noticeable error accumulation" (13:05-13:27).
- Cost. "Usually when you generate an AI video, it's 5 seconds and you just share that. We have to generate minutes and hours of AI video and still have it be cost-effective… we've been able to make the models small enough and efficient enough so that the costs are about the same as a voice model. Which is crazy to me. Cuz think about a voice model, how much data is streamed there compared to a video model… it's much more pixel heavy in a video model than with voice. And the costs are about similar." The stated consequence: "this enables us to do consumer use cases. And a lot of our customers are consumer companies" (13:44-14:43). In Q&A he restates it as a price rather than a cost — "the cost of this is at the same level as an audio model in terms of what we charge for it" — adds that he was "very surprised at how inexpensive it is," and wants it cheaper still, both because consumer needs low costs and because "as the cost goes down we can do higher resolution which will help us a lot" (24:54-25:29).
- The model harness (captioned throughout as "model hardness"; the index record's description confirms "harness"). "I feel like the model harness is something that is often overlooked but is actually super important and super hard… I feel like a lot of our value actually in productizing this is in the model harness. And the way to think about it is you just have a bunch of separate threads… all of it is managing real data streaming through our system. And you have basically a bunch of stuff you do on a GPU and a bunch of stuff you do on a CPU. And you have to orchestrate this perfectly in a way that the video always remains real time. There is never any stutter that happens inside of the video. And this is especially hard when you have things like interrupts, you have queues, you're buffering data, you have to clean the queues. It's all like getting this orchestration right at production at scale has been a ton of work. And honestly, I feel like over time a lot more of the value of the things we build will be in figuring out the model harness. I think it's especially true for any real-time applications" (14:43-15:53).
- What is being built next. An "emotional engine… having them be more aware of what's actually happening inside of the conversation, and then be able to react… the right emotions at the right time for the right duration, and same for the actions." The current defect: "today when you interact with these avatars… it definitely still feels awkward. And I think a big part of that is they don't emotionally react to you. They're not listening to you. They're not emoting in the right [way]" (16:08-16:51).
- Capability versus controllability. Of the richer non-real-time next-generation model: "our model can already do this today. It has the capabilities to do this. It's just not controllable enough to make it real time with the conversation and not deterministic enough to be useful with the conversation. So a big part of our work here is making these actions actually controllable at the right time" (18:03-18:24). The mechanism: "we're building this emotion engine that is just literally predicting based on the audio input from the avatar and the text input for what the avatar is going to say what the action they should be doing at this moment in time," targeted to launch in "one to two months" (18:24-18:48).
- Long-term architecture. "There'll be a single model that is the EQ layer for AI… the model will literally take in directly the user video and the user audio… like you are in a FaceTime, and put out the avatar video and audio, and do all of that in an end-to-end model… Internally inside of the model it will do the audio understanding, generate what it's going to say, model its own internal state like own internal emotional state, and then based on all of that produce an output video and audio. And all of this will be trained in one model." He reports "early papers that are doing proofs of concepts around this" (19:08-20:12).
- The explicit EQ/IQ split. "What we're not saying is that this EQ model will be very intelligent. It'll have very high EQ and it'll be very good at interacting with people, but there'll be a separate model that will drive the IQ that will basically give this EQ model input to do all the magical things that AI can do today. Like do the tool calling, do the deep thinking, do all the intelligent stuff. But there will be an end-to-end layer up top." Timeline: "within two or three years you'll be seeing these kinds of end-to-end EQ models coming on the market" (20:13-20:58). Asked whether LemonSlice will build the IQ side: "not for us. That's not what we plan to do… you could imagine people using our technology, giving it the right context, having the right harness around it, and then having the nice IQ brain around it… but we don't want to solve that problem" (24:10-24:34).
- Internal state, today and later. "Today basically the way we think about actions and emotions is basically words. If you can describe it in words, you can generate those actions and emotions." Long term: "more of an internal state where the model will have an internal state in latent space that defines what the emotional state is or the goals are of the model and they will be more implicit. So, they won't be human interpretable necessarily," tracked "over some history of time" (21:31-22:22).
- Evaluation plan. Asked how the Turing test would actually be measured: "we haven't done this yet, but we're in the process of figuring out our own version of the Turing test for these avatars, which will just include real people. And we're planning to run that this year. And we won't pass it this year, but it'll just be cool for us to track it over time to see when we can actually solve the Turing test, and ideally allow other people to also go through the test as well… we've planned to create a test and then publish around the test" (23:06-23:45).
- Cost forecast. Two named vectors, "algorithm improvements and hardware improvements," with the observation that "things have been improving on all vectors faster than expected and we're just hoping that continues." One architectural prediction: "there'll also be very cool architectural updates to move to more of a token approach instead of a diffusion approach that will make this type of video generation way cheaper" (25:29-26:00).
