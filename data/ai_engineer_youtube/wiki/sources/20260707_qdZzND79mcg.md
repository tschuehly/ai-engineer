# Beyond the Harness: A Journey Towards Adaptive Engineering

Source: [Beyond the Harness: A Journey Towards Adaptative Engineering - Rajiv Chandegra, Annicha Labs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qdZzND79mcg)
Uploaded: 2026-07-07
Transcript: `raw/20260707_qdZzND79mcg/qdZzND79mcg.en-orig.vtt`

## Summary

Rajiv Chandegra (a practicing medical doctor in London and director of Annicha Labs, working on multi-agent/multi-human/multi-institutional collaboration) proposes a design philosophy he calls **adaptive engineering** and contrasts it with today's dominant paradigm of the **fixed (factory) harness**. In the current paradigm, an engineer picks or builds a harness — Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Pi, LangChain, Cline, Goose, Hermes — ahead of runtime; the harness (system prompts, `AGENTS.md`/`CLAUDE.md`, tools, roles, sequencing, memory, loops) is pre-engineered and remains fixed while agents run inside it. He frames this as "Taylorism for AI": a factory assembly line where each agent has one job, a fixed place, and a defined handoff, giving reliability, auditability, and linear traceable causality. That is exactly right for **complicated** problems (a clock, a jumbo jet) that are decomposable, knowable, and predictable, but wrong for **complex** problems (a flock of birds, a market, a human organization) whose whole cannot be derived from its parts — what Russell Ackoff called a "mess," a system of relationships in motion. Chandegra argues that as models get exponentially more capable and AI engineering leaves the sandbox to touch the messy, shifting real world, the fixed harness becomes brittle: reliability is bought by suppressing the variance novelty needs, models keep making yesterday's scaffolding obsolete, and every unanticipated situation forces a human to bolt on another rule until the harness is more complicated than the problem ("the factory method is the right answer to a fixed problem and the wrong answer to a moving problem"). Adaptive engineering instead lets the harness *emerge* from agent interactions at runtime — stabilize, adapt, and dissolve — so the engineer designs **constraints** (the rules of play) rather than structure, and the harness becomes the ongoing *output* rather than the *input*. He distinguishes **horizontal intelligence** (how groups of agents coordinate) from **vertical intelligence** (making individual agents smarter, e.g. Hermes learning skills from experience), betting horizontal coordination is the higher-leverage adaptive lever, and catalogs the failure modes: attractors that feel optimal but aren't, drift without genuine selection pressure, monoculture from shared training data, collapsing legibility, and no predictability ahead of runtime.

## Notes

- Current paradigm: the engineer's role today "is to use a harness and steer agents"; harnesses like Pi, Claude Code, Cursor, Codex remain fixed through runtime and are "largely constrained with fixed roles and tools and sequencing" that can be tweaked only before the process begins (00:54-01:30). The harness is "everything built around" the model-engine "to make that engine useful," and it is fully predetermined/pre-engineered before the run — "that's harness engineering" (04:16-05:03, 05:24-05:30).
- Harness types named: CLI coding (Claude Code, Codex, Pi), IDEs (Cursor), multi-agent orchestration (LangChain), and "Hermes as well, Cline and Goose"; they differ on configuration and, more importantly, on an opinionated design/engineering philosophy (05:34-06:17). Even a "minimalist and maximally extensible" harness like the Pi agent has everything predefined — roles, fixed outputs, sequencing protocols, how memory is created — and customization "occurs ahead of the engineering runtime… not mid-engineering" (06:30-07:04).
- Three payoffs of the fixed harness: reliable (same input → similar outputs), auditable (inspect exactly what changed and when), and linear causality (trace a break back to its source) — "like a factory line… Taylorism for AI… each agent has one job, a fixed place, a sequence, and maybe a defined handoff" (07:19-08:00). It "shines in these closed deterministic systems" with a well-defined product/problem (08:16-08:25).
- The trade-off: "that reliability isn't free… you buy it by suppressing variance that novelty requires"; "determinism and emergence pull in opposite directions" (08:31-08:52). There is "a hard ceiling on novelty" in the real world because reliability is predicated on suppressing variance, and models keep accelerating so "you can build a careful harness today… but it could be irrelevant in the next month" once the model no longer needs the scaffolding (09:16-09:56).
- Brittleness dilemma: "every situation you didn't anticipate needs a human to patch the harness. The more real world it needs, the more rules you bolt on and eventually the harness just becomes ever more complicated than the actual problem" (09:57-10:18). Takeaway: "the factory method is the right answer to a fixed problem and the wrong answer to a moving problem" (10:20-10:32).
- Two ways to see the world (philosophical core): a **reductionist/analytical** view (understand the whole by taking it apart; things are stable and change is incidental; relationships are secondary — "the metaphysics of a factory and of nearly all software") vs a **systems/relational** view (processes and relationships come first; "a stable thing really is just a slow pattern in an ongoing flow… more like a flame than a crystal") (10:58-12:46).
- **Emergence**: simple local rules between parts give rise to a new level of order no part designed and that can't be predicted from the parts. Examples: water's wetness (its oxygen/hydrogen aren't wet) and a flock of birds from three local rules — "align with your neighbor, don't crash into them, stay close." "No single bird has a flock in it. The flock lives in the relationships, not the parts." (12:50-14:25).
- Russell Ackoff: managers aren't handed neat separate problems but "dynamic situations, tangles of problems that keep changing," which he called a **mess** — "a system of relationships in motion." You can't decompose a mess into tidy boxes and solve each, which is exactly why a fixed-harness factory approach struggles against live real-world scenarios (14:57-15:45, 16:57-17:22 restated).
- **Complicated vs complex problems**: complicated = a jumbo jet or clock, passive parts, experts can take apart/analyze/plan/predict/document — hard but knowable and predictable. Complex = a flock, a market, a human organization; parts constantly interact and adapt, so the whole can't be derived from the parts; you "probe and sense and respond" instead of analyze and plan (15:56-16:59). "One of the most expensive mistakes… is that we treat a complex problem like a complicated one… things fail not due to lack of execution, but because there's a failure in categorizing the problem spaces" (17:00-17:22).
- Ingredients of a complex system: a **diverse** set of agents/actors ("diversity is the fuel"), **local interaction** (no agent sees the whole; each responds to its neighbor, like the bird), and **recursive learning/adaptation** (agents adapt, then adapt to the results of their own adapting, in a constant loop). Out of that local adaptive looping you get **emergence** — a novel pattern no single part designed. Systems settle into **attractors** (stable states, e.g. water at room temperature): constant local change plus recognizable stable whole-system patterns, and "nobody's steering here — it's self-organizing" (17:24-19:50).
- Definition of adaptive engineering: "the discipline of designing constraints to the extent that the harness emerges on its own, stabilizes, and adapts as needed in response to the changing environment in ways that you could not specify in advance… the harness becomes the ongoing output rather than the input" (20:53-21:56). "You don't build the harness anymore. You let the agents form the harness that best fits the environment in that moment… a self-organizing, constantly evolving multi-agent system" (22:37-22:57).
- **Emergent specialization**: start with isomorphic, undifferentiated but interacting/learning agents; coupling builds until "roughly one connection on average" per agent, and a whole emerges. The engineer's new lever is the **rate of coupling** — dial it up or dampen it. Environmental pressure rewards breaking ties: two agents doing the same thing in the same place are redundant, so a tiny difference (who got there first, who's slightly better) gets amplified into niches. "The agent's identity isn't something you gave it. It's the position, role, or capability it took relative to the others and its environment" — specialization emerges from interaction. Emergent clusters, boundaries, and conventions then form "governance without a governor," letting adaptation stay decentralized (23:04-25:52).
- The engineer's role is relocated, not abolished: exploit the model's capability to interact/learn/change, and "dictate the constraints, the rules of the game… giving the agent space to explore," then **sense and respond** to the emergent harness rather than stopping and starting from scratch (25:57-27:15, 31:57-32:15). Three constraint questions: enable the agents more vs govern/guardrail them; reward them to cohere toward a goal vs cost them for falling outside a container; and how fast/slow (e.g. the rate of coupling) (30:52-31:47).
- Fixed vs adaptive is a **continuum**, not binary or "better than," with two different use cases — not "a swarm of agents loose with no roles" and magically-appearing intelligence (27:17-28:18, 32:21-32:34).
- **Horizontal vs vertical intelligence**: Hermes AI advertises "a self-improving AI agent that creates skills from experience," but that is *vertical* intelligence — making individual agents smarter. Chandegra's thesis targets *horizontal* intelligence — how groups of agents coordinate — as "the most adaptive… most agile… higher-leverage point," and the two directions are orthogonal (28:22-29:15). Pi is "adaptive at the design stage" (customizable while preparing) but not adaptive at runtime; adaptive engineering is "adaptive during the runtime, where the system reorganizes itself whilst running" — Pi "doesn't make any claims" to be a self-organizing multi-agent system yet (29:20-30:22).
- An emergent harness can only be **sensed and responded to**, not hard-edited: trying to edit it "in some hard engineering fashion" isn't in the adaptive engineer's best interest (31:49-32:15). In the adaptive path, control is **decentralized** and continuous in the field because the problem space keeps changing (32:34-33:12).
- **Failure modes** of adaptive engineering: emergence leans toward stability (an **attractor** that "feels stable and optimal, but that doesn't necessarily mean it's the best"); you need **genuine selection pressure** or you just get **drift**; risk of **monoculture** (no real diversity because agents are "all trained on the same data"); **legibility collapses** as adaptability rises ("you can't really pin them down" — a feature of complex systems); and **no predictability ahead of runtime** because things keep moving (33:17-34:47).
- Closing thesis: as models improve exponentially and AI engineering moves into ongoing real-world "continual production," "the limiting factor… is not going to be the strength of the model. It's going to be the adaptability of the harness," and adaptability means decentralized multi-agent coordination that becomes adaptable *mid-runtime* rather than ahead of it (35:13-36:41).
