## Summary

A twenty-one-minute two-hander — Tisha Chawla frames the problem for the first ten minutes, Susheem Koul walks the design and a three-scenario live demo for the rest — proposing a cost control plane, "TokenOps," that sits at the *agent run* rather than the *model request*. The framing argument is that each software era grew a control surface and this one has not: SaaS had usage caps, seat limits and tier policies against a UI; cloud had autoprovisioning and autoscaling policies against pay-as-you-go; the agentic era prices in model calls, and "the part like where the code you know calls the model" has no control plane (01:30-02:57). Existing tools — "this light LLM, port key, cloudflare" — are named as acting "again at the request level," which cannot see the loop between an agent and its tools, sub-agent spawning from one main agent, or context growth (05:56-08:34). The design splits into a bridge (attribution, a `boundary` annotation, a governor, a `wrap_complete` helper) and a control plane holding segments, a ledger, budgets, actions and policies (11:12-15:56). The two contributions most worth carrying out of the talk are structural rather than product-specific: actions come in two flavors where **halt** is a circuit breaker and **steer** mutates or injects into the run to fit it inside the budget, and the benchmark is reported as a *pair* — average spend down "almost 78%" and completion up "from 67% to roughly 96%" against simple throttling — because throttling wins on spend alone by killing runs (14:43-15:19, 19:00-19:26). The demo's first scenario is a preview mode in which policies evaluate with enforcement off, presented as the way you would introduce this to a production agent (16:23-17:32).

## Provenance and Limits

This is a vendor talk about the presenters' own pre-release system, and nothing in it is independently measured. The two headline numbers use different baselines — the 78% spend reduction is against ungoverned runs, the 67%→96% completion figure is against "simple throttling" — and neither the throttling configuration, the absolute spend, the number of iterations, nor the task set is described beyond "browser use as well as metagp[t]," "multiple iterations," "stress tests," and "simple scenarios hard scenarios" (18:44-19:26). Most importantly, **completion is measured and output quality is not**: the steering action injects an instruction to make outputs "more succinct or more summarized," which is a quality intervention reported only through a completion counter, with no judge, rubric, or human grade anywhere in the talk. The RAG example that motivates the push-down channel — a retrieval tool returning 20 chunks where "the LLM is not even using the chunks that are after five" — is offered as an illustration, not as an observed workload. The closing self-learning module (a component reading the ledger to generate or refine policies) is explicitly roadmap. The Uber datum ("the AI budget for Uber getting exhausted within 4 months," 03:00-03:21) is relayed from news reports and corroborates the same figure already recorded in this wiki from an unrelated source. One transcription note: the instrumentation layer's unit is heard as "cost in microns," almost certainly micro-dollars.

## Notes

- The era framing is the cleanest statement of the gap: SaaS priced a UI and controlled it with "usage caps… seat limits or tier based policies"; cloud priced pay-as-you-go and controlled it with "autoprovisioning and you know autoscaling policies"; the agentic era prices model calls and has nothing at the layer where "the code you know calls the model." (01:30-02:57)
- Four first principles are stated before any product appears: the token is the unit of cost so value must also be counted in tokens; cost is created at the model-call boundary; without attribution ("what agent, what run made that particular call") you can see the bill but "can't trace it back or narrow it down"; and in-place policies should fire before a halt, with "a halting… from a budget cap" as "the last resort." (03:47-05:46)
- The stated reason a request-level tool cannot do this job: what needs controlling is "the loop between… the agent call, the tool and the agent," "the spawning of multiple sub agents happening from a one main agent," and "the growing of context" — none of which is visible in a single model request. LiteLLM, Portkey, and Cloudflare are named as doing halting and routing "at a request and you can't control the cost at the… request layer." (05:56-08:34)
- Enforcement is deliberately placed "in call path rather than… a separate thing," with in-place compaction or caching as the first response to runaway context and the budget cap only "after basically exhausting the list of all in place policies." (07:05-08:01)
- The out-of-band plane has three modules: instrumentation (OpenTelemetry telemetry, per-call cost, an enrichment layer, attribution), accounting (a ledger accumulating runs), and enforcement (steering through policies, plus halt). (08:57-10:19)
- The `boundary` annotation is framework-agnostic — "It doesn't matter what framework you're using… lang chain lang whatever" — and does two things: floats input and output up to the control plane as a ledger entry tagged with the agent run ID, and acts as "a channel through which the control plane can push actions down to the agent." "We do not have a single directional highway." A `wrap_complete` helper applies the same thing to provider objects rather than methods. (12:01-13:50)
- The governor is the safety bound on that channel: it "knows what actions are allowed on your agent by you as a developer," receives control-plane actions and applies them "in a non-destructive way," configured by passing your own configs at instantiation "so that your control plane cannot just willingly do any random things on your… agents." (13:22-13:34, 15:39-15:56)
- The control plane runs "in your own tenant. So you do not need to worry about any data leaks" — relevant because the boundary annotation is floating method inputs and outputs to it. (15:19-15:38)
- Segments are built from whatever dimensions the attribution layer emits — the worked example is a preview agent tagging `cohort = AIE 2026` so a budget can be set for the whole audience — which enables "rollups… fine grain or coarse grain control" instead of budgeting only per agent or per run. Budgets themselves are "static thresholds that work across a time window against a particular segment or an agent run," and policies bind budgets to actions against segments or runs. (13:51-15:19)
- The demo test bench is a two-agent workflow: a research agent with a search tool, "allowed to look up on the web as many times as it wants," handing findings to a summarizer that writes a research report. (15:57-16:19)
- Preview mode, scenario 1: "all the policies run as is, but the enforcement doesn't happen." The run completed, the dashboard showed the cost budget and cost guard policies having executed, and the actions attached to them "were not allowed to be executed." The stated purpose is to "firstly put it in your production environment, test the guardrails, tweak the guardrail, see what's the policies are doing and then finalize the thresholds." (16:23-17:32)
- Halt, scenario 2: with governance enforced, a cost cap "exceeded. So you had a budget allotted for this run but the agent exceeded the budget and it was killed immediately… the simple circuit breaker sort of a methodology." (17:32-17:49)
- Steer, scenario 3: a larger but still insufficient budget triggers the cost guard, which "takes into account two things. First how much of your allotted budget have you consumed? Second what is the velocity at which you're consuming tokens," and on a predicted overrun "it's going to inject something into your system instructions… as simple as hey you're running out of budget so make sure that the LM outputs are more succinct." (17:49-18:39)
- The policy catalog spans spend management, context management (context compaction, tool-output reduction), and loop and progress detection, "researched [from] what are the different failure modes that are there today out in the wild." Steer actions are "allow, mutate, inject and so on"; halt is "a simple kill." (19:26-19:58)
- Roadmap, not shipped: a self-learning module inside the control plane that reads the ledger, asks "what is the failure mode that I'm still not able to catch," and either generates new policies on the fly or refines the parameters of existing ones. (20:03-20:46)
