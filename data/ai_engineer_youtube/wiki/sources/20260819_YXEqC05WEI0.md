## Summary

Rashi Agrawal, who leads AI/ML at Hinge Health, argues that most healthcare AI safety failures are architectural decisions made before a single token is generated, and gives the practitioner's version of what that means for member-facing health AI. The first half is three layers: strip PHI at the pipeline boundary so the reactive redaction problem never exists, put must-not-fail behavior in a code layer that runs *above* and *before* the model on every turn (emergency escalation, intent routing, identity verification), and run safety as a continuous judge layer over live traffic rather than a pre-launch gate. The argument for the code layer is borrowed from the frontier labs themselves: they publish an authority hierarchy in which every layer above the user is one prompt injection from being overridden, so "if the labs themselves don't trust the prompt as a security boundary, neither should you." The second half is what happens when the architecture is not enough and a human has to choose between shipping and holding — five rules for deciding when five stakeholders see five different risks, plus one discipline that comes before all of them: in a non-deterministic system the judge is non-deterministic too, so when a production score drops, first ask whether the judge is right.

## Notes

- **The production baseline, not the frontier.** "40 million people actually use these models for triaging their health care issues." Three cited data points frame the risk: a healthy 60-year-old who asked a popular AI assistant how to cut salt from his diet, was told to swap in sodium bromide, did it for three months, and landed in the ER with paranoia and hallucinations at "bromide levels 200 times the safe limit" and three weeks in hospital; the first independent safety test of a consumer health AI, out of Mount Sinai, finding it "under triaging life-threatening emergency 50% of the times" — diabetic ketoacidosis, respiratory failure — telling people "to go see a doctor in a day or two" when "the right answer was ER right now"; and ECRI, "the patient safety group that hospitals trust to rank their top risks," naming AI chatbot misuse the number one health technology hazard of 2026. Her conclusion: "This is not really a frontier problem. This is the production baseline that we're working with right now." 00:39-02:27
- **Three non-negotiable foundations.** (1) "The constraint is the architecture" — "most AI safety failures in health care are not model failures. They are architectural decisions that were made before even a single token was generated." (2) "Deterministic rules belong above the model, not inside it" — "what can never be wrong cannot be left to probability." (3) "Safety is a continuous evaluation layer, not a one-time gate" — "launch of your product is where the real risk starts, not where it ends." 02:39-03:20
- **Layer one, PHI.** "Protecting PHI takes both policy and architecture. Policy tells you what to protect and architecture makes sure that it actually happens." Most teams treat PHI as a runtime problem — "something to redact when a log gets written to a dashboard. That's the reactive version." The architecture version "strips PHI at the pipeline boundary. At ingestion, before it ever reaches the data lake… so a developer opens a dashboard, there's nothing to redact. The PHI was never there." 03:49-04:26
- Production and non-production "stay completely separate. No pipes in between, because even a single pipe is all that it takes for member data to leak into a dev environment." Access depends on two things, "your role and your geographic region" — teams are geographically distributed, PHI access is "a certification, a policy that is applied to specific regions only," and "an engineer outside the regulated region cannot reach raw PHI at all." 04:28-05:15
- Compliance rules — HIPAA, the FDA's good machine learning practice, and state laws (Texas's, rendered by the captions as "Triaga") — "are not afterthoughts. They are the grounding input in how you actually design your systems. You cannot slap on HIPAA on top of an underlying system or an architecture. You start with it and let the architecture grow around it." The payoff is stated as a capability property: "you're not just trusting that the policies will get followed. You're actually relying on a system that's incapable of certain failures." 05:18-05:53
- **Layer two, the code layer.** "Probabilistic systems are great at generation… however, they are unreliable for things that can never be wrong. So the rule is very simple: must-not-fail behavior belongs above your prompt, above the model." Above the prompt means "there is a code layer that runs first, on every turn, before the model even runs. The code layer is what makes your irreversible decisions… whether this is an emergency escalation, should they be routed to 911, should a clinician step into the loop." The model "handles the long tail of your conversations." The picture: "code on top, model below. Every turn goes through the code layer first. Most turns do reach the model, but the model never gets a vote on high stake calls." 05:56-07:13
- **The authority-hierarchy argument.** "A model is not a guardrail. A model with a system prompt is also not a guardrail. Code that runs above the model is closer." "Even the labs that build these frontier models publish the authority hierarchy: root, system, developer, user, guideline. Every layer above user is one prompt injection away from being overridden. If the labs themselves don't trust the prompt as a security boundary, neither should you." 07:13-07:49
- **Three things that live in the code layer.** *Emergency escalation* — if a member mentions self-harm, suicidal ideation, or an acute medical emergency, "the system must route to 911 or 988. The model should not even see this turn." *Intent routing* — which capability in the multi-agent architecture owns a turn (clinical, tech support, education from accredited articles, exercise recommendation); "the model can help to classify, but high-stakes paths must again take a deterministic route," because "a clinical question quietly being routed to your generic tech support agent… that's unrecoverable." *Identity verification* — "anything that touches member data has to check that the right member is at the other end. That's an authentication check. And authentication is a security boundary. Prompts are not." The shared pattern: "code runs first, code makes the irreversible decisions, the model handles what's left." 07:52-09:33
- **Layer three, continuous evaluation.** "Most teams treat evals as a pre-launch checklist… that's necessary, of course, but that's hardly enough. What actually holds up in production is judges that continuously keep scoring real conversations as they happen. Not a saved golden data set. Live traffic." 09:35-10:14
- Three signal sources, each catching something different: automated judges ("30, 40, name it, as much as you can scale") on many always-refreshing dimensions — clinical accuracy, safety, escalation, relevance, drift, refusal — to catch regressions and "even sensitive drops in quality"; member feedback, thumbs up/down "on each and every single message," described as "your gold mine of information… the truth signal… the only one that comes straight from the person that you're serving it to," catching tone problems that judges miss; and sample traces, "random samples spread across capabilities with high-stake cases checked every single time, 100% sampling on those." 10:16-11:25
- Two consequences. First, people have to read the signals — "people are going to catch what no single metric is going to catch" — and "the bottleneck is not the compute, the models, the capability. It's actually having enough people to read the signal and act on it." Second, some failures resist prompt fixes: "you ship the fix, it comes back under new conditions. New prompts, new tools, the model shifts. You ship the fix again. Each round buys you less and less. The rate never hits zero." So "monitoring is not a last resort. It is the first resort, which is always on," and "a new failure that you see in production simply means you now have a new judge" — the architecture has to keep scaling with new judges as the consumer base scales. "Monitoring is how you know that the architecture is still holding." 11:25-12:37
- **The decisioning problem.** One issue left on the board five days before launch, and five stakeholders each see a different risk: clinical sees member safety risk and wants to hold; legal sees regulatory exposure; compliance sees audit risk; product sees adoption risk (a broken feature "won't land"); engineering sees velocity risk (can't fix without slipping the date). "Five rational people, five different risks, and five very different fixes." 12:55-13:54
- **Rule one, worst case always wins.** "Severity is set by the worst plausible outcome, not the average… A bug that lightly annoys 100% of users is way less severe than one that could cause serious harm in 0.1% of cases." So when triaging, "don't ask how often does this happen. Ask, what's the worst version of this? That sets the severity." 14:12-14:54
- **Rule two, severity is not capacity.** "This one keeps politics out of it." A bug's severity "comes from the harm that it causes. Not who owns it, not whether your team has the capacity to fix it, not how hard the fix is." Three legitimate options: "fix, delay the launch, or accept the risk with explicit sign-off. Those are the three. You never quietly downgrade a bug just because you can't get to it." 14:54-15:37
- **Rule three, asymmetric default.** "When you don't know what to do, always pick the safer mistake," and the default flips by bug class. For safety bugs "the math is one-sided. Shipping a real safety bug is much worse than delaying for a false alarm," so hold and fix. For polish bugs "the math runs the other way. Delaying a launch costs more than shipping a small flaw," so ship. "The framework doesn't decide for you. It just tells you which way to lean." 15:37-16:28
- **Rule four, revealed risk tolerance, not stated.** "Your launch bar is what your org already accepts in production, not what it says it will accept." If a behavior "has been live in your existing product for weeks, months, without escalation, without member complaints, without leadership concern, you cannot call it a launch blocker just for a new thing." "Your stated risk tolerance might be no bugs in production, but your revealed risk tolerance is what's actually shipping today. Calibrate to the revealed one. That's the floor." 16:28-17:12
- **Rule five, humans are the constraint.** "Judges scale, pattern interpretation doesn't. Always design for human in the loop." Judges score traces automatically and "dashboards refresh every few hours. None of that is hard anymore. But what's hard is having enough people to read the signal and act on it." Attached to it: "fast follows are committed debt, not an optional backlog. If you didn't ship it at launch, it's not a wish list item. It's already committed." 17:14-17:52
- **Verify the scorer before you trust the score.** The five rules "all assume one thing, that your underlying signal is true," and "in a non-deterministic system, the judge is also non-deterministic." The worked case: a clinical accuracy judge steady at 4.9 for weeks drops to 4.5 and stays at 4.5 the next day. "The immediate instinct is, let's start changing the prompts. The agent is broken… That's reactive, and it's risky. You fix one thing and you break another. Worse, you're changing the agent based on a signal that might not be true." 17:55-18:56
- The side-by-side that separates the two cases. Scenario A: a member asks about caffeine, the agent gives FDA standard guidance (400 mg for most adults, less if pregnant or on certain medications), and the judge flags a hallucination "because the agent mentioned pregnancy and medications without checking. But that's just clinical context. The judge is over calling in this case. Fix the judge." Scenario B: same question, the agent says "1,000 mg a day is fine," which is well above the safety limits, the judge correctly flags it, "and the agent is wrong. In this case, fix the agent." The rule: "always ask, is the judge right, before changing the agent's response," and "fixing a judge prompt is not cheating. Judges are software, too. And they need to continuously evolve." 19:02-20:14
- **The whole talk in one slide.** Architecture side, phrased as "don't X what you can Y": "don't policy what you can architect; don't prompt what you can code; don't gate what you can monitor." Decisioning side: "score by the worst case and default to the safer mistake; calibrate to your org; always design for the human in the loop; fast follows are debt, not backlog." 20:18-21:00
- **The cost is acknowledged, not hidden.** "Building guardrails first is slower than bolting them on later. But that's the design, not limitation. We are not building a generic low-stakes chatbot. We are building a system that has to be worthy of someone's health. The architecture is how, the decisioning is when, and member trust is why." 21:03-21:26
