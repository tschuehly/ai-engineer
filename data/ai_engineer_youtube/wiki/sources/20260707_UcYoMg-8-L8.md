# 500 people vibe-coded for 30 days. I was one of them.

Source: [500 people vibe-coded for 30 days. I was one of them. - Sanja Grbic, Automattic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8)

## Summary

Sanja Grbic, a product designer at Automattic (the ~1,400-person, fully-distributed and asynchronous company behind WordPress.com, Jetpack, WooCommerce, Tumblr, and Beeper), reports on "Radical Speed Month," a 30-day org experiment: pause the roadmap, pair with a partner, and ship something real, with AI encouraged but not required. About a third of the company (~500 people) ran the first wave and started ~794 projects. She frames the sprint not as a tooling story but as an organizational-behavior-change intervention that only worked because of pre-existing scaffolding: a role-specific two-week immersive AI enablement course, a systems/operations team that built security, processes, and documentation good enough that a non-engineer can spin up a working dev environment, and an internal knowledge MCP server ("Context") that gives AI tools access to decades of documented company knowledge. She traces her own arc across three projects — a 2-hour board-game session manager (four people), a 2.5-week solo design-system status tracker, and a 6-day two-designer iOS WooCommerce merchant-chat proof of concept — to extract three durable lessons: on mixed-ability AI teams the specialist's highest-leverage move is to enable and teach (engineers shift from builders to enablers); coding agents let a designer iterate on a running coded prototype and demote Figma to late visual fine-tuning, turning a designer into a "design engineer" who owns the whole plan-build-deploy loop; and shifting process in a large org means shifting human behavior via access, enablers/champions, space to experiment, and agency to break habits.

## Notes

- Automattic is ~1,400 people, fully distributed and asynchronous, so "most of our work, our thoughts, our concepts from the past couple of decades are very well documented" — which is why an internal knowledge MCP server is useful. (01:20-01:55, 04:05-04:40)
- AI-adoption ladder that preceded the sprint: access to tools and courses → an "AI enablement" program where every employee takes a two-week role-specific immersive course with lectures and hands-on time → encouragement to contribute in code → a systems/operations team providing security, processes, and documentation good enough that a non-engineer can spin up a working dev environment → "Context" (an MCP server exposing all documented company knowledge/data to AI tools) used daily for research and planning. (03:20-04:40)
- Radical Speed Month rules: pause roadmap work for 30 days, pair with a partner, ship something real; AI welcome but not required; run in waves; first wave ≈ 500 people (a third of the company) starting ≈ 794 projects. (03:56-04:48)
- Project 1 — board-game session manager, built in 2 hours by four people (two designers weak in code, an engineer, a product lead) during the in-person enablement course: create/view/join/manage board-game sessions, start/stop a session, see available games/spots, chat; 16-bit office illustration made with Nano Banana; built with Cloud Code. The engineer set up the GitHub project and taught Git/versioning so everyone could commit; the speaker "thought she understood Git but didn't." Low-risk throwaway (unrelated to real products) kept the focus on learning. Insight: enabling others can beat doing more engineering — "engineers will need to become enablers and teachers." (06:04-09:40)
- Project 2 — design-system status tracker, a solo 2.5-week proof of concept (discovery was three people; design and build was just her): pulls links from the GitHub repo, Storybook, and Figma; sorts them by status; tags them with the correct library names; live component previews; search. Built the prototype first, used Figma only later for visual fine-tuning (hand the agent an image when something can't be explained in words). Deployed as an internal tool and as a curated external public-facing site. This was where she moved from designer to "design engineer," enabled less by AI than by owning the entire process with agency and no negotiation/handover — "hard to do in large organizations." (09:43-13:20)
- Project 3 — iOS chat for WooCommerce merchants, 0→working proof of concept in 6 days by two designers: wordpress.com authentication, a Jetpack connection, a site-theming-inheriting visitor widget, and a "fully capable AI agent" that scans the site for information, answers visitors' questions in real time, and discerns whether it can answer a question or not. Two designers aligned quickly because they "always have the users in mind." (13:27-14:35)
- Process practice: they ran the exploration "as a Cloud Code project folder where all of the chats and ideas were recorded into a file system," which "significantly sped up collaboration as well as build" — a practice she has continued in daily work. The prototype (not Figma) was the main artifact; the old process was to start in Figma and stay until very high fidelity, the new one is plan → build the prototype/product → return to Figma later for a mood board and UI fine-tuning. (14:56-16:07)
- Org-change recap: the engineer's role can be elevated from builder to enabler; pushing code to production in a large established system needs serious onboarding and security practice; all the company initiatives together "gave me the space I needed to experiment and find my own process." To implement even at team level in a large org, you must shift the human behavior behind tools/process: provide access to new tools, find enablers and champions, create space for experimentation, and give people agency to break out of their habits. (16:08-17:42)
