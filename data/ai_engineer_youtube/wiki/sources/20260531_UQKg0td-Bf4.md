# Spec-Driven Testing for Agents With A Brain the Size of A Planet

## Summary

Steven Willmott (CEO, Safe Intelligence) argues that "specifying what an agent is supposed to do" is more than an eval dataset, and that a bigger/smarter model is not automatically a safer or better agent. Poem-wrapped jailbreaks illustrate the safety inversion: a small model cannot decode the poem and so cannot act on the hidden instruction, while a large model understands it and executes the bad instruction — so for automated deployment there is a real smart-vs-safe tradeoff, and you want a model good enough to perform but not capable of arbitrary harm. He frames "arbitrary harm" as two surfaces: what instructions/prompts the agent can receive, and what tools/tasks it can carry out in your infrastructure (answering questions is far lower risk than wiring millions of dollars). His positive method is spec-driven validation: an agent spec captures ground-truth examples plus explicit rules (never discount >10%, no refunds after 30 days), domain ontologies/dictionaries (an airline only flies certain destinations; gross profit vs gross sales are not substitutable), rights and roles (logged-in vs out, permissions), and robustness requirements (how many typos or rephrasings before it fails — the agent analogue of detecting a runway under fog or camera shake in vision). The spec drives both security testing (you know the domains it will engage and where it has power to act, so you know where it is most vulnerable) and robustness testing (vary inputs to measure its valid range). Specs should be written independently of the implementation so they survive a model or framework swap, versioned in a GitHub repo like an OpenAPI spec, and pulled into whatever tool runs the integration/unit/penetration tests — closing a "backyard RL" loop that iterates the agent (not the model) to fill robustness gaps.
