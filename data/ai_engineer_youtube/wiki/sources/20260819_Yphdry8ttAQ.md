## Summary

Ayush Bhardwaj did applied AI at a hedge fund and now does it at a pharma-tech startup, and reports that despite opposite operating cultures — "fast and mostly right" versus "absolutely right, you can take a week more" — the applied-AI job was identical. He abstracts it into seven steps and locates the failure point in the middle of them: the first four (narrow problem, proprietary data, prompt modeled on the person, observability) "fit one screen" and are not the moat, and the fifth, iteration, is where vertical AI projects quietly die, because an engineering team can instantly judge generated code but has no equivalent instinct for a trade thesis or a drug candidate. LLM-as-judge does not rescue this — it "jargons its way out" without knowing what alpha means, and RL from verifiable rewards works for math and code only because answer keys exist. Worse, the reasoning data is structurally absent: funds hide holdings because disclosure erodes returns, roughly 30% of firms never disclose clinical trials despite the legal requirement, and so the frontier labs do not have it either. His answer is to hire the user — a senior scientist onto a team of young engineers — and build a learning loop where that expert curates sources, refines prompts, encodes their decision procedure, and does the judging, climbing a cost-ordered ladder that starts at error analysis. Production is not the bar; ROI is. And the deployed shape is AI-in-the-loop, not human-in-the-loop: the AI proposes candidates, the expert still decides.

## Extracted Concepts

- [You Cannot Iterate on Output You Cannot Judge](../concepts/you-cannot-iterate-on-output-you-cannot-judge.md) - the team's build capability arrives long before its ability to tell whether the build is good, and an LLM judge cannot substitute in a domain with no answer key.
- [High-Value Vertical Data Is Withheld by Design](../concepts/high-value-vertical-data-is-withheld-by-design.md) - in finance and pharma the reasoning data is deliberately not published, so no frontier model has it and none can be bought into it.
- [Hire the User to Own the Judgment Loop](../concepts/hire-the-user-to-own-the-judgment-loop.md) - the domain expert becomes staff, then curates data, refines prompts, encodes the decision procedure, and judges, on a cost-ordered ladder starting at error analysis.
- [Keep the Expert as Decider With AI in Their Loop](../concepts/keep-the-expert-as-decider-with-ai-in-their-loop.md) - in irreversible high-stakes verticals the AI generates candidates and compresses expert time; the expert still picks.

## Notes

- Speaker background: applied AI for a hedge fund, now "everything tech plus applied AI" for a pharma-tech startup (Allos, rendered "Elos" in captions), 00:15-00:28.
- Definition: applied vertical AI is "applied AI but built for one very specific industry," whose aim "is to simulate a job of a person in that particular industry." Google Translate is the general-purpose contrast; "we specifically build drugs with AI" is the vertical case; legal tech and prop tech (real-estate tech) are named as other instances, 01:16-01:57.
- The two industries' cultures are opposites — a hedge fund is "you need to do it fast and mostly right. It does not matter if we lose at one paradigm as long as we are overall winning," a pharma firm is "we have to be absolutely right. You can take a week more" — yet "the core part of my job, applied AI, remained the exact same," a pattern he confirmed by talking to people in legal AI and prop tech, 02:00-03:03.
- He rejects the framing "are people actually putting agents into production?" as "such a wrong question," since "everyone is putting agents into production." The question is "whether they actually work, whether they actually make or save money, whether they justify their ROI," 03:12-03:38.
- Step 1, formulate the problem: "pick a very narrow task." The bad prompt is "fetch me top three market opportunities that I could invest in"; the good one picks a market (US equities), an industry (IT), and a ranking parameter (capital expenditure, AI investment). "Last I checked, there was no tax on building more AI agents. So why do you want your single agent to do everything?" The pharma version is the same: break the process into steps and ask pointed questions, 03:56-05:05.
- Step 2, identify the data: everyone has news, sell-side reports from JP Morgan and Morgan Stanley, arXiv, PubChem, research papers — "what actually makes your application better than let's say ChatGPT or Claude? It is your proprietary data." It is "really expensive to buy, and most people won't sell it to you," so curate it yourself: an organization three years old already has it, just unstructured, and "an LLM workflow could do it overnight," 05:06-05:57.
- The named proprietary assets: in finance, the trade thesis — "what trade work and why it worked"; in pharma, the data for *failed* experiments, since successful-experiment data is obtainable and failure data is not, 05:58-06:18.
- Step 3, model the problem: write the prompt "after the person who you are trying to replace" — "encode how a person would solve this job into multiple steps," i.e. transcribe a mental model. He adds the caveat that "we're not trying to replace anyone with AI, but that's the ideology behind writing prompts," 06:23-06:48.
- Step 4, observability: "if you can't see it, you can fix it" (spoken; the intended sense is that you cannot fix what you cannot see) — traces to understand and debug what the application is doing, 06:51-07:10.
- "All of this was the easy part… All of this fits one screen. The mythical 10x engineers can do this stuff in minutes… that's why it's not the moat. Of course, except your proprietary data," 07:11-07:29.
- Step 5 is iteration, and it is where he hit the wall: "I could build it, but I just could not tell if it worked cuz I'm not a trader. I'm not someone who has a PhD in biology or chemistry. I just don't understand what the model is saying, what the output of my AI agent is," 07:44-08:12.
- The engineer's analogy for the missing instinct: "you can instantly tell that Sonnet 5 sucks because you have your own training… you've been trained for this for life. You have a mental model to judge these things." The same mental model does not exist for a trade thesis, 08:12-08:38.
- "This is also the place where a lot of vertical AI projects quietly die because on the surface it looks like you have made it, you have built it, let's put this into production and start selling it. But no one would buy it the same way you won't use an inferior coding model," 08:43-08:58.
- His first attempt, and his verdict on it: "I thought I could LLM as a judge my way out of it… this was a really, really stupid mistake." Mechanism: the model is "predicting the next probable word… it's just like jargoning its way out. It does not understand what alpha means. It does not understand how to actually create value unless you have taught it some way. Whereas a human can just tell it instantly," 09:00-09:35.
- Why self-verification fails here: "reinforcement learning via verifiable rewards is really good at math and code because you have answer keys, you can verify your code is compiling or not… but in these fields, there is just no way to model it. And if any error gets in, it just compounds," attributed as consistent with Yann LeCun's view, 09:42-10:10.
- Finance withholding: any institutional manager holding over $100 million in qualifying US equities must publicly file long holdings every quarter, and once a fund does, returns decrease "because everyone just sees those, reverse engineers, and takes away their moat," 10:27-10:47.
- Pharma withholding: disclosure of "every clinical trial, pass or failure" is required by law, but "30% of the funds, which is nearly 1/3 of firms, never do," and in 2026 the FDA "had to publicly remind… about 2,000 sponsors" that withholding unfavorable results does an injustice, 10:48-11:14.
- Why it matters for models: this is "the exact data which helps your LLM actually reason through these complex and niche industries. And they hide it because for them, it's like a chicken laying golden eggs. Why would they sell their chicken? So naturally, neither OpenAI nor Anthropic has this data because it's gatekeeper," 11:14-11:33.
- Why annotation cannot fill the gap: "you just cannot hire a trader for $100 an hour and have them annotate that stuff because there's lots of NDAs and they definitely earn more," 11:33-11:41.
- The answer: "you hire the person who you want to sell it to cuz there is, to be honest, no other way around. I have tried a lot of stuff. You just need to hire the user," 11:53-12:06.
- At the hedge fund the user was already there — "the user was kind of like my boss, the trader. We worked together." At the pharma-tech startup, "we were like a bunch of young engineers and we were like, oh, we need a [senior] scientist in our company to tell us what to do? Yeah, I guess we do." The caption renders the seniority as "a 20-year-old scientist," which the video description resolves as hiring a senior scientist, 12:06-12:25.
- The observed effect: "that someone actually changed the trajectory of our tools. Our tools started making sense. When we pitched to the other pharma companies, the big ones… they started liking our tools because it kind of spoke their language versus the normal jargonish LLM language," 12:25-12:41.
- The learning loop the expert runs, from the ground up: think about prompts ("let's not ask the LLM to do this, let's ask a very specific query"); curate data, because "a pharma expert or a trader knows which sources are more reliable than the other," analogous to an engineer knowing which conferences and paper venues are credible and who is a leader versus an influencer; build thinking models — "if you follow five steps to solve a problem, you just cannot do it in any random order. There has to be a logical flow" — so they decompose a problem, gradually refine, "and then finally judge." The summary line: the person "who has lived through the complete of the industry… their judgment is now turning into agents," 12:42-13:58.
- The ladder of methods for capturing that judgment, each of which "could have been an hour-long session on its own": supervised fine-tuning, where "model mimics human demonstrations"; RLHF, where "human preferences train a reward model"; rubrics as a reward, which he calls "reinforcement learning from AI feedback" — a human writes a rubric and the AI grades itself against it, with the caveat that "there is a slight chance that you might run into an echo chamber with rubrics as rewards"; and error analysis, "the cheapest of all, and I think the highest ROI," which reads the logs from the observability step, finds where the model goes wrong, and corrects it with no weights touched. Climb gradually, "and probably later on go to the ultimate reinforcement learning from human feedback cuz that's… kind of the golden standard these days," 13:58-15:32.
- The fine-tuning pitfall is a treadmill: fine-tune GLM 5.2 and then "Alibaba Cloud or let's say DeepSeek will release a newer model, then you have to fine-tune that too as well. So there is a cost. It's not cheap," 15:33-15:48.
- The loop compounds: hire one user, then more; they ask more queries, the scoping and data grow, "at this point you're kind of generating your own data… that exercise itself is generating a crazy data set of what works and what does not work. And this loop never stops." Ship when confident, to external paying users, 15:49-16:21.
- The Stanford AI Index stat he cites is that 89% of enterprise AI agents never reach production; he disagrees with the framing: "Every AI reaches production, but it just fails to work or justify its own cost." In finance and pharma, "if it does not make money, it's shown the door… It has to instantly make money," with no tolerance for "maybe it'll work in 2 years," 16:22-17:05.
- The seven steps as summarized: formulate the problem statement, source your data, define the prompts, observe how the tool performs, *do not iterate yet*, hire the user, let the users form an endless learning loop — and ship when it delivers "that alpha over let's say Claude and ChatGPT," 17:06-17:42.
- On human-in-the-loop: "I would say not yet. Finance and pharma are still those two industries where it's AITL, AI in the loop cuz everything is done by the expert, but the AI assistant really helps save time." Concrete shape: forming trade theses takes a trader X amount of time, "and AI can just give him five candidate trade theses, but which one would actually work in the market and which won't is the discussion [that] still lies with the trader." Same for picking drug candidates. "You just reduce the time of expert by a lot… and it will stay this way for really long," 17:45-18:31.
- His stated blocker for changing that: "for the models to actually make good decisions, they don't need to do correlation, they need to do causation," and as LeCun puts it "these are text statistics, not real-world models. You cannot just pattern match with past and use future to predict." He calls the crossing point "the AGI line" — past it, "you will have vibe coded drugs," 18:32-19:02.
- Final takeaway: "Model, infra, ecosystem, everyone selling you tons of stuff at this conference is just commodity. Everyone has it… But what is moat and no one will come and sell it to you, you will have to curate it on your own, is the domain expertise. You need your data. You need other people's data. That is just not out there on the internet," 19:03-19:34.
