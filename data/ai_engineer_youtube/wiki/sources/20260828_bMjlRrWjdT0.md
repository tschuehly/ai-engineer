## Summary

Two engineers from DoorDash's GenAI platform team — a horizontal team whose stated value is helping product teams "balance these three forces which is accuracy, latency and cost" — describe how eval became their fourth pillar next to an LLM gateway, an agent gateway, and open-weights model hosting. The trigger was that the teams asking for evals wanted incompatible things: the consumer discovery and shopping assistant team needed "session level quality judgments," personalization ML needed "a way to scale up human judgment," and multi-agent systems needed "trajectory based evals." The question they set themselves is the one the rest of the talk answers: "how do you cater to all these different needs under a common platform."

The answer starts with who the users are. "We needed to empower the people who are the domain experts, and in our case that was strategy and operations folks, it was product managers, it was even labeling partners, and not only engineers." That produced a three-stage progression in the platform's primary surface — UI first (a decision credited to co-founder Andy Fang), then API first "so that engineers can also build and not be blocked on the central platform," then workflow first, because "with the coding agents now we have become workflow first where we kind of empower S&O and PMs to also being able to navigate the platform and run operations as well." Underneath, the platform is two surfaces: a telemetry layer holding traces, scores, and observations, reachable "using an MCP, using an SDK, using our APIs," and a workflow layer "where a lot of our strat ops, our product teams operate on the platform" — setting annotation tasks, reviewing golden datasets, creating judges, calibrating judges. The loop those surfaces serve is deliberately plain: trace, sample down "to a very small set that you actually want to look at," annotate with domain expertise, review, promote a golden dataset, calibrate against it, monitor over time, repeat.

The most transferable decision is what the platform team stopped doing. Annotation workflows varied so much across use cases — grading restaurant menus, image annotation, manual testing — that "it's almost hard for a platform team to build like a UI specific for each use case." Instead of a configurable generic screen, they doubled down on the API layer and let the operators generate their own interfaces: "because we had these APIs we were actually able to enable our stat ops teams to use something like a Codex or a Claude Code and vibe code their own annotation UIs." The precondition is stated explicitly and is the part worth carrying: "what stood out to us was the underlying patterns were similar. So if we are API first we can actually enable our partners to simply vibe code these UIs for annotation." The variation was in the presentation, not the data model, and the APIs were "very stable APIs that our team owns," powering SDK and MCP access from "this single plane."

Judge calibration got the opposite treatment — a first-party self-serve UI, built after the API-first move. The loop is a judge prompt, baseline scores from running the judge across traces, and an optimizer (the caption-garbled "JPEA library," described as "a pretty commonly used library out there for prompt optimization") iterating against a golden dataset; when partner teams are satisfied "they're going to then elevate that judge prompt as their LLM as a judge." Two design choices carry the weight. It is self-serve because "what we wanted to do was really reduce the friction of back and forth with an engineering team," so a PM or operator sets the configs and runs the loop on any model they choose. And it is reviewable because "a lot of this is a closed box… it's hard to see what's actually happening," so the surface shows "the original system prompt and the calibrated prompt to our partners so that they are also able to gain that trust." The talk's one org-design observation follows from this: the judge prompt is owned by strategy-and-operations in some teams, the PM in others, engineering in others, and the platform enables all three "because we are all learning. So even the org design is improving and we are enabling that." The reported outcome is unquantified — "a lot of reduction in the spend at per annotation cost," against "thousands of rows that need to get annotated every week," plus faster loops and self-serve judge calibration.

## Provenance and Limits

- This is a first-party retrospective by the platform team about its own platform, presented at a conference. Nothing in it is independently evaluated, and no counterfactual is offered for any of the three sequencing decisions.
- **The talk contains no measurements.** The only quantity stated anywhere is "thousands of rows that need to get annotated every week." The headline outcome — "we actually did see a lot of reduction in the spend at per annotation cost" — has no figure, no baseline, no period, and no comparison. The video description's stronger phrasing ("per annotation cost fell sharply") is not supported by any number in the transcript either.
- The judge-calibration result is narrated over a screenshot as "one of the good examples where we saw like a significant amount of improvement in the judge prompt." No score, accuracy delta, or before/after is reported, and the example is explicitly selected as a good one.
- No adoption figures accompany the vibe-coded annotation UIs: no count of how many were built, by whom, whether any were rebuilt after an API change, who maintains them, or what fraction of annotation work runs through them.
- The claim that varied judge-prompt ownership reflects an org still learning is an interpretation, not a finding. No team is reported to have changed owners as a result of what it learned, which is the observation that would separate an experiment from a durable inconsistency.
- "Workflow first," the third and most novel stage of the platform's progression, is described in about fifteen seconds with no artifact behind it beyond the calibration UI and the operator-built annotation apps.
- The talk is a platform pitch to internal partner teams as much as a conference talk; the recurring framing that partner teams "are happy" and "aren't always necessarily blocked by our team" is the platform team's own account of its customers' experience.
- Speaker attribution: Swaroop Chitlur Haridas opens (00:13-03:47, plus the ownership interjection at 13:02-13:23) and Nachiket Paranjape delivers the rest from 03:47. The auto-captions garble Swaroop's name repeatedly ("Farup," "Harup," "Faroo," "Surup") and Nachiket's once ("Nachig").
- Caption artifacts resolved from context: "v code"/"vcoded" → vibe code/vibe-coded; "codeex or a claw code" → Codex or Claude Code; "statops"/"SNO"/"stat ops" → StratOps (strategy and operations); "doash" → DoorDash; "aent"/"multi- aent" → agent/multi-agent; "LM as a judge" → LLM as a judge; "openi" → OpenAI; "self-s serve"/"selfserve" → self-serve. Left explicitly unresolved: "the JPEA library" (a prompt-optimization library; GEPA is the closest phonetic match and this wiki already carries GEPA material, but the transcript does not confirm it), "Ragago"/"Ragav" (a DoorDash colleague who spoke earlier the same day), and "titrate" (used at 08:28, possibly the speaker's own word for narrowing down).
