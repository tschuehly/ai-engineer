## Summary

Martin Harrysson and Natasha Maniar argue that AI-assisted software development will not deliver enterprise-level value if companies only add coding tools to Agile-era structures. The source emphasizes matching operating models to work type, redesigning roles and team size around agent orchestration, and measuring outcomes such as delivery speed, quality, resilience, developer sentiment, and economic impact rather than usage alone.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Match AI operating models to work type](../concepts/match-ai-operating-models-to-work-type.md) - supports choosing factory-style, iterative, or other collaboration patterns based on task context and output shape.
- [Use one-pizza pods for agentic engineering](../concepts/use-one-pizza-pods-for-agentic-engineering.md) - supports smaller, consolidated teams where product builders orchestrate agents with broader architecture fluency.
- [Measure AI transformation by outcomes instead of adoption](../concepts/measure-ai-transformation-by-outcomes-instead-of-adoption.md) - supports input-to-economic-outcome measurement instead of tool usage as the success criterion.

## Notes

- Individual AI productivity stories can coexist with modest enterprise impact; the speakers cite a survey of about 300 mostly enterprise companies where many reported only 5%, 10%, or 15% overall productivity improvements. (03:12-03:43)
- The talk identifies new bottlenecks after AI adoption: collaboration practices lag faster work, generated code creates more manual review, and generated code can amplify technical debt or complexity. (04:04-04:59)
- Work allocation becomes harder because AI impact is uneven across task types and because people vary in AI-tool experience. (05:09-05:54)
- Legacy modernization is described as high-context but output-defined, which can fit an agent factory where humans provide initial specifications and final review. New greenfield or brownfield feature work may need iterative loops where agents co-create options and accelerate feedback. (07:36-08:36)
- Top performers in the survey were described as seven times more likely to have AI-native workflows across more than four software-development lifecycle use cases, and six times more likely to have AI-native roles with smaller pods and new skill sets. (08:36-09:16)
- The supported AI-native workflow shifts include continuous planning, spec-driven development, PM-agent iteration on specs, one-pizza pods of three to five people, consolidated full-stack product-builder roles, and PMs creating direct code prototypes. (09:33-10:27)
- A bank intervention used agents for sprint-story assignment from velocity and delivery history, prototype and acceptance-criteria iteration around security and observability, workflow-specific squads, cross-repository impact checks, and real-time customer feedback for reprioritization. (10:43-12:26)
- Rollouts failed when tools were added without changed expectations, hands-on upskilling, coaching, habit formation across the first sprints, and a measurement system. (16:32-17:48)
- The measurement framework should connect investments in tools, upskilling, and change management to adoption, velocity, capacity, developer NPS, craft satisfaction, security, quality, resilience, time to revenue, price differential, customer expansion, and cost per pod. (18:31-20:24)
