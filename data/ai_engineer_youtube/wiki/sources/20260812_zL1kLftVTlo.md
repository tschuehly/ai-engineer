## Summary

Ronak Malde — previously growing the research team at Windsurf, where they trained the model that "ended up leading to the two billion acquisition at deep mind," now founder of Trajectory — gives the wiki's second independent account of on-policy self-distillation, and the first one that says what breaks when you scale it. The talk's organizing device is a four-property scorecard for post-training algorithms: an *online* task distribution, *on-policy* sampling, *parallelism of one* (no group rollouts), and a *per-token* reward. He walks SFT, DPO/RLHF, and GRPO across it and shows each buying some properties at the cost of others — GRPO in particular is a "Faustian bargain" that maxes on-policy sampling while exploding parallelism (and therefore environment infrastructure) and collapsing all feedback into one sequence-level scalar, which he likens to being handed 87 out of 100 on an essay and told to work out why. On-policy self-distillation claims all four, and the argument for its ceiling is mechanical rather than empirical: because the loss matches the teacher's full distribution over "let's say 65k tokens" at every position, it can move probability onto a token the student never sampled, which is "not just taking now a distribution like RL and slightly sharpening it, but… actually shifting entire distributions."

The motivating claim is an economic one. Benchmarks saturate "within first years and then months," take "4 hours, 6 hours, 24 hours or even several days" to scale, and are "not tied to real world use cases," while "we are actually spending hundreds of trillions of tokens every single day on inference" that already records how models fail and succeed — "that should be signal that we should be capturing and training on." The method that makes that exhaust usable is the same one Applied Compute described at this event: at the frontier there is no smarter teacher, so you give the *student* privileged information — a hint — in the teacher's prompt and match the log probs of the hint-free student to the hinted teacher, shifting toward "those log probs had it known the answer in the first place."

The failure modes are the part of the talk with no counterpart elsewhere in the wiki, and both appear only above a scale threshold — the 120B range with "50 or 100" tool calls rather than one or two, where eval accuracy goes "all over the place," run-to-run variance is "extremely high," and the model stops honoring the tool-call format it was instruction-tuned on. First, the **"but wait" problem**: on a long task the student drifts far enough that the teacher course-corrects "every single time it gets a chance," so the tokens the update favors fill with *wait*, *but*, and *maybe* until the model parks between two divergent distributions and "everything just turns into maybe." The fix is to weight each step's tokens by the student/teacher KL divergence rather than using KL as a penalty, so a step that has gone off the rails is trained lightly and one that has come back on track is trained normally. Second, **hint leakage**, which he names as OPSD's analogue of reward hacking: a hint containing the answer teaches a rollout that states the answer and back-fills the reasoning ("Oh, actually, I know what the solution is. It's 000. So, let me go back and put that into my reasoning trace"), a behavior that "is not going to occur whatsoever in the real world." Two mitigations: an LLM that rewrites the raw signal into "something reasonable that they should have known," and **residual guidance** — run a half-hint teacher alongside the full-hint teacher and take a linear combination, which both measures how far out of distribution the hint is and keeps the student from being "shift[ed] into unknown territory." Q&A supplies the most honest sentence in the talk: today's practice is "pseudo continual learning," batch offline updates and a model re-upload, and merging 10,000 concurrent production rollouts into one update is unsolved.

## Provenance and Caveats

- This is a founder talk for Trajectory, a company selling exactly the loop described, closing on a customer-logo slide and an invitation to get in touch. Read the algorithm as a design account and the results as marketing until published.
- **No measured number appears anywhere in the talk.** The two results claims — that GRPO "saturates around sonnet level performance" on LiveCodeBench while OPSD reaches "brand new territory," and that the scaled system "has even surpassed RL" on Mercor APEX agents — are stated without values, baselines, seeds, sample counts, or variance, which is a notable omission given that his own diagnosis of the 120B regime is that "run-to-run variance is extremely high."
- The "87 out of 100" essay figure is an analogy, and the "65k tokens" vocabulary is an illustrative round number, not a measurement of any specific model.
- The failure modes are the better-supported half, but they are supported by artifacts rather than statistics: a word cloud of favored tokens for the "but wait" problem, one rollout excerpt for hint leakage, and two before/after distribution sketches for residual guidance. No frequency is given for either failure — how often a long-horizon run collapses into hedging, or what fraction of hints leak, is unstated.
- Both mitigations are described qualitatively with the hyperparameters withheld. Step-level divergence weighting names KL as the weighting factor but not the functional form, the normalization, or any ablation against unweighted OPSD. Residual guidance says "cut it in half" and "linear combination" without the mixing coefficient or how it is chosen.
- The claim that OPSD needs no parallel rollouts and therefore "takes away the environment bottleneck" is a real structural difference, but the talk does not account for the compute the method *does* need — teacher forward passes over the full vocabulary at every token, for every rollout — nor compare total cost against GRPO.
- The framing that benchmarks are "not tied to real world use cases" is an argument that serves the product; the talk offers no evidence that models trained on production exhaust generalize better than models trained on benchmarks, only that the exhaust exists and is large.
- The named third-party attributions are all one-line name-drops with no citation: Ilya, Karpathy, Schulman(?), Demis, Satya, and a Dwarkesh video are offered as evidence that the field is converging on continual learning, not as sources for any technical claim.
- The talk's own honest boundary is in Q&A and should travel with every claim above it: the current state is "pseudo continual learning" with offline batch updates and a model re-upload, and merging 10,000 concurrent rollouts is unsolved — so the "software gets smarter every time it's used" vision is not what is being run today, by his own account.
