## Summary

Clare Liguori is a senior principal engineer at AWS who works mostly on Kiro, "our agentic coding assistant," and has "been working on agentic AI for over 3 years" (00:12-00:43). She opens by ranking the industry's phases against her own experience: inline completion, then chat, then vibe coding "sometime last year," and "completely anecdotally, based on my own experience, I've really only felt maybe 10 to 20% more productive with all of these phases that have come before" (00:48-01:22). What changed is the internal pilot data — "a median of 4.5x productivity improvement and sometimes more than 10x" — and a named target state she calls frontier development, defined not by tools but by three observable behaviours: hands-off coding, where "frontier developers write maybe 1 to 2% of the code that they produce. The rest is agents"; infrequent interaction, aiming "to get their coding assistant to run for up to hours at a time without their intervention"; and minimized idle time, running "multiple agents in parallel churning through a backlog of tasks" (01:22-02:20).

The most reusable part of the talk is the *shape of the evidence*, because Liguori narrates three internal studies in sequence and names the objection each one leaves open. The Bedrock Mantle team needed a new inference data plane estimated at "30 people over 18 months"; instead "they took six people and they built it in 76 days with Kiro," which "proved that it was possible to get up to 20X improvement" measured on commits (02:22-03:26). She then disqualifies her own headline: it was six of "the top engineers literally in the company including two distinguished engineers… experts in distributed systems, experts at LLMs and their architecture," and the story was "very unachievable for a lot of teams," so "can this actually be reproduced on another team?" (03:27-04:05). Prime Video answered the staffing objection — six ordinary engineers in a 10-day sprint who "brought down the project delivery time estimate from what was going to be 90 weeks down to 24" — and she disqualifies that one too: "they had no on-call duties, limited meetings, very few distractions," and "the senior engineer on the team had spent the previous 3 weeks creating very detailed, small, well-scoped tasks with detailed requirements for these six engineers to just go churn on" (04:05-05:47). Amazon Stores then ran the study that removes both confounds: 50 teams with "normal distribution of early career folks, mid-career, senior engineers," working on "existing systems with existing code bases. Nothing green field," watched "for the better part of last year," measured on "deployment velocity to production. So, not just commits… how quickly are we getting changes out to customers?" (05:48-06:50).

That study is where the talk's thesis comes from, and its structure matters: **the tool was held roughly constant and the outcome still split in half**. "For half of the teams, they achieved less than 3x increase," while the other half saw "a median of 4.5x, and in some cases more than 10." The difference "was how they used the tools. 90% of these teams used Kiro, among other internal tools that we have, and what they found was it wasn't about the tools, it was about the way that they worked. The teams that achieved step function improvements intentionally changed the way that they worked, and the other simply kind of sprinkled Kiro and some of the other tools that we have on top of their existing way of working" (06:50-07:34). Interviews across the pilot teams, Bedrock Mantle, and Prime Video produced five *habits* — a word she chooses deliberately, because "it's not about that one sprint. It's about doing this day-to-day… it's hard to build these habits. It takes time" (07:49-08:24).

The five habits are unglamorous and mutually reinforcing. **Invest in agent context**: the knowledge normally transferred through "Slack conversations… onboarding, mentors… code reviews… stand-ups and sprint planning" has to be written down, and the habit is a question asked at every failure — "every time the agent makes a mistake or does something not the way that you would have done it, what am I missing in my skills files? What am I missing in my steering files that the agent needed?" But the same habit runs in reverse as models improve: Sonnet 3.7 "had a lot of quirks that we had to put a lot of do nots in our steering files, and now we don't have to do that as much with Opus 4.5," so "the new habit… is do I still need this in my steering files or is this just bloating context?" (08:26-09:39). **Slow down to speed up**: "in almost every team that was interviewed, they reported that their productivity actually went down as they intentionally adopted a new way of working," because "we have to do real work in our code base first for agents to be successful there, especially in brownfield existing code bases" — building agent context, improving "existing tools error messages so that the model knew what was going on when it failed," building "new tools, new MCP servers," restructuring the codebase for navigability, and in some cases changing the language outright, away from Python and JavaScript because "they're untyped languages. It's hard to test. There's no compiler errors. So the model kind of guesses," toward TypeScript and Rust, where "the compiler gives great error messages" (09:39-11:08). **Feed agents, don't babysit them**: "if you are having a back-and-forth conversation with your agent all day long, of course you're not going to see four to five x productivity improvements because you are in the loop the entire time"; sitting through 30-second to one-minute generations means "you can't go off and do other stuff… It's very difficult to clone yourself into multiple agents." Feeding means supplying "what it needs to do and how it can self-validate," so the agent "only come[s] back to you when it meets a certain quality bar, when it actually runs and compiles and passes tests, when it's testable, when it actually has high coverage" — and then "the next level is put all of this content into your steering file so it does it every time without you having to prompt it" (11:09-12:31). **Make intent explicit**: "it is less productive to iterate with the agent on code when the intent itself was incorrect," so for "ambiguous complex features" engineers write the specification first, and "it's a lot easier to iterate with the model in kind of a back and forth conversation about a document than it is about code that's… spread across a code base" (12:33-13:53). **Shift testing left**: fast feedback "is what lets it go off for hours at a time and self-correct," delivered through linters and unit, integration, performance, and security tests — "things we all know we should have been doing all along," except that "now the ROI is, I think, finally high enough" — and specifically through "mock services that run entirely locally with deterministic responses because it lets the agent do everything locally," since "the more that your agent can get fast feedback means the more loops that it can do" (13:53-15:19).

She closes on costs rather than nirvana — "I would be remiss if I would tell you if you adopt all of these habits, you will achieve nirvana… We are still very much in an early adopter phase" (15:19-15:43). Three individual costs: burnout, with engineers "staying up late late at night trying to get that perfect prompt that's going to make their agent run for hours overnight"; rising cognitive load from "constantly shifting between terminal tabs"; and an unevenly distributed review cost, because "reviewing AI output is often harder for some than actually writing it, especially early in career" — senior engineers "have already spent a large portion of their career reviewing others code," while "early career engineers don't have that muscle yet" (15:43-16:44). Three organizational ones: leaders who will not fund the dip — "my fellow leaders have been guilty of… saying, 'Well, you have the AI tools now and the models are so amazing now. Why are you not going faster?' And that's because you have to take those two months to invest in your code base" (17:02-17:54); rolling out too broad too fast, since "if we had expected all teams in massive organizations to be frontier teams immediately, we would not have had the learnings that we had from the Pathfinder, from the sprint experiment, from the pilot teams," with 2026 framed as scaling "to the next 2,000 teams instead of 50 teams" (17:54-18:46); and new bottlenecks, the sharpest observation in the talk: "previously, writing code manually was the bottleneck… the speed of decision-making becomes a new bottleneck… because the code only takes 1 to two months to write. All of the review processes associated with the launch of a product become the bottleneck. When it used to take 9 to 12 months to build a new product, it didn't matter so much… if it took two months to make the decision to build the product and then two months to approve the launch. But now those are the bottlenecks. Those are the long pole." Hence: "frontier engineering teams spend more time making decisions than they do writing code. And so the more that you can make fast decisions, especially ones that are easy to be reversed, the better" (18:45-19:56).

## Provenance and Limits

- Every figure is a self-reported internal Amazon number presented on a slide. No raw data, sample sizes per group, baseline window, variance, or confidence interval is given anywhere, and none of it has been externally audited or published.
- The productivity proxies are output proxies, not value or quality measures. Bedrock Mantle and Prime Video were measured on commits (03:20-03:23, 04:36-04:48); the 50-team pilot was measured on deployment velocity to production (06:36-06:50), which Liguori explicitly presents as an upgrade over commits. Neither is adjusted for defect rate, rework, incident load, revert rate, review effort, or the value of what shipped. Nothing in the talk reports a quality outcome.
- **There is no control arm.** "90% of these teams used Kiro, among other internal tools that we have" (07:08-07:13), so the pilot compares Kiro-users against Kiro-users. That design supports the claim the tool is *not sufficient* — the dispersion is real with the tool roughly held constant — but it cannot support "it wasn't about the tools" as a claim about tool choice, because no team worked without one.
- The speaker works on Kiro, and Kiro is the tool in all three studies. The habits framing is tool-agnostic on its face, which is the direction that generalizes; but whether a different assistant would produce the same split is untested and unaddressed.
- The 4.5x/10x result is reported as "half of the teams… less than 3x" against a median of 4.5x for the other half (06:50-07:06). No distribution, no per-half n, no statistical treatment, and no definition of how the halves were separated other than post-hoc interviews about working style.
- The Bedrock Mantle comparator is an *estimate*, not an observed alternative: "they had estimated it at 30 people over 18 months" (02:46-02:51) against 76 days with six people. Nothing establishes the estimate was accurate, and the team was greenfield and staffed with "two distinguished engineers" (03:33-03:40) — Liguori raises both objections herself.
- The Prime Video 90-weeks-to-24 number is likewise a *re-estimate* made after a 10-day sprint (04:25-04:37), not a delivered project. The sprint excluded three weeks of prior task decomposition by a senior engineer and ran with "no on-call duties, limited meetings, very few distractions" (05:00-05:31). Again, self-flagged.
- The five habits come from interviews with participating teams (07:49-08:00). They are practitioners' self-reported explanations of their own results, which is a weaker instrument than the deployment-velocity measurement they are meant to explain, and no habit is individually attributed to any share of the gain.
- The costs — burnout, rising cognitive load, and reviewing being harder than writing for early-career engineers — are stated as observations with no survey, hours, attrition, or review-latency data behind them (15:43-16:44).
- No cost, token, or infrastructure-spend figure appears anywhere. "Now the ROI is, I think, finally high enough" for testing investment (14:29-14:36) is an assertion with the hedge attached.
- The "frontier developer" definition — writing "1 to 2% of the code that they produce" (01:51-01:59) — has no stated measurement method, population, or period. It functions as a description of a target state, not as a metric anyone reports.
- Time anchors are all relative. Given the 2026-08-28 upload, "the Sonnet 3.7 in the middle of last year" and "Opus 4.5 as of last November" (09:09-09:23) read as 2025 releases, and "we've had more than 6 months of improvement since then" places the model-behaviour claims at roughly nine months stale by upload. That mapping is an inference from the upload date, not something the talk states.
- The talk names no failure: no pilot team that changed how it worked and did not improve, no habit that backfired, no project that the approach damaged. The selection is from teams Amazon chose to study and interview.
- Caption artifacts resolved: the speaker's name renders as "Claire La Gory" → Clare Liguori (00:12-00:14); "Kuro" → Kiro throughout (00:19, 03:05, 04:22, 07:10, 13:34); "our agent encoding assistant" → our agentic coding assistant (00:19-00:22); "Bedrock mantel" → Bedrock Mantle (07:56); and at 12:50 "what I've typically seen with live coding as opposed to frontier engineering is giving a very high-level prompt" reads as *vibe* coding, matching her own usage at 01:01 and 11:21. `[snorts]` and `[clears throat]` are caption renderings of speech sounds, not content.
- Left unresolved: "flow mat is real" (15:53-15:55), the burnout term she credits to someone else at another conference — the phrase is not recoverable from the captions and no plausible expansion is asserted here; and "the Q product" (12:41-12:47), where behaviour-driven development is said to be built in and which most plausibly refers to an Amazon Q product, but the captions give only the letter.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Stage Productivity Pilots to Strip One Confound at a Time](../concepts/stage-productivity-pilots-to-strip-one-confound-at-a-time.md) - three internal studies run in sequence, each removing the objection that invalidated the last: elite staffing, then artificial conditions, then nothing.
- [Being in the Loop Is the Ceiling on Agent Parallelism](../concepts/being-in-the-loop-is-the-ceiling-on-agent-parallelism.md) - conversational back-and-forth caps the multiplier at one human's serial attention, and the way out is shipping self-validation criteria with the task.
- [When Code Stops Being the Long Pole, Approvals Become It](../concepts/when-code-stops-being-the-long-pole-approvals-become-it.md) - a two-month decision and a two-month launch approval were rounding errors against a 9-12 month build and are now the critical path.
- [Budget the Productivity Dip That Precedes the Agent Speedup](../concepts/budget-the-productivity-dip-that-precedes-the-agent-speedup.md) - almost every team interviewed got slower first, and the organizational failure is a leader who reads the dip as underperformance.
