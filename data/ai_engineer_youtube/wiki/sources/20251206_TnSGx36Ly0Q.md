## Summary

Mark Myshatyn frames government and national-lab agents as high-consequence systems that must combine scientific acceleration with strict data, governance, isolation, explainability, and deployment requirements. The talk argues that regulated agents should go deeper than generic office automation, connecting literature review, hypothesis generation, code execution, and HPC simulation while satisfying federal controls and classified-data constraints.

## Notes

- Los Alamos treats agents as a way to move science faster, not only as chatbots or generic productivity assistants; the ICF example starts with a paper, has the agent find related papers, generate a hypothesis, produce a capsule design, and execute code against HPC simulation assets. (01:34-03:18)
- The lab positions its model context as more than the LLM: decades of math, science, nuclear-stockpile stewardship tools, and HPC simulation infrastructure become part of the agentic system. (02:42-03:06)
- Government AI adoption has to move quickly while respecting real-world consequences; the talk cites OMB memoranda M-25-21 and M-25-22 as current federal guidance pushing agencies to define AI strategies, pilot governance, and high-risk versus low-risk use in their context. (06:09-10:23)
- For federal and DOE workloads, ordinary SOC 2 claims are not enough; vendors may need to account for NIST SP 800-53, FedRAMP, DoD cloud security requirements, CNSSI 1253, continuous monitoring, data impact levels, and classified-data boundaries. (07:24-09:43)
- The architecture advice for federal agent tools is to build for explainability, isolation, governance, and speed, including deployment in constrained environments, SBOMs, open-source dependency handling, patching plans, and federal-region feature parity. (11:56-15:01)
