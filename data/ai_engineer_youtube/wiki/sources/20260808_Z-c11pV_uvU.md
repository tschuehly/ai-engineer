## Summary

Frank Coyle — a computer science instructor of more than thirty years, now teaching at Berkeley, whose students face the fact that "computer science is no longer the magic pathway to a job" — reads Anthropic's Claude Certified Architect exam as an engineering document rather than a credential. His premise for doing so is that a vendor's exam encodes what the vendor watches go wrong: "I think if you're interested in a career in agentic AI, then certainly take a look at least what the exam is about, because I feel that Anthropic knows how people are using their system and what the issues are going to be." The exam was released in March, is scenario-based, timed, and proctored, is available to companies in the Anthropic ecosystem with individuals paying $99 once every six months, and is multiple choice in form but "based on realistic constraints and realistic scenarios." Five weighted domains are listed — agentic architecture 27%, configuring the Claude Code system and workflow 20%, then prompt engineering and structured output ("using JSON all over the place"), tool design and Model Context Protocol integration, and context management and reliability, with only the first two percentages given in the captions. Six production scenarios exist and the exam "will randomly choose four and all the questions will be centered around the four."

**The reading method is the talk's organizing idea and its most transferable one.** Coyle walks each scenario backwards, leading with the anti-pattern, on the analogy of the early-1990s design patterns movement, which arrived alongside a catalog of the moves that quietly ruin you: "we have patterns for agents, but there's also anti-patterns. And I think anti-patterns are a key to understanding what you should not do because understanding what you should not do is the key to leading you to what you should do." He offers the same as exam tactics — "one of the big things is what not to do and that often can be the key to getting these questions right" — and as a working philosophy, bracketed by a Sister Corita Kent quote ("Nothing is a mistake. There's no win and no fail. There's only make") and Edison's "I have not failed. I've only found 10,000 ways that don't work."

**Scenario one, a customer support resolution agent, produces the sharpest concrete pattern: branch the loop on the stop reason rather than on the text.** The anti-pattern is "just to let the agent go and do something and get the response back and use it." The reason the branch is required is that the model is not the executor — "the LLM can't do anything. It is just a probabilistic next word predictor. It can't execute tools," but "if you point it to a tool, it can figure out how to set things up so that you or your code can execute it," so what comes back on a tool-use stop is "basically the parameters that it has extracted from the data that you provided it." The loop shown is a `while True` with three blocks: call the model with messages, prompt, context, and tools; read the stop reason and, if it is tool use, run the tool and hand the result back; and when the model returns without a tool call, exit. Two things hang off that structure. The exit is the natural gate — "this is an opportunity for you to have a human in the loop potentially. You check the confidence. If it looks good, you keep it. If you don't, then you escalate to a human" — and the stop reason carries a second, quieter case: "one of the stop reasons may be you have run out of tokens, and this response is based on partial when the LLM had to stop. And it's going to give you a response, but if you have run out of tokens, then you need to take action."

**Scenario three, a multi-agent research system, supplies the talk's most original claim.** The anti-pattern is the generalist: "you have one agent and you load it up with tools," which he likens to hiring a carpenter who arrives with plumbing, carpentry, and electrical tools announcing "I can do anything" — "maybe you want a professional carpenter." The prescription borrows from functional programming, that "functions should do one thing," so an agent doing one thing "with maybe one or two tools available to it… that's going to be a win." The sharper move is what the specialist is *shown*. For a critic agent, "we're passing it the claim and the evidence… but we're not giving it the thought processes that went in to creating this claim," because "when you get a bunch of agents together collaborating and talking to each other, there's a tendency to have group think. And all the agents seem to kind of devolve into one idea" — illustrated by a party where everyone wants pizza except you and talks you into it. The generalization is that "you're going to give each agent only a slice."

**Scenario four, developer productivity, is where the two context controls are named as two.** The anti-patterns are "let every subtask dump its full output into the primary thread, crowding out the context" and "let the context grow unbounded"; the prescription is "you want to isolate your subtask output, and you want to compact long sessions." Isolation is a context fork — for a "scan all the logs for error" task, the agent forks "into like a separate thread where whatever the agent does and thinks and adds tokens to does not come back and pollute the main context," and only "that summation without all the other stuff" re-enters. Compaction is a measured branch on top of it: "you can check your token count… and if you have more than 150,000 tokens, then what you want to do is you can run a compact." Vendor compaction is treated as opaque ("Not quite sure how the implementation is of that, but there is compaction"), with a pointer to a framework whose base class you can extend for "your own compression of your data, whatever you think is important." The underlying rationale is stated once and covers both: "context means tokens, tokens mean money, and the more context you have, the more confused the LLM is going to be," so "even though oh, a million token context window, I can put everything in there. No, no, don't put everything in there."

The remaining material is thinner. Scenario two reduces to the hierarchical `CLAUDE.md` — three levels of instruction file, at the top level, in the project folder, and within directories, giving "a hierarchical set of rules that can then control how the system is going to respond." Claude Code for CI gets one anti-pattern, "always have interactive modes in a pipeline," since interactive mode means "Claude will stop and ask you… Can I have permission for that?" and "there are ways to set it up so that it'll just run straight through." A closing operational tip: batch your prompts and "for 50% fewer token cost you will get the result they promise in at least 24 hours" — worth it "if you're going to go take a nap, you're going to go on vacation." A digression argues that loops are not new, only newly available: quoting Boris Cherny ("he doesn't write code, but his job is to write loops") and Peter Steinberger ("I don't code anymore. I just design loops that prompt your agents"), he points at Böhm and Jacopini's 1966 result that Turing completeness needs only sequence, conditional, and loop — "up to now we've had sort of sequences. You have prompts, you have maybe if-then, but now we have a loop. And now this is what's giving us the power."

## Provenance and Limits

- A ~20-minute conference talk that opened with technical difficulties, was delivered against a visibly short clock ("I've only got a few few minutes left, few seconds left"), and ended with no Q&A. Two of the six exam scenarios were named but not opened: the CI scenario received a single anti-pattern and the structured-data-extraction section was cut for time.
- Nothing here is measured. There is no incident data behind the anti-patterns, no ablation behind the groupthink claim, no basis given for the 150,000-token compaction threshold, and no evidence that the exam's domain weights track real support volume — the inference that they do is the speaker's, offered as a feeling ("I feel that Anthropic knows how people are using their system").
- The speaker is an instructor with no stated affiliation to Anthropic and no stake in the product, which is unusual for this wiki's recent sources and worth weighing on the other side: the argument for reading the blueprint costs him nothing either way. He also does not report having taken the exam.
- Code was shown on slides the transcript does not capture, so the loop, the critic call, the fork, and the compaction check are described in prose here rather than reproduced. The full set of stop reasons is never enumerated, and the remedy for the out-of-tokens case is only "take action."
- The compaction-framework recommendation is a live plug for a book being handed out at the venue, with the speaker explicitly disclaiming any relationship ("I have no connection, I didn't even know Sam").
