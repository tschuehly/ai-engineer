## Summary

An eighteen-minute talk from Sourcegraph's "agent advocate" — a research scientist who "last year… was an astronomer" (00:43), had zero GitHub commits a year ago and now has 12,000 while maintaining an open-source multi-agent orchestration framework (03:56). The title was chosen by their manager, an actual developer advocate, who submitted it and then went on vacation rather than deliver his own eulogy (00:52-01:26). The argument is not that developer relations died but that its audience changed: an agent reads your docs, calls your API, hits your errors, and recommends your tool, so it is a user whose experience is worth instrumenting. What makes the talk useful to the wiki is that it supplies two measurement instruments for that claim rather than only the claim. The first is a with-and-without benchmark — hundreds of software-lifecycle tasks run by agents with and without Sourcegraph's code-navigation MCP tool, with the traces read afterward. The second is a generative-engine-optimization experiment that asks assistants questions two different ways and counts product mentions, which produced the talk's sharpest number: 65% of the time when the prompt is a tool comparison, zero when the prompt describes the underlying pain.

**The agent is both a user and a recommender.** As a user it is "going out reading your docs, but it's just reading them differently cuz it's a machine… it's calling the API. It's… ha[s] its own frustrations with how it's encountering errors and recovering from them" (04:15-04:44). As a recommender it occupies the position developer advocacy was built to reach — bottom-up adoption — except "now the adoption and the recommendation system, a lot of it's being driven by the agent itself," either answering product questions directly in ChatGPT or Claude or installing libraries into a developer's workflow unattended (04:44-05:22). Jarmak references an earlier speaker asking how many people had let an agent install a library for them, and many hands going up.

**CodeScaleBench, and the trace that shows why it exists.** The first project after becoming agent advocate was "a benchmark called CodeScaleBench… hundreds of tasks that were reflective of the software development life cycle. And I basically unleashed these agents with and without um our product tooling" — the product being Sourcegraph's code navigation MCP tool (05:36-06:17). The stated purpose is two-sided: "how is our tool helping the agent do the work," and "when it isn't working well, why isn't it working well? So that we can then go in and actually fix that" (06:06-06:17). Thousands of traces came out of it. The example given is a read tool where "the model had these expectations based off of its… biases from how it… from its training data of what it expected for a particular… command… And there's nothing in our description uh that would have led it to believe otherwise. So it tried to use like read line instead of start line" (06:33-07:01). The failure was recoverable — "at least the error told it why it failed… So it was able to fix itself" — and that is exactly the problem: "but then it's burning right an entire turn just failing. And you could just go in and fix that" (07:01-07:20). The purchasing consequence is stated directly: organizations evaluate "not just is it working well, but like how many tokens is the agent dealing with to work with your tool? And how fast is it?" (07:23-07:38).

**GEO, and the 65-to-zero gap.** The second experiment is "GEO instead of SEO… generative engine optimization" (07:47-07:50): a set of prompts run against chatbots and agents, counting whether Sourcegraph is mentioned. The design step that produced the finding was asking "what is your ICP actually doing when you would want your product to be surfaced" (08:18-08:38). Prompts written around someone "actively shopping for this sort of code intelligence sort of tooling and doing a comparative sort of thing" surfaced the product "like 65% of the time" (08:38-08:57). The prompt written as a pain — "we keep breaking downstream services when we change shared libraries because we can't see all the consumers," which is what Sourcegraph's cross-repo observability layer exists for — produced "zero mentions," and the assistant instead suggested "you could just have your developers make a wiki page" (08:57-09:32). Jarmak's read is a messaging hypothesis, not a model failure: "maybe the messaging that we're putting out there isn't attributing some of these pains and use cases clearly enough for the agents to be picking it up," now the subject of a content campaign whose lift will be measured — and measured specifically through "how the agents that are using those… web search tool calls… are then interpreting the information about your product," not through what got baked into training data (09:32-10:10).

**Stale content compounds, and a newer model made it worse.** The GEO pilot ran on Claude Sonnet 4, which "kept pitching Cody, which was… one of our older products." Re-running it the afternoon of the talk on 4.6 thinking, on the expectation that a newer model would know better, produced the opposite: "it pitched Cody even more" (10:30-11:15). The mechanism offered is compounding rather than lag: "now you have all of these like old models like outputting content that then is like compounding in the internet. So, you have to figure out like how to bury all of that noise with your true signal" (11:15-11:27). `llms.txt`-style pages are named as one response — "more authoritative sources of truth that you're hoping to direct the agent to" — with an immediate caveat that the agent still has to be "using the tools and using real-time information and provenance to be able to give accurate answers about your product" (11:27-11:45).

**Practical instructions.** Give the agent "something to quote… they want to bring something that they can really sell to the user," so keep current examples and keep everything fresh "even if your stuff hasn't changed in 2 years… because that… is how they have their relevance algorithm"; and note that assistants "really really like charts and FAQs" (11:45-12:12). Be where the agents are: "go to market. So, go go to agent market… make sure you're in the marketplace in the MCP registries, everywhere that you would expect an agent to be able to easily find you" (12:12-12:28). Cut the distance from discovery to adoption, because gating disqualifies you from being recommended: "if an agent realizes your tool requires like three different demos and emailing sales reps and stuff, they're never going to say, 'Hey user, like here's what you should do, but FYI, you're going to have to do all this other stuff'" (12:28-12:53).

**The role split, and what survives.** Asked where agent advocacy sits — engineering, product, or marketing — the answer is "yeah, yes, yes. It's all of those things," and agents have made the seams fuzzier rather than clearer across the whole organization, "so that actually helps in a lot of ways" (13:10-13:48). Three mixable flavors follow: the engineering flavor partners with engineering on the MCP interface, evals, and instrumentation; the product flavor owns the end-to-end agentic experience and translates evals into agent-experience rubrics; the marketing flavor owns pipeline generation and how agents enter the funnel and bring developers with them (13:48-14:40). The four DevRel functions survive with changed content: enablement now educates both developers who orchestrate fleets of agents and the agents themselves via machine-readable content and agent-friendly APIs (14:40-15:20); community is "more important than ever" but inherits a new problem, developers bringing agents into a Discord that then records everyone's conversations (15:20-16:08); the feedback loop gains a capability humans never offered — "you can also basically spin up like thousands of these agents to perform experiments on them and experiments that you can't really do as easily with the developers who don't want to maybe talk to you that much" (16:08-16:20); and credibility splits in two, because humans reject LLM-styled prose ("don't… us[e] Claude's slop at them… tell your AEs to stop that as well. Nobody… everybody knows what it is and nobody likes it") while "Claude loves its own slop… there's a bias, right, from agents of their own content," so agent-facing content "as long as it's structured" can carry "as many m dashes and whatever as it wants" (16:20-16:45).

**The curb cut.** The closing argument: curb cuts were built for wheelchairs and now serve strollers, suitcases, and anything with wheels, so "by serving the agents, the human path gets cleared, too. There's just one more user in the room now, but they are still serving the human on the other end" (16:45-17:21). Two starter actions are given: point a coding agent at your docs and read the transcript as the beginning of an agent-experience report; and on the GTM side, write GEO prompts and count mentions versus recommendations (17:21-17:56).

## Provenance and Limits

This is a first-person report from one vendor about its own product, delivered by someone who had held the role for under a year. Neither instrument's results are published in the talk: CodeScaleBench's with-versus-without outcome is described as existing ("thousands and thousands of these traces") but no aggregate number is given, and only one trace is narrated. The GEO study is explicitly labeled a "pilot study" with no prompt count, no repetition count, and no statement of which assistants were queried beyond "different chatbots and agents"; the 65% and zero figures are single-condition point estimates, and the Sonnet 4 versus 4.6 comparison is one re-run performed the afternoon of the talk. The content-campaign lift is future work, not a result. The talk names an accompanying QR code and "a couple different toy repos" with templates, which are not reproduced in the transcript. Treat the numbers as an existence proof that the shopping-versus-hurting gap can be large and measurable, not as a calibrated effect size.
