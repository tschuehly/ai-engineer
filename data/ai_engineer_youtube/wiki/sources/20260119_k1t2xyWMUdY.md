## Summary

This METR discussion explains why rising model time-horizon and benchmark measurements do not automatically imply near-term speedups for experienced open-source developers. It emphasizes field measurement, unreliable self-reported task duration, task-distribution effects, messy data-science work, and the need to distinguish monitored from unmonitored long-task capability.

## Notes

- A learning-curve or "J-curve" explanation is plausible for new tools, but this source argues it did not explain much of the observed gap in the METR developer study; participants already using Cursor as a primary IDE did not show a large point-estimate shift, and subset plots had wide uncertainty. (07:26-16:12)
- The discussion states that surveys can ask how productive people felt, but asking how long a task took is unreliable because people are often wrong about elapsed time. (08:53-09:15)
- The source treats real developer work as harder to interpret than benchmark tasks because experienced people, familiar repositories, brownfield work, task allocation, and measurement noise can all affect observed speedup. (10:41-16:38)
- A randomized greenfield hackathon with AI-allowed and AI-disallowed teams had noisy unpublished results; the speaker cites roughly four percentile points higher for AI-allowed teams after controls, but explicitly warns not to draw conclusions from it. (21:41-23:09)
- The discussion highlights data-science and ML-engineering work as a benchmark gap: models may help write SQL, Pandas, or Polars, but complex messy-data tasks such as computing deployment metrics across enterprise systems can fail badly and may lack good benchmarks. (28:56-30:23)
- For long autonomy, the source suggests measuring time horizon under different supervision regimes: an agent that can do long work unmonitored may have a different risk profile from one whose transcripts are monitored by another model and escalated to humans. (48:32-50:39)
