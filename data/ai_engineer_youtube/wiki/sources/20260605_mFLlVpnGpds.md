## Summary

Hervé Bredin (CSO/co-founder of pyannoteAI, creator of the open-source `pyannote` toolkit) argues that transcription answers "what was said" but understanding a conversation needs progressively more: who said what (speaker-attributed transcription), who said what *and when* (interruptions, backchannels, pauses), *and how* (laughter, coughing, stress, prosody), and even who is talking to whom in what acoustic environment. The core of the talk is speaker diarization — "who speaks when" — which he frames as a distinct, still-unsolved task with its own pipeline (voice activity detection → segmentation/overlap → speaker assignment), its own difficulties (unknown speaker count, permutation-invariant labels, overlapping speech, very short turns, speaker imbalance), and its own metric, diarization error rate (DER = confusion + false alarm + missed detection over total speech). He shows the headline benchmarking trap: NVIDIA Parakeet reports 11.4% WER on AMI on the open ASR leaderboard but 26% on the same model and data when you use the table microphone instead of the headset — most voice-AI benchmarks measure single-speaker speech and call it solved. Finally he shows that gluing diarization and STT into speaker-attributed transcription is harder than it looks (boundary words with no clean owner, timestamp disagreement, overlap), and that pyannoteAI's STT-agnostic "STT orchestration" plus an "exclusive diarization" trick reconciles any STT with its diarization model.

## Notes

- Conversation understanding is a ladder, not a single step: transcription ("what was said") → speaker-attributed transcription ("who said what," enough for meeting note-takers to assign action items) → who said what *and when* (precise timestamps reveal interruptions and a short backchannel "yes"/"mhm" agreement, which can carry the most important information) → who said what, when, *and how* (laughter, coughing, stress; "the dog ate the cake" with different stress means different things) → who is talking to whom in what acoustic environment (quiet room vs. street vs. restaurant). (02:04-07:34)
- `pyannote` is an open-source speaker-diarization toolkit; downloads inflected when OpenAI released Whisper, because Whisper gives great free STT but no speaker tags, so people combined Whisper + pyannote. Nearing ~10K GitHub stars. (01:04-01:55) On Hugging Face, sorting audio-tagged models by downloads puts three speaker-identity/diarization models in the top seven (the rest STT), evidence "who said what" matters as much as "what was said." (07:50-08:38)
- Diarization pipeline: (1) voice activity detection — is anyone speaking; (2) segmentation — split speech regions into turns, find speaker-change points and overlapping/interrupting speech and backchannels; (3) assign a speaker identity to each turn. The system finds the number of speakers itself. (08:42-10:08)
- Why it's hard / unsolved: the number of speakers (classes) is unknown a priori, unlike classical ML — an attendee list is only a hint (two people on one channel, an uninvited joiner); labels are permutation-invariant (output is "speaker 1/2/3," not names, and swapping colors is still correct, which the eval metric must respect); plus overlapping speech, very short turns, speech-time imbalance, and acoustic conditions. (10:25-12:24)
- DER (diarization error rate) = (confusion + false alarm + missed detection) / total speech duration, computed with the `pyannote.metrics` library. Confusion = wrong speaker; false alarm = detect speech where there is none; missed detection = miss speech, often during overlap when only one of two speakers is detected. (14:16-15:24)
- Demo on a 30-second two-women telephone conversation: open-source `pyannote` Community-1 pipeline (downloaded from Hugging Face, run on a Mac via the MPS PyTorch backend) scored ~5% DER; pyannoteAI's cloud Precision-2 model scored ~3% DER on the same file. (12:57-16:01)
- State-of-the-art DER varies enormously by use case: roughly single-digit on clean two-party telephone speech (the talk abstract states ~2%; the spoken slide's auto-caption read ~8%), but ~41% in a noisy restaurant with background chatter — far from solved. (16:11-16:49)
- Benchmark trap: NVIDIA Parakeet reports 11.4% WER on AMI on the open ASR leaderboard, but Bredin gets 26% WER on the same model and same AMI recordings. Difference is the microphone — the leaderboard uses the per-person headset mic (single-speaker, close-talk), he uses the central table mic (multi-speaker, distant). AMI = meetings of ~4-5 people with both headset mics and one central table mic. "Most voice AI benchmarks are measuring single-speaker speech and calling it solved." (17:28-18:39)
- Speaker-attributed transcription (diarization + STT) fails for two reasons: (1) most STT models are trained on single-speaker data and degrade badly on multi-speaker audio (overlap, speaker change, crosstalk, interruptions, code-switching, distant mics); (2) reconciling diarization and STT timestamps is genuinely hard — STT doesn't transcribe overlap well, timestamps disagree, and each can detect speech the other misses. (16:52-19:43)
- Boundary-word problem: a word ("Oh") whose timestamps straddle two diarization turns has no clean owner; also a single transcribed word can land in a region where diarization says two speakers spoke. (20:39-21:27)
- pyannoteAI ships an "STT orchestration" cloud API that takes a diarization model (Precision-2) + an STT model (Parakeet) and reconciles them, even interleaving the two speakers' words correctly through overlap. (21:27-22:42)
- The reconciliation trick is partly proprietary, but one published part lives in the open Community-1 model as "exclusive diarization": when speech overlaps, select the single most-likely speaker to be transcribed by the STT, simplifying reconciliation. It is deliberately STT-agnostic — designed to work with any STT, including internally fine-tuned ones — so the orchestration is not part of STT training. (23:34-24:48)
- Companion tooling: `pyannote.metrics` (DER evaluation), an interactive visualization widget the speaker calls "i-pyannote" ("i" for interactive, not iOS/iMac) for inspecting confusion/false-alarm/missed-detection errors, and an SDK for the premium Precision-2 models; the demo notebook is published in pyannoteAI's tutorials GitHub repo. (22:42-23:19)
