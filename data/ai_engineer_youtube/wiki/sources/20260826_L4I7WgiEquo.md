## Summary

A twenty-minute architecture talk from an engineer on Notion's product growth team, now on its GTM engineering team, about a system still being built. The opening line is the frame: "a year ago, I would have told you that building a GTM system was a marketing ops problem. And today, I think it's one of the most interesting distributed systems problems that I've worked on" (00:20-00:31). Where the wiki's other go-to-market sources are a founder's stack (Exa) and a long-running internal assistant (Snowflake), this one is a four-layer system design with a security stance, a serving-latency decision, and two early numbers.

**The starting state.** GTM "involves a spiderweb of tools… stitched together by customer notes, proposals, contracts that are passed back and forth" (00:33-00:44). The pieces are not new — lifecycle messaging, product recommendations, sales automations, customer data, onboarding — "but the solutions were fragmented because the underlying technology forced them to be" (01:01-01:18). Two things changed: the CEO spent a winter break building a video game and "came back convinced that software engineering could be applied to many problems that were previously unwieldy or too costly," and "our ability to execute skyrocketed with agentic technology and the breadth of problems we could solve did too," so "the costly, time-consuming, and previously unsolvable spaghetti could now be sorted" (01:20-01:51). The conclusion is a reclassification, not a feature: "GTM had become a systems problem. That made us realize we could chip away at this holistically" (01:52-01:59).

**The problem statement is organizational before it is technical.** Notion's business moves between self-serve growth and sales-assist and "customers move between these two motions all the time" (02:15-02:22), but "customers experience one journey… internally it is supported by disconnected systems that don't actually talk to each other very well," which is "rife with human error" and loads cognitive burden onto teams (02:23-02:39). The rep-specific version: "sales reps are probably not the strongest at managing systems, but their strength is in sussing out human signals during the buying process," so every context switch between a call, research, and drafting follow-up is time not spent with customers (02:42-03:01). Marketing, sales, and customer ops run on separate toolsets, "all of them looking at a customer independently and making decisions separately," so the goal is "a single decisioning system that spans self-serve growth and sales assist" that is "programmable, proactive and continuous" (03:02-03:41).

**Four roadblocks.** No single source of truth — customer data across Salesforce, Gong, Outreach, ZoomInfo "and many more," product usage in Snowflake, and "a decade of the most important context" in notes and meeting docs (03:43-04:02). Internal innovation trapped in "single player mode… or you could say here single department mode," with employees already running MCPs and their own agents inside their own departments, each tool serving "a tiny slice of that customer journey" (04:04-04:33). Data quality: "conflicting systems of record, wrong contacts tied to different accounts. One bad mapping was enough to lose trust for a sales rep" (04:36-04:48). Data latency: "every vendor added a hop and this lag was causing us to act on stale data, and that meant we were automating on yesterday's world" (04:49-04:59). And the one flagged as big: structured versus unstructured, because "the most important facts about a customer were left in notes, like *the champion just left* or *don't contact this customer again*, or *they're blocked in legal*… if an automation couldn't see it or process it, it could do something catastrophically wrong" (05:00-05:27).

**Four questions became four layers.** A cross-functional team (CX, RevOps, product, engineering, sales) found that "every workflow could be reduced to four questions. What do we know about the customer? What should happen next? How do we execute that safely? And did it work? That became our architecture" (05:29-05:57): **know** a trusted context layer, **decide** the single next best step, **act** by firing a concrete action ("a lifecycle email, an in-app nudge, or a task handed to a rep"), and **learn** by watching what happened and feeding it back "so that it's a loop" (05:59-06:25). Then the correction: "this architecture is missing something important… the most important part is that humans and agents are operating on the same loop," which "concretely means that the context needs to be displayed so that humans and agents can read and operate on it together" (06:25-06:45). Roles differ within the shared system — agents do "the repetitive work at scale like gathering context, researching, drafting recommendations, and writing artifacts"; humans "provide the judgment, adding nuance, deciding what to do next, and if a recommendation is correct, and owning the customer relationship" (06:49-07:08) — and the design rule is stated as a rejection: "instead of building an AI layer on top of our business, we designed our architecture so that the agent can operate as another operator within the same system as humans" (07:09-07:20).

**Three implementation choices, made before building.** First, "we deliberately chose not to let an agent talk directly to a customer. For sales-assist workflows, humans stay in the loop by default and approve anything the agents do. The agents do the busy work." The same decision carries a security argument: "if a prospect fills out a contact sales form online, we treat that as untrusted user input. And so trust boundaries don't break down, especially because there is an agent in the middle" (07:23-07:58). Second, "routing and eligibility became a first-class primitive" — the checks "used to be scattered all over the place… one check or rule in an email tool, another in sales" and were pulled "into one place so that these rules can be consumed across our codebase, product, sales, engineering," covering customer segmentations and signal definitions, after which "a single classifier will route what the customer should do," which "will actually prevent double sends from our system and create very cohesive communication" (07:59-08:37). Third, ownership: "we decided that it was very important to own the context layer and we decided to rent everything else. Since we are a lean team, we will not build our own email vendor or enrichment services like Clay. We use Clay. And we believe that we understood our customers the best, so we will not give that away" (08:37-09:00).

**Know: compute in the warehouse, serve from the key-value store.** "Snowflake, which is our data warehouse, is where we compute this truth. We ingest data from all the vendors in our GTM stack to Snowflake. We run daily transforms and in some cases real time to produce a small set of modeled, versioned entities" — accounts, contacts, workspaces, eligibility, and facts — with "clear ownership of what teams or tools they come from and timestamps" (09:04-09:36). "DynamoDB is our key-value store and it's where we serve our truth. We publish a denormalized, key-addressable profile that agents can quickly query in milliseconds with no joins. We also persist agent-generated artifacts — research snippets, summarized notes, rolling summaries — and these unstructured data are also keyed by the same IDs so that downstream systems can read all of this in one shot" (09:36-10:07). The normalized result is then brought into Notion "so that we could work with structured and unstructured data at the same time" — product usage, activity logs from across the vendor stack, research reports and notes (10:10-10:35) — and this pays off twice: GTM teams "didn't need to jump between many tools anymore," exploring context, investigating accounts, and taking actions like sending to Nooks or Outreach from a tool they already used ("they didn't need to open seven tabs anymore"), and "because we weren't building an AI layer, humans, workflows, and agents could all operate on the same source of truth. In a very literal sense, we are using Notion to grow Notion" (10:36-11:17).

**Decide: signals, ownership, and a default.** "The unit here is a signal. A signal is a single customer event that's important enough to change what should happen next for a customer." Some are user-driven ("a customer hitting their AI limit," reaching out to contact sales) and "some of them are not user initiated at all" — company raising funding, hiring signals, a shift in tech stack — and "those external signals are what allowed us to be proactive instead of reactive" (11:20-11:59). The signal service "watches the customer profile, decides whether a single action is available, decides who should own that action, and then it emits a concrete task," which "could be for a human or an agent"; a rep's task "lands in their Notion database and they can quickly view it and act on it" (12:00-12:27). The fallback is the part with no counterpart elsewhere in the wiki: "if there is no signal about a customer, the marketing component of our system kicks in. We have a predictive engine that will recommend product features most relevant for that customer and it will send out lifecycle emails and in-app nudges or multichannel communication to drive a customer towards adoption automatically" (12:29-12:53).

**Act: shadowed workflows on Temporal.** "We shadowed our best reps to capture something that was the most repetitive part of our job and encoded it as a durable multi-agent workflow. Every signal becomes a workflow on Temporal, which is something we rent, and a single run will touch enrichment, web search, draft generation and more. Each of these is a network call that could fail or rate limit," so Temporal "lets us focus on writing the sequential logic for our GTM use cases while it will handle the retries, dedupes, and going back to exactly where failures left off. And one malformed transcript can't take down the whole batch, which was really important to us" (13:04-13:52). Two worked shapes: a cold outbound signal runs "a research sub-agent [doing] concurrent researches, then it'll draft an email — there should be three of them, so they're scored — and then a review agent will pick the highest-scoring one and make any updates if necessary… this also operates on a loop so that the email drafts are improved," landing as a sales task (13:52-14:23); and a reactive post-call signal where "the Gong transcript will come in, our agent will parse the transcript and extract the critical sales MEDDPIC data — metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, plan, and champion — and draft a grounded follow-up." "Every LLM step is traced so that we can evaluate quality and improve over time" (14:24-14:52).

**Learn: outcomes threaded back to decisions.** "The third layer is what turns this automation into a system that self-improves. Every action is a decision log and every outcome threads back to the decision that caused it. So the naive version of this is a data analyst coming in and trying to understand if the output of this could be better. The rebuilt version of this is wiring our engagement history back into the decision layer so that the system decides whether or not to continue a thread, advance to the next step, or pivot," extended to lifecycle message performance history; "these verification loops are really critical so that the system can self-heal and continuously improve" (14:53-15:37).

**What the rep sees.** In the shared customer view a rep sees product usage, recent activity, and can "get an answer using that same data," where they "used to find all of this across many different tabs"; the agent replies "querying our context layer," and Notion custom agents "which are shareable across companies" access the same context for "recurring automated workflows" (15:38-16:13). In the task view "a rep starts their day with an already prioritized task box… and the email draft for an outreach task is already pre-researched and available for them to review. The human is still in the loop and actually adds their own judgment and taste and sales secret sauce, but they're no longer starting from a blank slate" (16:14-16:41). The stated goal is distributional: "this does more than help one rep be productive. Our goal is actually to raise the floor for the entire team," so a new rep "can learn the Notion sales process, understand what signals are important to look for, understand which playbooks are effective, and basically know what good follow-up looks like… without needing every lesson to be passed down manually" (16:42-17:14).

**Build or buy, per layer.** "It's very tempting and trendy to say build everything. But what we found is that there are still key areas to build and rent access to at every single layer. Internal agents are actually cheaper and faster to build than most people assume, and so since we have the most data on our data model, we build it there first and then we rented the generalizable parts later… We will not build a lot of these tools like orchestration, email, CRM. Vendors do that really well. We refuse to outsource the context layer because that's where our edge is. A generic tool can't capture all of our esoteric data models or workflows, and we do not want that context layer to be something we can't debug" (17:16-18:17). The context layer is Notion, "built off of plain markdown, a language that agents are fluent in, and we have databases and hierarchies that they can navigate easily; at the same time this is well designed for humans… this is what lets our engineers, agents, and GTM work off the same context" (18:18-18:44).

**Numbers and takeaways.** "It's very early for us. We're still building this out, but the initial signs are promising. In the last 13 weeks, we are already seeing enterprise reps have increased qualified opportunities. And on the lifecycle marketing side, users who received context-aware recommendations were 63% more likely to take the next step" (18:48-19:14). Four takeaways: "before you build, shadow your best human… I saw how many tabs and tools they were navigating between and that was chaos, but it was also the spec. And so if you encode a mediocre process you get a mediocre agent"; "start with the most legible workflow — that's the one that's documented and repeated. And let humans stay in the loop where there are risky possibilities"; "model GTM as primitives — entities, context, triggers, actions, eligibility rules — and the alien world becomes a system you can engineer"; and "be headless by default and design for agents as operators and not just co-pilots. If humans and agents can't read from the same substrate, you're basically building two systems that will eventually drift apart" (19:26-20:29). The closing projection: "right now, humans are the primary consumer of GTM data and agents are helping at the edges. Soon, agents will become primary first-class consumers within the system, moving from drafting to acting within guardrails" (20:30-20:49).

## Provenance and Limits

- The system is explicitly incomplete: "we're still in the midst of it" (00:53-00:56), "it's very early for us. We're still building this out" (18:55-18:57). Nothing here is a retrospective on a finished deployment.
- Only two outcome figures are given, and they are asymmetric. "In the last 13 weeks, we are already seeing enterprise reps have increased qualified opportunities" has no magnitude, no baseline, and no comparison group. The "63% more likely to take the next step" for context-aware lifecycle recommendations has a magnitude but no absolute rates, no description of the control, no sample size, and no statement of what "the next step" is or over what window it was measured. Neither is described as an experiment.
- No accuracy, precision, or quality figure is reported anywhere for the agent steps: not for the parsed Gong transcripts, the MEDDPIC extraction, the three scored email drafts, the review agent's selection, the predictive feature-recommendation engine, or the single routing classifier. Tracing is stated as in place ("every LLM step is traced so that we can evaluate quality") but no eval result, rubric, or judge is described.
- The "learn" layer is described partly in the future or aspirational tense — the naive version (an analyst) is contrasted with "the rebuilt version," and the self-healing loop is asserted rather than demonstrated. No example of the system pivoting a thread based on engagement history is given.
- The security stance is a design choice with a rationale, not a tested control. Treating the contact-sales form as untrusted input and keeping agents off the customer channel are stated as policy; no injection attempt, filter, classifier, or incident is described.
- Team and scale figures are absent: "a small team and myself" (00:46-00:48) is the only sizing, with no headcount, cost, latency measurement (the "milliseconds" claim for DynamoDB reads is unquantified), volume of signals, or number of workflows in production.
- This is Notion's GTM team using Notion as its context layer, which is both the honest version of the argument ("we are using Notion to grow Notion," 11:15-11:17) and a vendor demonstration. The claim that markdown plus databases is the right dual-audience substrate is not compared against any alternative.
- Vendors named as rented rather than evaluated: Salesforce, Gong, Outreach, ZoomInfo, Snowflake, DynamoDB, Temporal, Clay, Nooks. No selection criteria, cost, or migration experience is given for any of them.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "Dynamob" is DynamoDB; "compute our truth or serve our truth" at 09:39 is a self-correction to *serve*; "durable multi-aent workflow" is multi-agent; "MEDDPIC" is transcribed as "medpic"; "a Neil sales rep" is a new sales rep; "blocked illegal" is blocked in legal; "self-s serve" is self-serve; "our CEO Ivan built spent it building a video game" is a disfluency for spending the winter break building a video game. Left as heard: "Nooks" as the outbound tool name, and "notion custom agents which are shareable across companies."

## Extracted Concepts

- [Reduce Every Workflow to Know, Decide, Act, and Learn](../concepts/reduce-every-workflow-to-know-decide-act-and-learn.md) - the four questions a cross-functional team found under every GTM workflow, turned directly into four layers with a closed loop.
- [Put Humans and Agents on the Same Substrate Instead of an AI Layer on Top](../concepts/put-humans-and-agents-on-the-same-substrate-instead-of-an-ai-layer-on-top.md) - the correction to the four-layer diagram, and the takeaway that two substrates become two systems that drift apart.
- [Compute Truth in the Warehouse and Serve It as a Denormalized Profile](../concepts/compute-truth-in-the-warehouse-and-serve-it-as-a-denormalized-profile.md) - Snowflake computes modeled versioned entities on a daily cadence; DynamoDB serves a join-free profile agents read in milliseconds, with generated artifacts keyed alike.
- [Make Routing and Eligibility a Shared First-Class Primitive](../concepts/make-routing-and-eligibility-a-shared-first-class-primitive.md) - segmentation and signal definitions pulled out of individual tools into one consumable rule set with a single routing classifier, which is what prevents double sends.
- [Keep Agents Off the Customer Channel and Treat Inbound Forms as Untrusted Input](../concepts/keep-agents-off-the-customer-channel-and-treat-inbound-forms-as-untrusted-input.md) - one decision that is simultaneously a quality policy and a trust-boundary control, because an agent sits in the middle.
- [Emit Owner-Assigned Tasks From Signals, With a Marketing Default When None Fire](../concepts/emit-owner-assigned-tasks-from-signals-with-a-marketing-default-when-none-fire.md) - the signal service decides availability *and* ownership, and the absence of a signal is itself routed rather than dropped.
- [Shadow Your Best Human Before Encoding the Workflow](../concepts/shadow-your-best-human-before-encoding-the-workflow.md) - "the chaos was the spec," encode a mediocre process and you get a mediocre agent, start with the most legible workflow, and the payoff is raising the team's floor.
- [Own the Context Layer and Rent Every Other Layer](../concepts/own-the-context-layer-and-rent-every-other-layer.md) - build-versus-buy decided per architectural layer, with the context layer kept because it holds esoteric data models and must stay debuggable.
- [Thread Every Outcome Back to the Decision That Caused It](../concepts/thread-every-outcome-back-to-the-decision-that-caused-it.md) - decision logs plus outcome attribution let the decision layer continue, advance, or pivot without an analyst in the middle.

## Notes

- The reclassification is the talk's opening move and its organizing claim: a GTM system is a distributed-systems problem, not a marketing-ops problem (00:20-00:31), and the evidence offered for that is the failure list — vendor hops causing stale data, conflicting systems of record, and partial failure of a batch on one malformed transcript (04:36-04:59, 13:44-13:50).
- The unstructured-data roadblock is the one with the sharpest stakes and the least mechanism. The examples — "the champion just left," "don't contact this customer again," "they're blocked in legal" — are exactly the facts an automation must not miss, and the talk describes bringing notes into the context layer keyed to the same entities but never says how those statements are extracted, validated, or enforced as constraints (05:00-05:27, 09:53-10:07).
- Cross-functional composition is stated as the reason the four-question decomposition was found at all: the project team was CX, RevOps, product, engineering, and sales, "and after brainstorming together, we all kept finding the same patterns underneath that complexity" (05:29-05:43).
- The single-department failure mode is named before any architecture: employees were already using MCPs and building their own agents, but "single player mode… or single department mode" meant each tool served "a tiny slice of that customer journey" (04:04-04:33). The consolidation argument is therefore not that nobody was building — it is that everybody was.
- The internal-agent cost claim is one of the few generalizable assertions in the build-versus-buy section: "internal agents are actually cheaper and faster to build than most people assume," which is why they built on their own data model first and rented "the generalizable parts later" (17:35-17:51).
- The rented-versus-owned list is explicit and unusually clean: orchestration (Temporal), email, CRM (Salesforce), enrichment (Clay), and outbound (Nooks, Outreach) are rented; the context layer is not (08:37-09:00, 13:16-13:20, 17:52-18:07).
- Temporal's contribution is framed as isolation as much as durability — retries, dedupes, resume-at-failure, and the property that "one malformed transcript can't take down the whole batch" (13:29-13:52) — which is the reliability requirement that follows from processing per-customer events in bulk.
- The multi-draft pattern is small but concrete: a research sub-agent runs concurrent research, three email drafts are generated and scored, a review agent picks the highest scorer and edits it, and the loop repeats to improve drafts before one lands in a human's task (13:52-14:23). No scorer is described.
- MEDDPIC is used as the extraction schema for post-call follow-up — metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, plan, champion — which makes the sales methodology itself the structured output format for transcript parsing (14:32-14:44).
- The projected end state is a role change rather than a capability change: agents move "from drafting to acting within guardrails" and become "primary first-class consumers within the system," with humans currently primary and agents "helping at the edges" (20:30-20:49).
