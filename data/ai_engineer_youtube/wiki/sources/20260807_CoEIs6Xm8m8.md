# Open Source Is Dead. Long Live Open Source. — Saoud Rizwan, Cline

## Summary

Saoud Rizwan founded Cline, which he describes as "the first ever coding agent," built in the open before subscription pricing existed — "when people had to pay for each and every API request," before prompt caching, with some users paying "hundreds of dollars a day." He credits open source for that: developers could "inspect our code and trust it and connect to any API so they could be comfortable with spending so much money on it… know that they weren't getting screwed over," and features like custom rules and plan mode came out of the community around the project. The talk splits open source into two halves and argues one of them is dead.

The dead half is the community. Zig's code of conduct now bans AI on pull requests, issues, "or even comments," and Rizwan reports the reason as an ordering of values rather than a review-capacity problem: the core team "value contributors more than they do the contributions… the primary goal for reviewing PRs and things isn't to add new code, but it's to help grow new contributors who can become trusted over time. And AI assistance completely breaks that." curl's lead is quoted as saying the project is being DDoSed by AI-generated bug reports and is considering shutting down a bug bounty running for decades; tldraw closes all pull requests on sight "whether they're AI generated or not"; and GitHub shipped a switch to disable third-party pull requests altogether, "which is really sad because pull requests were the thing that made GitHub what it is today." His summary: "when I say that open source is dead, I mean some parts of it like the community — it's just not worth cultivating anymore, especially because building software is so cheap."

The trust collapse has a second driver: dependency risk. LiteLLM, a Python package taking "three and a half million downloads a day," was compromised for three hours when attackers used a GitHub app to steal its PyPI publishing tokens and shipped a release that installed a credential harvester for "your API keys, your SSH keys, your crypto keys" plus a backdoor for remote command execution. Nothing caught it: "the only reason this was even caught as quickly as it was was just pure luck because the malware had a bug in it where it would cause Cursor to crash if you ran the LiteLLM MCP server. And a security researcher noticed that." The blast radius he names is the inverted one — "a lot of the people using LiteLLM are like the enterprise customers and developers that have their own internal gateways," so the package is installed precisely where the most valuable keys are.

The surviving half is open weights, argued economically. He stacks demand-side evidence — an anonymous CFO report of accidentally spending $500 million on Claude in a single month "because they didn't set the usage limits on their thousands of employees on their Anthropic dashboard"; Uber's CTO reporting 95% of engineers on Claude, 70% of committed code from it, $2,000 per user per month, and the entire 2026 budget consumed in four months — against supply-side evidence that the labs are absorbing the difference: a SemiAnalysis experiment running long-horizon coding tasks until weekly limits ran out valued a $200 Claude plan at roughly $8,000 of API usage and a $200 Codex plan at roughly $14,000. He reads that as deliberate: "they're essentially going to subsidize this until they have as many engineers dependent on their tooling as possible with agents in their CI and background cloud agents and looping agents… and then inevitably the price gouging, once they've got you trapped where your developers can't work without the tools." The move of Anthropic and OpenAI from API businesses into the application layer is, on his reading, where "they can set these sorts of traps and build their moat for the day that these models inevitably become a commodity."

His counter-argument is that the trap does not hold, because quality has stopped being the binding constraint. "It doesn't matter how many features your CLI agent has, developers and businesses will just jump to whatever offers them the best value for their dollars," and open-weight models "are powerful enough where you don't always need the best one for all your work." The engineering version of the claim: "to get the best output from these models, it's more a problem of what context and tools you give the agent access to and less about its raw intelligence. With the right AI native development infrastructure, with project skills and rules, systems of verification and quality gates, even a mediocre model can produce similar results as a more intelligent model. It just might take more tokens… the intelligence is better placed in the system and guard rails around the model." Cline's own N-of-1 test is offered as evidence: skeptical of a benchmark putting GLM above Opus, they ran both on a real bug in the Cline repo; both fixed it, GLM spent twice the tokens at half the cost, and Opus finished faster with half the tool calls but "left a bunch of type errors and it broke the production build" while GLM cleaned up dead code and verified the build compiled before finishing. Coinbase is cited as the same trade at company scale: defaulting an internal LLM gateway to GLM and Kimi cut AI spend nearly in half while token usage kept growing.

The historical argument is Open Compute. Facebook's 2011 data-center work was not a competitive advantage because Amazon and Google had already built theirs, so they published the schematics and CAD files; the supply chain reorganized around a shared standard, manufacturers switched from expensive small custom runs to massive standardized ones, no vendor could charge a premium, and the price drop "saved them billions of dollars down the road." The lesson he draws is about adoption rather than generosity: "the industry will adopt and standardize on something that they can build on top of even if it isn't the best thing." He extends it with cost projections (nearly $3 trillion and over 100 gigawatts of new data-center capacity by 2030, roughly doubling global capacity; inference on a trillion-parameter model 90% cheaper by 2030) and the 2014 cloud price war as precedent — Google cutting compute 32% and storage 68%, AWS matching within days on its 42nd price cut, after which hyperscalers stopped competing on raw compute and moved to databases and serverless. The closing ask is to American labs: release open weights, not research, because published weights let others "extract the traces and train your copycat models on them more easily but not in a way that can leapfrog" — and because once the industry standardizes on foreign models, "there won't be a reason to switch back to GPT or Claude or Gemini no matter what the marginal improvements are." Cline launched an open-weights subscription plan three days before the talk, priced below direct API cost through volume discounts and inference-host partnerships.

## Provenance and Limits

- This is a founder keynote for a company that launched an open-weights subscription plan three days before the talk (16:01-16:24). Every economic argument in it — that closed-lab pricing is a subsidy trap, that open-weight quality is now sufficient, that inference cost will keep falling — supports that product. Nothing here is disinterested.
- The GLM-vs-Opus comparison is explicitly "an anecdotal experience": one bug, one repository (Cline's own), one run per model, no repetition, no independent scorer, and no version pinning for either model. It is a strong illustration and not a measurement; the wiki's [Re-Run One Remembered Hard Task on Each New Model](../concepts/re-run-one-remembered-hard-task-on-each-new-model.md) and [Don't Trust a Single Leaderboard for Model Selection](../concepts/do-not-trust-a-single-leaderboard-for-model-selection.md) pages both apply to it.
- Every third-party figure is relayed second-hand and none is independently verified here: the $500 million month is from "an anonymous report from a CFO at an unnamed company"; the Uber, Coinbase, and SemiAnalysis numbers are read off slides; the LiteLLM incident is recounted, not investigated.
- The 2030 projections ($3 trillion capex, 100+ gigawatts, 90% cheaper trillion-parameter inference) are given as "estimates" with no source named. Treat them as the shape of an argument rather than as forecasts to plan against.
- curl is led by Daniel Stenberg; "the CEO of curl" is the speaker's framing, and the bug-bounty decision is described as under consideration, not made.
- The claim that open weights let competitors "extract the traces and train your copycat models more easily, but not in a way that can leapfrog" is asserted without evidence and is a contested position, not a settled one. The same is true of the geopolitical framing around keeping the lead, which is advocacy rather than a technical finding.
- The Open Compute analogy is doing heavy lifting and has an unstated gap: schematics describe an artifact that manufacturers reproduce cheaply, while model weights *are* the R&D output, so "giving the design away" costs a lab something Facebook's server drawings did not cost it. Rizwan's hedge about traces and leapfrogging is where he addresses this, and it is the weakest-supported part of the talk.
