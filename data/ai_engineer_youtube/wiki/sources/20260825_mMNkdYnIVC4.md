## Summary

A seventeen-minute talk from James Zou on work at Together AI in collaboration with Stanford, built around one design claim and two artifacts that test it. The claim is a substitution: stop designing the agent's *procedure* and design its *place*. "The current paradigm of how people often are using or deploying AI agents… involves designing workflows that sort of tells the agents what to do… or how the agent should work. And it's typically done through a series of steps or prompts, tools, and instructions. In contrast, the way we imagine the environment is that the environment should really specify not how the agent should work, but really where the agent should work… and the environment then should provide a set of incentives and infrastructure for the agents and guardrails and resources so that agent can then flexibly work within that environment" (00:39-01:19). The thesis is explicitly conditional on capability trend: "as agents become more and more powerful… if we try to design workflows that often can limit the capabilities and creativity of the agents. Whereas if we properly design the environment, this can enables a lot more creativity and capabilities and intelligence for the agents to naturally emerge" (01:20-01:40). The closing slide states it as a three-stage progression — individual models and tools, then agents and harnesses and workflows, then environments defined as "a set of infrastructure and incentives that motivates the agents [to] solve more and more challenging [problems]" (15:39-16:20).

**Einstein Arena** is the first artifact: "one of the first environments that enables AI agents to be able to collaborate in the wild and to compete to really solve open-ended scientific problems" (02:02-02:18). It is agent-native at the door in both directions. Entry is documented for machines — "it's very easy for agents to just read the skills [doc] on our arena and be able to access the arena" — and humans are deliberately excluded: "it's actually also designed so that it's intentionally very hard for humans to enter the arena… you actually have to solve a little puzzle to prove that you're an AI agent in order to participate," while "any agent in the world can openly and freely participate" (02:18-02:47).

Problems pass a two-part curation filter. First, live human demand: "there's actually an existing community of human researchers that are interested in these problems. So, these are important problems for human scientists." Second, mechanical gradeability: "for each of these problems, we can actually create a well-defined and deterministic verifier to assess the quality of the solutions" (02:47-03:21). Inside a problem an agent gets three surfaces: a precise description; "a discussion forum where the agents can communicate… almost like a social network where the agents can actually communicate and talk to each other and ask for help or give recommendations"; and a leaderboard where "at any time they want, the agent can actually submit a solution… and because we have this verifier, we can actually then determine what is the quality of that solution and provide a score in real time," with the further affordance that agents "can also see other agents' solutions and download those solutions" (03:25-04:20). The design is explicit that both dynamics are wanted: "there's both a collaboration dynamics and also a competition dynamics in this arena… that's why I think this also sort of simulates how human researchers can compete and also collaborate" (04:20-04:36).

Launched "earlier this year, I think in March," within a few weeks agents on the arena held "new solutions to 11 problems that are of the best solutions that have ever been found… better than any previous human solutions or any solutions that we acquired using more specialized AI tools" (04:39-05:11). The worked example is the **kissing number problem** — "what is the maximum number of spheres that you can place around the central sphere so that these additional spheres do not overlap each other," trivial in one dimension (two) and two dimensions (six), open in higher ones and worked on "for the last several centuries" (05:12-06:17). In eleven dimensions the progression given is 440, then 582 in a year the speaker fumbles as the 1980s, which "stuck there for about 40 years, right, until 2022, where a mathematician is able to publish a new advance… 592 spheres," then "another breakthrough from DeepMind the following year that advances that to 593." Arena agents reached "604 spheres in 11 dimensions" in a few days (06:19-07:34). The application is named: denser sphere arrangements in high dimensions "creates the better coding systems including ways of like doing error correction codes for information transfer" (07:34-07:56).

The collaboration is presented as load-bearing rather than incidental: "this is a problem where not a single agent is able to solve by itself… GPT 5.5 or [Claude] models… can't really solve the problem by itself. So the collaboration among multiple agents is really critical," supported by "a lineage trace of how the agents are able to collaborate and then basically take each other's solutions and refine that and further optimize it to arrive at this breakthrough" (07:57-08:27). A forum screenshot shows one agent asking others "Have you tried… some of these [SDP] approaches?" and other agents reporting what they had already tried and found — "the information sharing on the forums on the arena is actually really important to help the agents to arrive at this solution together" (08:28-08:55).

**Retargeting the arena** is the second half of the artifact's value. For GPU kernels, "we use the same environment, right? Where the agents can compete and they also can collaborate and they see these leaderboards. And we basically change the back end instead of trying to verify the solutions to this mathematics problem, here we're basically trying to… compile and benchmark and test and verify the quality and the speed of the individual kernels… and then we'll provide a feedback to the agents in real time in the form of these leaderboards" (08:57-09:41). Here a second mechanism is added: "we also found it to be quite useful to have different agents with different personas… these different personas actually corresponds to different roles and priors that agents can actually have. So, for example, we have one agent that looks at… more of the profiling, another agent that tends to look at more of the memory consumptions, a third agent that looks at the precisions, the tensor computations. And these agents… across different personas, they can collaborate and compete on the arena to speed up the kernels" (09:43-10:12). The reported outcome is "sometimes over two-fold speed ups in some of these production kernels," shown for "things like page attention… for specific shapes, but we also have generalized this to many different shapes and different hardware types," and "these improved kernels created [and] designed by the agents are actually already used in production at Together AI" (10:14-10:54).

**DSGym** is the second artifact — "data science gym… a unified environment that we created for both for evaluating and for training data science agents" (10:57-11:19). It supplies a unified list of datasets and tasks reached "through a unified interface and through code execution," and unified infrastructure where "the agents can actually spin up many different Docker containers to test their data science algorithms and actually run them in parallel" (11:21-11:52). Its most transferable finding came from trying to reuse existing benchmarks: "many of the existing widely-used benchmarks actually have many problems. And one big problem is that they're actually very vulnerable to shortcuts." The audit is an ablation — across three popular data-science benchmarks, a red bar shows "what fraction of the benchmark the agents can actually solve without actually using the data sets themselves… just by reasoning or by doing other shortcuts without actually working with the underlying data sets," and "across many of these different benchmarks… sometimes up to 20 to 50% of the tasks can be solved without actually looking at any of the underlying data" (11:58-13:01).

DSGym's own tasks were curated along two tracks. For scientific analysis and discovery, "we actually went through recently published papers and then carefully curated data and then also tasks from those papers. And then we also had human scientists and experts to review each of those tasks." For predictive modeling, "go through all the different Kaggle competitions to look for some of the recent Kaggle competitions that are still open and where also you have high quality data sets and also high quality evaluations" (13:03-13:47). The result is "over a dozen different tasks" spanning "dozens of different scientific domains ranging from biology to physics to economics," with many data types and modalities (13:48-14:03). Frontier models "often are only still achieve less than 50% accuracy performance on the DSGym tasks. Right? So these are definitely not saturated benchmarks" (14:06-14:25). The same artifact is then a "training factory": "the gym itself will actually create all these execution verified trajectories, which means that these are trajectories generated by the agents that have been verified through actually executing the code from the agents. Right? So by generating these execution verified trajectories, then we are able to fine-tune sort of small open source models that actually now achieve… the best in class open source models in terms of solving these kind of data science tasks. And these models are small enough that you can actually run them locally on your laptops" (14:26-15:06).

## Provenance and Limits

- Everything is reported by the group that built the arena, the benchmark, the verifiers, and the fine-tuned models. No third-party replication is offered, and the references slide at the end is shown too briefly to read.
- **The eleven "best ever found" solutions are unenumerated.** No list of problems, no verification protocol beyond the phrase "deterministic verifier," and no description of the "more specialized AI tools" the arena results are said to beat. Only the kissing-number case is worked.
- **The kissing-number result is a construction, not a proof of optimality.** 604 is a lower bound — a placement of 604 non-overlapping spheres — and the talk does not claim otherwise, but it also does not state the distinction. Nothing is said about how the verifier certifies non-overlap under floating point, which is the whole load-bearing detail for a packing claim.
- **The historical progression is spoken with a visible fumble.** "In the 1980s… 440" and then "in 1980… 582" cannot both be right as stated; the speaker audibly gropes for the year ("And in I think 19 uh…"). The 2022 → 592 and DeepMind → 593 steps are stated cleanly. Read the pre-2022 dates as approximate.
- **"No single agent can solve it" is asserted without an ablation.** No single-agent baseline number is reported for the kissing-number problem, and the named models ("GPT 5.5 or [Claude] models") are given as an aside. The lineage figure shows that refinement happened, not that a solo agent given equal compute would have failed.
- **The kernel speedups have no evaluation methodology attached.** "Sometimes over 2x" on "specific shapes" against unnamed "previous state-of-the-art kernels," with no correctness tolerance, timing protocol, warmup/cache policy, hardware list, or aggregate distribution. The wiki's own [kernel evaluation page](../concepts/evaluate-generated-kernels-for-correctness-performance-and-benchmark-gaming.md) holds that large reported speedups are exactly the case that warrants suspicion; "already used in production at Together AI" is a deployment fact, which is stronger than a benchmark number in one way and weaker in another, since it reports adoption rather than a measured margin.
- **The persona mechanism is reported as "quite useful" with no comparison.** No run of identical agents against differentiated ones, no attribution of the speedups to persona diversity versus the arena mechanics versus the underlying models.
- **The shortcut audit's method is not stated.** Which three benchmarks, which agents, what the agent was shown in place of the data, and how "solved" was scored in the no-data condition are all unreported. The 20-50% range is read off a bar chart. The finding is nonetheless constructive and cheap to reproduce on your own benchmark, which is why it is recorded here as a method rather than as a result.
- **"Over a dozen tasks" spanning "dozens of scientific domains" does not parse arithmetically.** Most plausibly the task count refers to task *families* or datasets; recorded as spoken.
- **Using still-open Kaggle competitions as benchmark tasks is stated without addressing its hazards** — the ground truth is held by a third party, leaderboards move, and public discussion of an open competition is itself a leakage channel.
- **The fine-tuned models are unnamed and unquantified.** No base model, size, score, comparison set, or definition of "best in class open source." "Small enough to run on your laptop" is the only size signal.
- No cost, compute, wall-clock, or agent-count figures appear anywhere in the talk, including for the "few days" that produced 604.
- The incentive design is described only at the level of "leaderboard plus visible solutions." Nothing is said about how the arena resists an agent that submits noise at high volume, copies a leaderboard solution unchanged, or games the verifier — a gap that matters given the arena's stated openness to "any agent in the world."
- Caption artifacts resolved: "James So" is James Zou; "AI instant arena" / "AI instant" is Einstein Arena; "DS Gym" / "DSGM" / "DSG" is DSGym; "a cloud models" is Claude models; "over two two x" is over 2x; "page attention" is left as heard (most plausibly PagedAttention). Left flagged as unresolved: "these STP approaches" in the forum screenshot (08:41), most plausibly SDP — semidefinite programming, the standard technique for kissing-number bounds — but recorded as heard; and "But, with Alpha Zero, we know by having these agents able to collaborate actively" (07:18), where the sentence's subject should be the arena and the phrase is either a mangled reference to it or a stray reference to DeepMind's system.

## Notes

- The environment/workflow distinction is stated as a bet on the capability trend, not as a claim about today's models: workflows limit agents *as agents become more powerful* (01:20-01:30). That makes it falsifiable in a specific direction — the argument weakens, not strengthens, if the constraint is the model.
- The arena's three surfaces map to three different functions: the description is the task spec, the verifier is the reward, and the forum is the only channel carrying *negative* results ("we have tried these approaches and here are some of the things that we found," 08:44). A leaderboard alone would publish what worked; the forum is what publishes what did not.
- Downloadable solutions are what turn a leaderboard from a ranking into a substrate — the lineage trace exists because agents could take each other's submissions and refine them (08:15-08:25).
- The kernel retarget is the strongest evidence for the environment thesis because it holds everything else fixed: same arena, same leaderboard, same social mechanics, only the verifier changed (08:57-09:41).
- The shortcut audit generalizes past data science. Its form is: identify the input your benchmark claims to be testing use of, remove it, re-run, and treat the residual score as the benchmark's floor. It requires no new tasks and no new grader.
- DSGym is presented as eval and training factory in one artifact, with execution verification as the filter that makes the trajectories usable as training data (14:26-15:06).
