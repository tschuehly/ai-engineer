## Summary

Dmitry Kuchin argues that reliable AI applications need evals built from product and business outcomes rather than generic data-science metrics. The talk recommends creating scenario-specific criteria from real user tasks, generating persona and wording variants, inspecting individual failures instead of averages, and using the resulting benchmark to compare prompts, models, RAG designs, agentic approaches, and guardrails.

## Notes

- A proof of concept can work half the time while the remaining reliability work becomes hard because model behavior is nondeterministic and changes to prompts, models, code, logic, or data can shift outcomes unexpectedly, 01:51-02:43.
- Generic metrics such as groundedness, factuality, and bias may not answer whether the latest change improved the app for users; in the support-bot example, human escalation rate was the more important business outcome, 02:49-04:17.
- The recommended eval design starts with real-world scenarios and reverse-engineers very specific criteria from product experience and business outcomes rather than measuring generic averages, 04:20-05:01.
- LLMs such as o3 can help generate user questions, personas, and answer criteria from source materials, including variants where the same intent is expressed differently by different personas, 05:24-08:22.
- Eval failures should be inspected at the case level because a failure can mean the test is wrong or the solution needs a change to the model, logic, prompt, or data, 08:25-09:37.
- Prompt changes can fix one test while breaking behavior that used to work, so frequent benchmark runs are needed to catch regressions, 09:37-10:19.
- Once a benchmark baseline is trusted, teams can compare alternative models, GraphRAG versus simpler retrieval, and agentic versus simpler logic while accounting for time and inference cost, 10:47-11:45.
- Different application types need different eval mechanics: support bots may need LLM-as-judge checks, text-to-SQL or text-to-graph systems can use mock databases with known answers, classifiers can use exact rubric matches, and guardrails should include questions that should not be answered or should be answered differently, 11:46-13:23.
