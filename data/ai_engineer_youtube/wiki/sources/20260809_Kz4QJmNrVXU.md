## Summary

Matt Dailey (CEO and founder of Ref) names **velocity sickness** — "the stress caused by sudden output increases thanks to AI," whose result is "output without impact" — and argues it is one problem with four faces at the team level: too many PRs to merge (merge conflicts, "merge queue breaks down"), work sprinting in too many directions at once, the ritual of declaring **agent bankruptcy**, and, the one he calls most important, critical decisions being made by agents. The generalizing anecdote is not from engineering: a newsletter writer with an agentic pipeline good enough to amplify their own voice rather than flatten it — "very much not slop" — told him "I'm basically writing a book every week," and when asked whether the audience was reading a book every week answered "No, they're probably not." Those pages go unread; the parallel he draws is that software teams are producing at a rate that no longer converts into anything landing. His diagnosis is structural rather than motivational: the pre-AI process was plan → implement → polish, and every tool we own — "our IDE, our workhorse" — was built for the implementation and polish of one person working heads down. The agent now owns implementation ("arguably should not even be on this slide cuz it's not our human work anymore"), which leaves two human parts, both "creative and collaborative," and promotes planning into a distinct **decision layer** whose work is to "figure out what decisions matter and then make those decisions and then get out of the way while the agents fill in the rest." The skill that follows is self-observation: "what gear am I in? Am I using the appropriate tools for the gear… [for] what I'm trying to accomplish right now." A tool for that layer, he argues, is "built for docs and not chat," because chats are "the relic of building for implementation" — "default isolated and ephemeral and… brain off" — so the decisions made inside one evaporate while only the code survives, and the agent's "this is the recommended option" prompt gets answered with "great. I don't even think about this. I'll just hit that one and we keep going." He explicitly excludes two adjacent things: plan mode, which is "largely… a rich chat message" and still "in this isolated ephemeral environment," and spec-driven development, which "operate[s] at the like product level [and] is a little far away from the engineering reality"; he wants "something in the middle." The mechanism underneath is a state/action split — "separate the agent as the action and the doc as the state" — so that "every agent is largely stateless and starts from this… same place," several agents can start from identical context, the team can read what decisions were made, and "you're ultimately doing context engineering in this doc." He rejects reconstructing decisions after the fact — "we don't have to have like some LLM summarizing it and maybe picking the wrong things later on" — in favor of pulling them out up front into a durable decision log. The reported tell that it is working is counterintuitive: "people start to plan and then not implement their plan… this is actually like a really good sign," because it means ideas are being prioritized after exploration rather than before, a shift "from code velocity to idea velocity" that escapes "prototype gravity" — building something, getting excited to ship it, and committing to "one path of the idea maze." Mapped back onto the four problems: the review point moves earlier, so "the hardest part of any code review… what actually matters here" is already agreed; realignment is cheap "before someone has spent even a day in AI going deep on some idea building a prototype"; agent bankruptcy "is just not a thing because you've made your agent stateless… If you need to rebuild your human context, you just read the doc"; and "humans own the decisions… that's how we retain ownership of our software and our products." Secondary benefits named: more and easier parallel agents, a durable decision log, and more collaboration — "the future of engineering is multiplayer… multiplayer by default sooner than we think." Three closing actions: notice which of the two gears (plan, polish) you are in and whether your tool serves it; treat the plan as "a portal to the software system" and ask it to show you what matters; and hand a plan to a teammate before you hand it to an agent.

## Notes

- The problem Ref works on, stated first: "individual engineers are going really fast with AI, but the team as a whole is not. And we're working to help close that gap." (00:22-00:31)
- Symptom 1 — PR volume: "too many PRs to merge. This is the like classic first problem you hit when you start adopting AI as an engineer… you push up those PRs and you're like, 'There's no way I can merge all these.'… Merge conflicts, merge queue breaks down, things get bad." (01:36-01:55)
- Symptom 2 — divergence, at two scales: individually "you have a bunch of agents doing different things. You're trying to remember who's doing what and your brain gets fried"; organizationally engineers are "picking up things and running in a certain direction… Maybe some are bumping into each other… you're not moving cohesively with focus because you're just sprinting in all sorts of directions." (01:59-02:25)
- Symptom 3 — agent bankruptcy: "you have like your 12 terminals open… at the end of the day you're like, 'Yeah, I did a lot of work'… Next morning you come back and it's like walking into just a room of strangers. Like, who are these people? What are they doing here?… no problem. They're agents so you just get rid of them and start over again." The cost is doubled: "it feels like you're doing a lot of work but you're doing the same work and you're spending tokens twice," which organizationally is "your team not being efficient with both their time and their token resources." (02:26-03:13)
- Symptom 4, called "the most important one" — ceded decisions: "if you as an engineer are letting an agent make a critical decision, you are [ceding] control of your code. You are no longer the owner of that code. The agent is. And if you imagine that at scale at your company… you no longer own the product." (03:16-03:43)
- The term: "velocity sickness… the stress caused by sudden output increases thanks to AI. Um it affects individuals or teams. And the result is output without impact." The felt signature is the mismatch: "we're moving really fast. This should feel great… but for some reason it doesn't feel awesome." (03:53-04:26)
- The newsletter anecdote in full shape: a non-engineer whose pipeline handles ideas, research, exploration, cohesion and editorial "to make sure it's in their voice," described as "very very much not slop… using agents to amplify their own voice… in a way that's like very impressive," who then says "I'm basically writing a book every week." Asked "is your audience reading a book every week?" — "No, they're probably not." The pages "are going unread." (04:38-06:04)
- Before AI: "planning up front… sit down and build… implement… iterative… exploring. But largely we're sitting down building in isolation… And at the end we sort of polish it up and ship it out the door." The tools matched: "all our history of coding tools were built for this style of work. Um our IDE, our workhorse… it was built for implementation and polish to be done by an individual, to be heads down building as a software engineer writing code." (06:39-07:27)
- After AI, the middle drops out: planning, then "an agent takes that idea and… implements it. This is like arguably should not even be on this slide cuz it's not our human work anymore," then polish — "we take back that thing the agent has made for us and we like hold it in our hands and we say, is this what I wanted?" The two survivors are "the two creative and collaborative parts of our work as engineers." (07:30-08:20)
- What planning actually is, in his description: "I have this vague idea. I have this complex system. I need to understand the contours of this system, apply this idea… And I need to pull out what's relevant and express my taste as an engineer. As to like, where do I want this system to go?" And the durable form: "we're always going to have this thing where we have a group of people responsible for managing a complex system and it's sort of deciding the future of that system. And that's this planning work." (08:21-09:12)
- The layer and the gear: "This is the decision layer. This is where we're thinking through what are the key decisions, doing that like craft of engineering… and expressing our taste as engineers, ultimately a different thing than implementation… it's a different gear as an engineer. The skill now is what gear am I in? Am I using the appropriate tools for the gear that I'm… trying to accomplish right now." (09:14-09:55)
- The one-line prescription, which he flags as dense: "It'd be a tool built for docs and not chat. And there's that's like a short sentence. There's a lot to unpack here though. So I'm going to spend a lot of time on this slide." (09:56-10:11)
- Why chats: "The problem with chats is that they are the relic of building for implementation. So they're… default isolated and ephemeral and… brain off. They're made to build things and get stuff done. And that's not really the same type of work we're doing at the decision layer." (11:11-11:35 — note the slide order; the critique bracket runs 10:11-11:12)
- What is lost inside one: "decisions are being made in that… chat that are not shared with my team, that are going to disappear as… I'm going to result in some code being output where those important decisions are not being made clear and shared with the team." (10:38-10:55)
- The in-chat approval collapse, in his own words: "the agent [is] saying, Okay, this is what I want to do. Is that okay? Let's go. Or… it'll ask you a question and it'll be like, you know, this is the recommended option and then you're like, great. I don't even think about this. I'll just hit that one and we keep going." (10:55-11:10)
- What docs do instead: "they're meant to… bring forward the key decisions. This is what our work is now. Our work now is figure out what decisions matter and then make those decisions and then get out of the way while the agents fill in the rest." (11:21-11:35)
- The alignment analogy: "Working in Docs is the classic way we would create alignment. If you think back to being a manager or a lead on a team before AI, if your team was… struggling with alignment, you would not tell them like, 'Let's go all work in Slack DMs'… You'd say, 'Let's… bring forward key decisions, align on them, spend time on these… find a way that we can really spend time getting our decisions right.'" (11:35-12:03)
- Two things he is explicitly not describing. Plan mode: "That's a great tool… but it's largely a rich chat message where the agent is saying, 'Hey, here's a like better visualization of what I'm trying to express to you.' Yeah, that's great. But it's still in this isolated ephemeral environment." And spec-driven development: "where we just define the behaviors, we operate at the like product level, is a little far away from the engineering reality." He wants "something in the middle" — "more durable, more shared, more long-lived that you and your team are spending time on." (12:03-12:52)
- The portal framing: "The engineering reality is that I need to understand my system and have a tool that… helps me understand that system and lay out those key decisions in a… technical sense. The way I like to conceptualize this is as the portal to the software system, where you are like Tony Stark and you're like, 'Show me what matters'… 'Pull out the bits that are relevant.' And AI is amazing at finding things that are related to other things… Organize the pieces in the way you want to represent how you want the system to grow." (12:52-13:25)
- The mechanism, called "the big conceptual flip": "we're pulling out the state. So, when you're living and working in a long-lived session with an agent, there's this implicit context being built up… over that work, and that's great… But… you're also doing actions and it's not shared. What you want is to separate the agent as the action and the doc as the state. And so, you can spawn new agents that have the same context or starting from the same place that are able to collaborate and work on the same piece of context and state. You're ultimately doing context engineering in this doc, so that every agent is largely stateless and starts from this… same place." (13:25-14:10)
- The consequence for the team: "your team and yourself can look into this and understand what actually is in here. What are the decisions that are being made… and have a clear understanding of what the key decisions are." And the summary form: "your core atom of your work is a doc rather than a chat." (14:10-14:30)
- The reported first-order effect: "the first thing we see happen actually is that people start to plan and then not implement their plan. Uh and this is actually like a really good sign. Because what that means is they're thinking through ideas… 'I have this vague thought. Don't just give me some code. Don't go off and… build it for me, but let… me understand the idea that I'm talking about.' And then they have a bunch of these and then some of them are getting built and some of them aren't. So, that means they're prioritizing the ideas that after they've explored them are the ones that are worth building." (14:33-15:10)
- The reframe and the failure it avoids: "you're shifting from code velocity to idea velocity… rather than… getting stuck in prototype gravity where we build something and we're so excited to just ship that thing and we're going down like one path of the idea maze, we can now… more effectively explore that whole maze and find that gold that's around the corner." (15:12-15:48)
- Mapping back — PRs: "We've moved the review point earlier into the process. So, we're aligning on the key decisions up front. That means the code review is easier because the hardest part of any code review is… what actually matters here… If you move that earlier, we've aligned on that, the code review becomes much simpler." (16:03-16:27)
- Mapping back — directions: "we're aligning early as a team and individually, I'm… understanding what I'm working on across many agents… it's easy before someone has spent even a day in AI going deep on some idea building a prototype, we can talk about it early and make sure we're aligned on where we [are] actually taking the system." (16:27-16:54)

- Mapping back — ownership: "humans own the decisions. Like, that's what we're solving for. Humans need to own the decisions. That's how we retain ownership of our software and our products and make it like a true expression of what we're trying to create in the world." (17:10-17:27)
- Secondary benefits: "when you have this state extracted and you're working from a shared context, you get more parallel agents. It's easier to work with parallel agents." A really great solution to that is let's pull out all the decisions up front and agree to them and put them in a place that's durable so that we don't have to have like some LLM summarizing it and maybe picking the wrong things later on." - The collaboration claim: "we're doing more work that is creative, which means we should be collaborating more as engineers. I think the future of engineering is multiplayer. It's going to be… multiplayer by default sooner than we think… everything's moving faster than ever. There's a pressure like everyone's company feels existential, small or big. You need to deliver. And the way we get through that as humans is by working together. And we need tools that help us do that." (18:05-18:41)
- Action 1: "think of your work in terms of planning and polish. So, recognizing that there are two gears I'm working in. - Action 2: "treat your plan as a portal to the software system… as this powerful, malleable tool to say like, what matters to me for what I'm working on right now? And asking it to show that to you so that you can make the best decisions possible." (19:16-19:33)
- Action 3: "share a plan… don't just write the plan, give it to your agent, and have them implement it. Give it to someone on your team. This is like, I feel like very unnatural for a lot of people. We… think we know what's going on. But it's always valuable. You have smart teammates. They have great context in their heads. You should tap into that. They will give you good feedback." (19:33-19:56)

## Provenance and Caveats

- This is a vendor talk by a founder-CEO, and the prescription is the product category: Ref is introduced in the first sentence as working on "individual engineers are going really fast with AI, but the team as a whole is not" and in the last as "a tool built for the decision layer." Every claim about what happens when teams adopt the doc-centered workflow comes from the vendor observing its own users.
- Nothing in the talk is measured. There are no before/after numbers, no team sizes, no adoption figures, no token or cycle-time comparisons, and no named customers. "The first thing we see happen actually is that people start to plan and then not implement their plan" is the only usage observation offered and it carries no denominator.
- The organizing anecdote is second-hand, about newsletter writing rather than software, and the parallel to engineering workflows is asserted rather than demonstrated ("I think it parallels our engineering workflows a lot," 04:46-04:49).
- "Declaring agent bankruptcy is just not a thing because you've made your agent stateless" is the strongest claim in the talk and the least supported. Reading the doc is itself a cost, the doc can be wrong or stale, and nothing is said about what happens when the doc grows to the size of the chat it replaced or when several people edit it concurrently.
- The talk names no cost for the new layer: no ownership model for the docs, no versioning or retirement policy against the code they describe, no answer to plan-document rot (which this wiki records from another source at [Retire Completed Planning Docs Before They Become Agent Doc Rot](../concepts/retire-completed-planning-docs-before-they-become-agent-doc-rot.md)), and no time budget for the doc work itself.
- The exclusions of plan mode and spec-driven development are characterizations, not comparisons: no plan-mode workflow or spec-driven toolchain is named, and no evidence is given that "something in the middle" outperforms either.
- No models, harnesses, protocols, formats, or commands appear anywhere in the talk, so nothing here is version-bound but nothing is directly reproducible either.
- A ~20-minute conference slot with no Q&A and no demo.
