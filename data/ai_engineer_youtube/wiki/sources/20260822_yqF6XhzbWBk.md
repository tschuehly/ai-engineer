## Summary

A twenty-minute argument that the standard remedy for generator errors — put an LLM judge after the generator — fails on exactly the errors that matter, and a proposed replacement. Fox is a medical doctor now at Composo, "where we build AI evaluation systems for high-stakes domains" (01:07). The domain is ambient clinical scribes, already "in about a third of US practices and climbing" (02:25). The opening case is a note that is entirely correct and still dangerous: the patient's jaw ache on chewing never reached the page, and a new headache over 50 with jaw claudication is giant cell arteritis, which "untreated… can take her sight within days" (00:35). The talk's structure is four questions — what is going wrong, why, why the checkers we build do not catch it, and what to do instead.

The mechanism claim is the reusable part. Verification is asymmetric only for the easy half of this task: spotting what differs between transcript and note is cheap, while deciding *which* differences matter is "harder than writing that plausibly good general note in the first place. Because that standard of good was never written down anywhere that the judge can read it" (11:20). Fox built the strongest judge he had seen — frontier model, faithfulness rubric with worked pass/fail examples, rubric auto-optimization, deterministic concept counting — ran it over the same notes, and "one in five of those clean passes still had some sort of serious error buried in it. And often that was an omission" (09:49). His conclusion is that such a judge "is not blind, it just can't tell what counts" (10:34), so it is "not a safety net… a second silent failure that just nods along with the first" (10:54). The proposal is to stop trying to specify the standard: keep it as examples — past judgments, expert corrections, references — and retrieve the ones bearing on each output into the judge's context, run as a loop of discover, capture, calibrate (13:19-13:36).

Evidence limits, which matter here because the talk's conclusion favors its speaker's product. The base rates (1 in 20 serious harm, ~1 in 5 important omission, >1 in 10 hallucination) are attributed to "the largest real-world study of these notes" with no name, size, or citation given (02:03-02:25). The error catalogue comes from notes Fox's team generated across "three of the best production Ambient scribes on the market" last week — unnamed, and generated by the team rather than sampled from real deployments (03:29-03:50). The scatter plot's y-axis ("whether a strong automated check catches it") is his own assessment, and the closing three-judge comparison is his own dataset, narrated off a bar chart with no absolute numbers, no denominators, and no inter-rater or ground-truth procedure stated; the winning arm is his company's method, and he qualifies it himself as "performing a lot better on this specific data set" (17:27-17:46). The retrieval design is described but never specified: no index, embedding, retrieval-depth, cost, or latency figure appears, and nothing addresses what happens when the retrieved precedent is itself wrong.

Caption artifacts corrected where quoted: "giant cell arthritis" for *giant cell arteritis*; "GPA" for *GEPA*; "verifies law" for *verifier's law*; "detona stick" for *deterministic*; "Composure" for *Composo*; "same English emission" for *omission*; "chemical notes" for *clinical notes*; "a drop to no" for *a dropped "no"*.

## Notes

- The opening case is chosen to make one point: the note contains no error. "Nothing in the note is technically wrong. The dangerous part is what isn't there," which is why "the dangerous failures are often the ones that actually look completely fine" (00:35-00:54).
- The contrasting loud case — a sore-throat consultation written up with chest pain, suspected angina, diabetes medications never taken, and "an address for a hospital that doesn't exist," ending in an invitation to diabetic eye screening weeks later — is used to dismiss loud failures as the easy class: "obviously, these kind of crazy ones someone notices, but it's those quiet ones that sit in the record uncalled that are the most challenging and can actually do a lot more damage" (01:19-02:03).
- The error map is a scatter plot with a deliberate axis choice: "Each dot is an error colored by type. Left to right, how much it matters. Bottom to top, whether a strong automated check catches it. And that split is the point. A handful up top get caught. But almost everything sits below the line" (03:50-04:11). Plotting detectability against consequence, rather than counting errors by type, is the presentation move that makes the argument; the population of dots is his own generated set.
- Two worked generation errors. An over-inference: asked whether the headache came on suddenly or gradually, the patient says she does not know, "it just happened," and the note records "abrupt sudden onset" — a red flag for a brain bleed. "She never said it. The model decided it. And now that one word drives the whole workup" (04:11-04:56). And a reversed decision: patient and doctor agree to try antibiotics instead of tests, and the note says "arrange tests today." "It kept the plan that they talked out of, not the one they chose" (04:56-05:19).
- Transcription-layer errors are named and then set aside as not the focus: Humalog heard as Humulin ("two insulins on completely different timelines, so swapping them could crash a blood sugar"), hyperthyroidism becoming hypothyroidism, and a dropped "no" turning "no evidence of cancer" into "evidence of cancer" (05:42-06:05).
- With a perfect transcript the generation failures reduce to three: "it adds something that was never said, it changes something that was, or it omits something that should be there." The blatant version of each is easy; "the hard part in all three is the same. It's telling whether that thing that was added or changed or dropped actually matters" (06:05-06:49).
- The three properties of the judgment that decides materiality: tacit ("your domain experts have it, but they can't fully write it down"), contextual ("the same detail is critical in one note, noise in the next"), and moving ("the model changes, guidelines change, two good doctors disagree, different hospitals have different definitions. So, there's no fixed target to write down") (07:15-07:34).
- The judge architecture he built and rejected is described precisely enough to reproduce: transcript, note, and context in front of the judge; "a detailed rubric for faithfulness with worked pass and fail examples"; the rubric "maybe auto-optimized with GEPA or something like that"; and "some deterministic NLP to sort of count up medical concepts that are differing between the two" (09:02-09:24).
- The title's figure does not appear in the talk. Nothing in the transcript states a count of notes, and the only sourcing statement for the error catalogue is "we generated a load of notes across them last week" (03:29-03:50) — generated by the team across three unnamed scribes, not sampled from deployed production traffic. The "847" and "production" framing comes from the video title and description; the spoken talk supports neither the number nor the provenance.
- The generalization out of healthcare names two non-clinical instances and no others: "the contract review that misses the clauses that change the deal, the support agent that promises a refund you don't offer" — both cases where the output is superficially correct and materially wrong. "It's watched, if at all, by a judge with no taste for what matters in your domain" (18:08-18:31).
- The closing instruction is the smallest starting step rather than the architecture: "the easiest place to start is your experts leaving free-form comments on real outputs. That's the raw material for everything else" (18:31-18:52).
