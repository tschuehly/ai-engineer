## Summary

A fireside Q&A with Joe Reeve (growth org, ElevenLabs) about the "talk to a statue" app — point your phone at a statue, an OpenAI deep-research call identifies it and generates historical context plus a voice description, the ElevenLabs Voice Design API synthesizes a matching voice, an ElevenLabs agent spins up, and a phone call starts, all in ~30 seconds. He built it in two hours one Sunday with a single one-shot Cursor prompt; posting it (and especially reposting the vibe-coding angle) drove 50,000 → 1.5M impressions and inbound from museums, auction houses, and travel platforms (one museum CEO who "had a team of 10 working on this for a year" tracked down his WhatsApp number). The durable lessons are mostly not about the app: that gluing managed, already-scalable APIs collapses the prototype-to-production gap so the differentiator becomes the integration glue and the distribution story; that auto-web-research content is a demo shortcut while production needs curator-designed narratives grounded in first-party data; and a long, rich discussion of voice as an interface — its low output information-density, the friction of interrupting and being interrupted, push-to-talk and "agent-wants-to-interject" cues, editing the agent transcript on barge-in using audio timestamps, and embedding voice agents in physical objects so the technology doesn't feel tacked on.

## Notes

- The pipeline: take a photo → OpenAI deep research identifies the statue and generates historical knowledge + a description of what the statue's voice would sound like → ElevenLabs Voice Design API ("really underutilized API where you put in a description of a voice and it generates something that matches") → create an ElevenLabs agent → start the phone call, end to end in ~30 seconds. 02:38-03:10, 01:57
- Built in two hours on a Sunday in Cursor from a single one-shot prompt; the prompt was published openly via the ElevenLabs blog and attached to the launch tweet. 03:25-03:45
- Virality mechanics: posted Tuesday → 50,000 impressions; the *next-day repost* framed as "I vibe coded this in two hours — not a brag, just this is interesting" → 1.5M impressions and inbound from three museum groups, Tripadvisor competitors, Bonhams/Christie's; one museum CEO found his WhatsApp number and called. 03:50-04:40
- Glue thesis: "pretty much all of what I've done is stitched together existing APIs which are designed to scale … there's no way I can make a dent in the API volume even if this goes gangbusters," so user management can be one-shot with Supabase (logins, magic links) and the agents/voice-design APIs do the heavy lifting; "the glue pieces and telling a good story about the glue is in part the most important thing of the project rather than solving hard technical problems." 05:55-06:50
- Evals / production content: take-a-photo-and-get-web-research-back is *not* the long-term solution — the important, hard part is going to curators to design the actual narrative ("let's not just take random things you found from Google"); museums see their core IP as their databases and often expose public APIs (the V&A has a public API), so production grounds in authoritative first-party data, not ad-hoc deep research. 07:00-07:45
- Voice-as-interface problems (most solvable): interaction is currently *binary* (voice OR another modality); "generative UI plus voice" — multimodal conversations where the app shows what the agent is extracting and you interact in parallel — is still underexplored. 10:30-10:55
- "The thing you talk to is often not the thing doing the work": you'd rather speak to a halfway-house product-manager agent that triggers your coding agent than talk to the coding agent directly. 10:50-11:15
- People are too polite to interrupt voice agents; learning to interrupt aggressively makes the experience much better, "but I don't know how to give people permission to interrupt." 11:30-11:55
- Voice's value is not always information — it can be companionship that "lessens the loneliness feel" and makes you more motivated to keep tinkering, even when you'd *learn* more from a diagram or text; different modalities trigger different feelings. 21:00-21:30
- Concise voice answers sound *rude* (whereas a concise text answer doesn't offend), so terseness is a worse default in speech. 21:55-22:05
- "Skim listening" is missing: forward/backward controls to jump half a sentence, scrolling forward in *concepts* not sentences (the Claude app shows higher-level sections you can tap into, different from what you hear). 22:00-23:00
- Push-to-talk / hold-to-talk (whisper-flow style) is a reliable workaround because pure voice interruptibility detection is unreliable — augment the audio stream with another cue. 25:30-25:45
- "Agent wants to interject" cue: a light nudge (a circle appearing) signaling the agent has something to add and *which topic* it wants to raise — richer than a human conversation because the topic is signaled before the interruption. 25:44-26:10
- Implementing a background interjecting agent: rather than a tool call, asynchronously re-read the running transcript over and over asking "do you have anything to add?" 27:30-28:00
- Barge-in transcript editing: platforms usually generate the full text then start TTS, and a mid-speech interruption just *appends* the user's message after the full generated text; but you have audio timestamps and know how far playback got, so you can edit the transcript to truncate at the interruption point and make the model "forget it even generated more text." Agents are often treated as black boxes, but the conversation transcript is an editable internal. 28:40-29:15
- Embodied / ambient voice (remove the screen so it doesn't feel "tacked on"): commissioning a physical statue with a microphone, speaker, and phone inside so you talk directly to it; a K6 red phone booth on floor three where you pick up the handset and talk to "Sir Michael Caine." 14:18-14:50
- Voice persona from provenance: an inanimate object's voice should reflect its history (a rock quarried in China, carved in Vietnam, 200 years in a British museum among tourists → a blended accent); "what should a lift sound like" becomes a real question as people start saying "I want to go to this floor please" to elevators. 07:55-09:45
- Distribution/social-virality template: the Facebook Instant Games API (now deprecated) bundled social-gaming primitives (get friends, leaderboards, key-value storage, rewarded/interstitial ads, async/await) — instrumenting a £15 Fruit Ninja clone with it produced 15M users overnight (tested in low-value ad regions like Vietnam first); something with those social primitives is likely the template that wins "social vibe coding." 17:56-19:10
- Video craft for distribution (tools are easy now; "getting your word out" is the differentiator): front-load a hook because median view time is only 6–12 seconds before drop-off; captions help a lot; music is "massively underrated" — generate it with ElevenLabs music generation, either make the video first then try genres or generate the music first and match speech to its vibe; he edited on CapCut on his phone (~20–25 min) with a £200 DJI Bluetooth lapel mic, noting phone editing is faster and cheaper than desktop (80/20). 30:30-32:31
- ElevenLabs platform context: audio-AI foundation-model company (TTS, transcription, first commercially-legal AI music with licensed training data, sound effects, voice design); ElevenLabs Agents is a fully managed agents deployment platform that supports knowledge files and MCP calling but not a first-class "skills" concept; "open claw"-style phone-number call-back interaction patterns are common with ElevenLabs. 00:55-01:35, 12:30-13:30
- Vibe coding hasn't gone consumer-mainstream: tools like Lovable still effectively build B2B SaaS (Supabase + standard components); at vibe-coding events people who've never coded ask for "wacky" things and the LLM just tries them (no engineer steering them to a hamburger menu or accordion), and the field is waiting for its "Instagram-filter / TikTok moment" (Spielworks is a TikTok-swiping mobile vibe-coding-for-games app). 18:56-20:00, 33:00-35:00
