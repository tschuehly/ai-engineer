## Summary

Vivek Muppalla, who runs engineering at Hippocratic AI, walks the production architecture behind a product that places outbound clinical phone calls to patients — 200M+ conversations across 60+ health systems with, he says, zero significant safety incidents and an 8.5/10 patient satisfaction rating. The talk's spine is a refusal of the usual tradeoff: models accurate enough for clinical conversation take tens of seconds to answer, and models fast enough for a phone call are not clinically safe, so the team built a vertically integrated stack instead of composing a generic voice pipeline. Three parts are reusable outside healthcare. First, their ASR is a decoder-only audio LLM that is fed the conversation so far and the domain context alongside the audio, so a drug name resolves against a finite candidate list rather than an unbounded one and prosody survives the audio→token projection. Second, the "Polaris" constellation runs 31 models per conversation — one model holding the thread plus 30 specialists — in parallel, kept inside the latency budget because each specialist first makes a cheap "do I even need to speak?" check and short-circuits, with asynchronous verifiers checking tool-call parameters and responses in the background. Third, he does the eval arithmetic that makes 99% unacceptable: at 10,000 calls a day a 1% error is 100 people sent to the wrong appointment, and catching a 1% failure rate statistically needs ~450 tests (~1,900 to observe it ten times), which is why 7,000+ trained clinicians run continuous evaluation alongside synthetic data and outputs are graded on the harm scale used for humans.

## Notes

- Framing: healthcare has always been built on scarcity — "not enough clinicians, not enough time, not enough money" — "and hence the word triage." What flips the math is clinically safe AI conversation at falling cost: "you don't have to have calls just for the sickest 5%, but you can call everyone." (00:40-01:32)
- Scale claims: 200M clinical interactions, zero significant safety incidents, 60+ health systems, 8.5/10 patient satisfaction. Company principles — do no harm, patient first, access for all — are taken as an oath by every employee, not just posted on a wall. (01:49-02:31)
- The recorded demo shows the safety-relevant behaviors concretely: the agent discloses it is an AI when asked; asks the patient to spell a medication and resolves "P-r-o-z-o-s-i-n" to *prazosin*; reads vitals back for confirmation ("121 over 45 and your heart rate is 91, right?"); detects that a med the patient was told to stop (nifedipine) was still taken and defers to the primary care doctor; and escalates to a live nurse on shortness of breath. (02:44-05:10)
- The core tradeoff, stripped of audio complexity, is two axes — intelligence and latency. Clinically safe models "take tens of seconds to respond, sometimes over a minute," which is "completely useless" for a two-way telephone conversation; fast models "are just not at the same level of clinical accuracy." Reaching the top-right corner required building the stack "ground up bit by bit." (05:18-06:11)
- Why generic stacks lose: of the three usual voice architectures (ensemble, cascaded, speech-to-speech realtime), none scored where they needed on the benchmarks that actually matter for their workflows — *lab results check* and *IVR navigation* — "things that you don't typically hear about or see in the most common benchmarks," where they need >99% accuracy. (06:35-07:21)
- Latency/intelligence flywheel: every optimization "buys back some latency, and we just don't bank that latency" — the freed budget is spent packing more intelligence into the conversation, then more optimization follows. "What seems like a tug-of-war between latency and intelligence for us is a compounding flywheel." (07:25-07:53)
- Polaris, the "constellation architecture," is three stages: a system that hears (speech detection models covering bilingual switching, background-noise detection, contextual understanding), a brain that reasons (31 models), and a system that talks back (custom personality, voice, HD quality, and a clinical documentation engine that feeds the health system). The whole round trip has to be fast. (07:55-09:03)
- Rationale for the constellation: "we see a singular model being as like one point of failure, and that's just unacceptable for a patient conversation." The architecture buys redundancy and safety, and "the patient never can tell the difference." (09:06-09:22)
- ASR reality check: benchmarks are recorded in quiet rooms but the real world is loud, and "most of what looks like model reasoning failures end up actually being model mishearing things" — the Spanish *sí* transcribed as the letter C, an Arabic drug name at >30% word error rate. (09:31-10:03)
- Their ASR is a decoder-only audio LLM: audio *plus* the conversation so far *plus* domain knowledge. Encoder is an open-source Whisper V3 large turbo fine-tuned on millions of clinical conversations; a conformer projector compacts audio into tokens the language model understands while maintaining prosody, "so the model hears not just the what, but also the how." (10:05-11:04)
- Context supplied includes the patient's medications, the task at hand (e.g. filling a form) and the policy, so a mentioned medication is resolved against "a finite list" instead of "an infinite list of medications." Contextual biasing also happens at training time: millions of synthetic patients with addresses and phone numbers are fed into training, so an utterance like "1100 Boulevard" — "ripe for phonetic garbage for most ASR systems" — transcribes correctly. (11:06-12:11)
- Single-word patient answers are the catastrophic case: "a *now* becomes a *no*, or a *five* becomes a *fine*." When the patient said only one word, a secondary round of scoring runs using the whole conversation as context. Combined, these bring the medical word error rate down by over 50% versus off-the-shelf models, at a P99 latency they measure as 3x faster than other systems. (12:14-13:01)
- How 31 concurrent models stay inside the latency budget: they all run in parallel, but "every single model does a really quick check to see if they even need to say something on this conversation" — the specialist that has nothing to add short-circuits. That is the synchronous half; asynchronous models run verification in the background. (13:07-13:50)
- Specialist example: when the patient mentions ibuprofen or another medication, the med-engine specialist raises its hand and passes context to the main model, which — already holding the goal and conversation — steers accordingly. (13:50-14:15)
- Tool calls "have innumerable failure modes," so background verifiers check both the parameters going into tool calls and the responses coming back; for scheduling this is credited with their high accuracy figure (the caption garbles the exact number). Where the use case allows, verifiers also run *offline*: for scheduling "we have the luxury of time" to detect an inaccurate appointment afterwards and course-correct on the back end or call the patient back and apologize. (14:18-14:57)
- Inference philosophy: "quality is our constraint and speed is the work" — every speed optimization has to be lossless. The three most meaningful were 4-bit quantization (down from 16-bit), speculative decoding (a smaller model drafts tokens, the main model verifies in one go), and KV-cache compression that keeps long conversations warm at a >96% hit rate and makes prefill 18x faster. (15:01-16:15)
- Eval arithmetic: at 10,000 calls a day, "most agentic systems would claim 80%, 90% accuracy… for us, even the 99% is pretty bad" because 1% error means 100 people a day get the wrong appointment type — showing up on the wrong date, or worse, missing a critical appointment. (16:24-17:03)
- Detection cost: "you need about 450 tests to be 99% sure that you can catch this 1% error rate, and 1,900 tests to be able to see that you've caught it like 10 times." (17:06-17:23)
- Because synthetic data alone cannot reach that accuracy scale, they combine synthetic data with humans: 7,000+ trained clinicians on the platform have run roughly 700,000-800,000 clinical conversations as continuous evaluation. (17:24-17:52)
- Grading rubric: across five shipped generations, outputs are not graded only on correctness/validity but on the scale used for human clinicians — correctness, no harm, minor harm, severe harm, death. Polaris sits at 99.89% on no-harm; humans on the same rubric score about 81%, which he attributes not to human incompetence but to fatigue and the absence of "30 plus supervisors helping us at any given point of time." (17:53-18:44)
- Safe is not sufficient: for people to open up to an AI, the voice and conversational lead have to be empathetic, and no good benchmark for empathy existed, so they built one — HEART — and published the paper. (18:45-19:14)
- Organizational note: hiring and internal tooling were reworked "from an AI native perspective," with unlimited tokens for everyone, a residency program for early-career engineers focused on writing safe healthcare agents, and an AI residency for experienced engineers focused on model training. Closing line: told to pick two of quality, speed, and safety, "we didn't." (19:27-20:16)
