## Summary

Ahmed Ahres, head of go-to-market at Reactor (a Series A company building a developer platform for real-time world models), argues that real-time generation is a change of medium rather than a speedup, and walks the application and infrastructure consequences. His definition of "world model" is deliberately narrow — video that is infinite, interactive, and generated fast enough to steer — rather than Gaussian splatting or longer clips. He supports the medium claim with two historical cases (maps to GPS, film to viewfinder), separates the current model landscape into three classes by what the user controls (a scene through prompts, a character in a generated world, a live avatar), reports what users build on them, and closes on the engineering consequence: streaming pixels instead of returning files, stateful sessions whose model memory must be maintained, and sub-100 ms latency that forces GPUs near users. He is candid about two gaps — live avatars are "not cracked," and evaluation of real-time consistency is unsolved by anyone, including DeepMind, so the current method is human judgment. This is a vendor talk with a promo code at the end; the durable content is the taxonomy, the infrastructure divergence, and the eval gap.

## Notes

- Framing and definition: "world models is a little bit of a marketing term," read by some "from a Gaussian splatting standpoint," others from video; Reactor's working definition is "real time interactive video," which Ahres believes "will actually change everything in how we produce and consume content" (00:24-00:57).
- Speaker and company: background in computer vision and machine learning "because this is the time where we used to actually train our models," shipped iOS/Android games, former founder, now head of go-to-market at Reactor, "a series A company building the platform for these real-time world models" — the models themselves are third-party; Reactor builds the infrastructure and developer platform so "anyone can integrate these real-time interactive video" (02:00-02:41).
- The problem statement: "we can today generate pretty much anything, but we just can't change it… a generated video… is still a recording. You get it back, you can't do anything about it." With the current batch models — captions "VO3" (Veo 3) and "C dance 2" / "CDNS" / "C dense 2" (Seedance 2) — "you prompt them, you get back a file, you watch and good luck. It's a slot machine" (01:11-01:31, 02:43-02:55).
- The medium claim: "real time changes what the medium is. It doesn't just make it faster" (02:55-02:58). Case one: before GPS "someone produces a map, you look at where you are, and that's it"; GPS made position continuously available, and "Uber would not exist if we did not have the GPS" (03:04-03:29). Case two, which he rates stronger: on film "they can't see what they're shooting," while digital capture lets you "see what's going on in the screen and adapt accordingly" — "Instagram and TikTok would not exist if we could not produce high-quality content," and the reason that content is possible is "it's not a slot machine" (03:33-04:22).
- The generalization: when generation is real time, video becomes programmable — "you can address it, you can condition it, you can change it," "a little bit like software" (04:24-04:37).
- Model class one, steerable infinite video: "think about Vio or Sora, but real-time and interactive" — infinite ("they don't stop after 5, 10, or 30 seconds"), interactive, and real time (04:47-05:08). The demo passes an image with a dog, generates live, and prompts a cat into the running scene; the argued extension is an unfolding story ("the dog starts running, starts jumping, a dragon shows up"), impossible in batch because "you would get back the video and you cannot do anything about it" (05:09-05:41).
- What class one unlocks: control, which he calls the standing complaint of every content creator — "yes, it's great to use [Seedance] to [Veo] 3 to generate videos, but I just don't have the control" — summarized as "instant feedback is the ultimate level of control" (05:47-06:23). Then advertising, a field he says he is "not particularly fond of" but expects to be big: "If I can know what you looked for a minute ago, why can't I insert the logo of whatever you've been looking for?… We don't need to pre-produce anything." He expects delay from brand risk aversion — "if their logo has one pixel that is white instead of dark" — but expects it eventually (06:23-07:05).
- Model class two, controllable-character worlds: "the Genie 3 like from Google," taking an image and typically text, where "you can control a character" (07:06-07:26). Applications: mixed game/film experiences, with Netflix's *Bandersnatch* as the reference point for picking your next scene (07:26-07:58); robotics, where "because you can simulate and you can control, you can actually create as much training data as you want" and world models in robotics are "a ginormous market today… I cannot tell you the number of robotics labs that are training and building these models" (08:00-08:25); and education, his own interest — "I don't actually believe that the future of education is LLM based or textbook based," because a child can be put inside a history lesson (08:26-08:53).
- Model class three, live interactive avatars: "it hasn't actually been cracked. They're still all kind of weird. If you speak to an avatar in any customer support or anything, it's still kind of off." He sees them rising in research preview and expects the combination with classes one and two to matter, for customer support, training, sales, gaming, and streaming services (08:56-09:38).
- What Reactor users actually build: interactive livestreams where "users can type what happens next and then they vote," on X, YouTube, or Twitch, "because pixels can be generated in real time"; medical simulation — generate a world, then "what if I put this medicine? What if I remove this medicine?" as a training playground for prospective doctors, which he found surprising; cooking simulation; and video-to-video editing, "able to just add visual effects in real time," with entire editing platforms built on it, though "they're not very good yet just because of the quality of the models" (09:39-11:00).
- The four models Reactor hosts, "the world behind an API" (11:00-11:50): **Helios**, the interactive video model, from ByteDance; **"Link bot,"** a Genie-3-like world model trained by Alibaba; **"Long live 2"** from NVIDIA, "multi-shot film" where "you can prompt things in advance and create a consistent story over time"; and **"sound streaming,"** also attributed to NVIDIA, a video-to-video editing model used by shooting or generating footage elsewhere (his example: on Seedance 2), uploading it, and "adding visual effects, removing people, adding background," which he calls interesting for previsualization on Hollywood films.
- Infrastructure, the part he most wants to land: batch video generation is "requests… a job runs in the cloud, and it gives you back a file," whereas "with real time, it's a different ballgame. You cannot just take what works for batch inference and apply to real time inference" (11:51-12:22). Three divergences follow — streaming pixels from server to client "adds entire new complexities that batch generation does not have to think about" (12:22-12:36); "everything is a live session… everything runs constantly, and there is memory to be kept into account," and memory is what real-time models struggle with, the Genie 3 symptom being that "the character can look back and then will not remember what's going on," so work goes into "maintaining that context window" (12:37-13:04); and global scale, since real time "needs to be sub-100 millisecond latency anywhere you are," requiring a user in India or Japan to be routed to a nearby GPU, "if not… the experiences are not real time anymore and it breaks completely the medium" (13:05-13:32).
- Integration and go-to-market: loading a model with an API key is "maybe 10 lines of code" with docs, integrable into "whatever video image plugin" (13:35-13:52); a QR code and promo code AIE2026 for $75 of credits, "significant… because we make the models extremely cheap" (13:56-14:12).
- Q&A, frame rate: asked what it takes to move the demoed 16 FPS to 30 FPS, the answer is serving-side — "we use multiple GPUs," plus "optimizing the model weights, applying quantization techniques… it's just a matter of priorities" (14:28-14:55).
- Q&A, deterministic checks: asked about checking the simulation against deterministic rule sets, "we don't do any of that today… building the infra for this is already a lot of work," and developers build such layers on the platform and open-source them, which Reactor may then use — "the community is doing it for us" (15:35-16:24).
- Q&A, evaluation: "you're asking a question that the entire research community in world models has not answered… fidelity is easy. It's just like pixels… but evaluation for these real-time models is an unsolved problem. So today it's literally just look at it and human judgment… this is including, by the way, DeepMind and everything. Nobody has solved this problem yet," with a research team working on it (16:27-17:06).
