## Summary

Chaitanya Asawa leads Abridge's engineering for clinical decision support and agentic experiences. His talk traces the company's move from ambient clinical documentation — the note a clinician must write after every visit, roughly two hours a day of "pajama time" — into products downstream of that same conversation: pended orders, clinical trial matching, and contextual clinical decision support. The engineering content is organized around three KPIs he says healthcare runs on hard mode (quality, latency, cost) and two answers. On quality, evals are "the operating system": internal benchmarks before any product, staged rollout from an alpha group of clinicians to A/B at scale, continual monitoring after, and expert-calibrated LLM judges that encode clinician judgment so non-clinicians can hill-climb. The hard case is contextual clinical decision support, where the generator–verifier gap collapses — a verifier good enough to trust would already be the generator — so instead of a golden answer, two physicians independently write rubrics of the elements a good response should contain, a third adjudicates them into one, a fourth runs QA, and a judge semantic-matches responses against those elements, with separate judges for safety, adversarial boundaries, and tone. On cost, at a run rate near 100 million medical conversations a year, the note is decomposed into its sections and smaller models are post-trained per section rather than running frontier intelligence over everything; the bet is that a dataset nobody else holds plus a narrow enough problem can beat the frontier's rate of change. Live in-visit order capture is handled by cheap gates that decide when to trigger the heavier models.

## Notes

- Speaker and scope: "my name is Chaitanya, you can call me Chai"; career in AI companies and startups — research engineer at Vicarious, whose goal "was actually to develop AGI, but they took very different methods … inspired by neuroscience and probabilistic graphical models," concretely working on robotics; then ~6.5 years at Glean ("basically the ChatGPT for your workplace") as one of the earliest engineers, 10 people to over 1,100. At Abridge he leads "our engineering teams for clinical decision support and our agentic experiences," 02:57-05:23.
- Stated goal of the talk: push back on the "stigma around maybe the technical problems weren't as interesting in health care" — conceding "a lot of things run on fax machines, for example" — and show problems "that are truly frontier AI product problems that have the highest stakes," 02:13-02:54.
- Domain economics that frame the product: in most industries the cost of a good falls as productivity rises, but in healthcare "administrative costs have only gone up over the past a few decades and productivity hasn't necessarily increased"; "a lot of our problems in health care we solve with labor, but even that we cannot actually keep up," which he links to "Baumol's cost disease." Alongside it: hospitals shutting down, razor-thin health-system margins, patient medical debt, and clinicians who "often don't recommend it as a profession to their children," 05:25-06:34.
- The documentation problem: after every patient visit "the clinician must create a note," typically a SOAP note with sections such as chief complaint and assessment/plan, with variations by specialty. "It takes like 2 hours a day to just write these notes and you often do it with what's known as pajama time after work itself," a common source of burnout, 06:36-07:15.
- Why the note is high-stakes in two directions: "these clinical notes are often used as a basis of billing" and they "provide context to the clinician of the patient's longitudinal medical record" for the next clinician or after a health-system switch, 07:22-07:50.
- Why documentation was the wedge into a "technology reticent" industry: clinicians "care a lot about getting these notes high quality and right," and the product produced higher provider satisfaction, let them "see more patients," and created "a higher record … as it relates to billing, auditing, and other reasons." Reported scale: "in the matter of 2 to 3 years got its way into 300 of the largest health systems in the United States, Kaiser, Mayo, Johns Hopkins, Sutter," on the documentation product alone, 04:51-05:02, 07:51-08:32.
- The company thesis for everything after documentation: "everything is around the conversation… Whether it relates to billing, whether it relates to things like clinical trial matching, or whether it relates to clinical decision support. It's all about the conversation, that sacred doctor and patient conversation, and we've just built all this administrative machinery around that. But how can we bring it back to that conversation and actually automate some of that administrative machinery?", 08:36-09:11.
- The demo shape (product video, voice-driven): a physician asks "Hey Abridge, is [patient] eligible for any clinical trials?" and gets a grounded answer — eligible for a named heart-failure study, "he remains symptomatic despite maximal therapy, and most screening criteria are already met. But an updated echocardiogram is needed to confirm his ejection fraction" — then says "please order that echo for him" ("Done. Confirmatory echo ordered"), and later "can you prepare my charts for tomorrow?", 09:28-10:10.
- The visit envelope the product now covers: pre-visit "suggested discussion topics … whether they're clinical or more billing related"; post-visit "the patient visit summary, the actual clinical note, and we actually pended orders," 10:10-10:38.
- Context sources: "we have access to all of the EHR context, so we know everything about the patient, the prior labs, the prior notes. We have access to the live conversation between the doctor and the patient. That's where the debugging happens in health care, where you learn about what the patient is facing now. And then we have access to world's medical literature that we can ground and clinical guidelines that we can ground all of our work in," 10:38-11:03. (The word captioned as "debugging" is used for what the live conversation reveals about the patient's current state.)
- Three KPIs on hard mode: "if you've ever worked on an agentic product before, regardless of vertical, three KPIs that tend to matter are quality and then latency and cost. In healthcare, I feel that we're actually playing on hard mode for all of these three." Quality, by contrast with his previous job: "when I used to work at Glean, while I loved that product, I could be wrong and it would have been fine. Maybe we answered a question incorrectly. But in healthcare, if we answer something incorrectly, there's actually consequences and we entirely lose our trust." Latency, because the system is live in the conversation: "you can't act on information too late and you have to act at the right time for it to be useful." Cost, because of the scale. He notes the company motto — "save lives, save time, save money" — maps onto the same three, 11:27-12:43.
- Evals as the operating system: "we really treat evals as the operating system, the life's blood of the company." Sequence: "before we develop any product … we start with a robust set of internal benchmarks" tested "against things that we've actually seen in the wild"; then a staged rollout because "we need to make contact with reality. Not everything offline will perfectly represent what happens in practice" — "maybe it starts with the alpha group of clinicians that we trust and they understand the stakes … then beta, maybe there's A/B testing at scale"; then "even after it's fully rolled out, you always need continual monitoring," because "you cannot get away with just being like a prototype that you ship out there and be like, 'Yeah, I mean I tested on a few cases and it works,'" 12:44-13:47.
- Expert-calibrated judges as the mechanism: "we have clinicians embedded throughout the entire company. The clinicians are domain experts, but not all of us are clinicians. I'm not a clinician. So how can we, the rest of the company, still move fast is by encoding that clinician judgement into LM judges." The design criterion: "a really great evaluation system … reflects the behaviors that you want in your product… we are making a product for clinicians and so who best other than our clinicians to actually create our judges that represent what they want. And those judges, once you have that, create a feedback loop so that anyone, whether you're a clinician or not, can actually hill climb and learn from that," 13:47-14:31.
- Online signals alongside the offline suite: "how you're editing the clinical note and your typical thumbs up, thumbs down, and star ratings and other free-form text," 14:31-14:40.
- Contextual clinical decision support, the product he works on: a provider asks something like "does this patient meet the criteria for febrile neutropenia?" — "a lot of context is under-specified in this question," so the system pulls EHR data and previous lab values, uses the live conversation, and uses "clinical guidelines and medical journals … as the reasoning sources for combining all this context to actually answer the provider's question," 14:48-15:33.
- The generator–verifier gap: "in some problems in AI, such as like Sudoku, it's really really hard to generate a solution … but it's extremely easy to verify once you do have the solution. And that makes it much easier to hill climb against and build evaluation for. But in a case like this, the generator and verifier gap is really small. If I had a really really good generator verifier, then that would just be my generator itself. So how do I create a reference that isn't just a language model itself and ground itself to I have trust?", 15:33-16:20.
- The multi-signal answer: "we tackle this by having many many different signals. We have a clinical quality judge … we have many signals from a boundary and adversarial judge. We have a clinical safety judge. And we also have judges that represent product aspects like tone and style, and that matters a lot as well for AI products. So all of these are different signals that try to get a piece of this like really really hard to measure problem and guarantee it in the way that we want the product to be," 16:21-16:49.
- The rubric pipeline behind the clinical quality judge: "what we need is we actually need human references to tell are we generating the right thing. But you can't just create a human golden response because there is a lot of variability in the potential responses. So what we did is we took a lot of real clinical cases. We had independent physicians create a rubric. So this rubric said elements of what we wanted in the response. So it's not here's the exact response because again, there's many infinite possible responses, but a good rubric elements that what a good response would look like. And then we had a separate physician that actually adjudicated it, brought these two independent rubrics together, created a final rubric, and we actually had a fourth clinician do QA on these rubrics," 16:50-17:32.
- How the rubric is consumed: a sample rubric holds the question, the case context, and "the elements that a response should look like"; then "we can actually have an LM judge that compares our agent's responses to these rubric elements and does some basic semantic match to tell, 'Hey, is our model performing well?' As we continue to hill climb, whether it's our agent architecture, our models, or search ranking algorithms," 17:32-17:57.
- Scale that sets the cost problem: "we do this live in the conversation, and we do on the run rate of 100 million medical conversations a year. How do we do this in a way that doesn't really break the bank for us?", 18:00-18:17.
- Decomposition instead of frontier intelligence: the clinical note "has many different sections to it. There's a history of present illness, past medical history, and there's the assessment and plan. So one of the core insights for us is rather than say using a foundation model to generate all this, is we can actually break, decompose this problem into simpler, smaller workflows. Health care is actually many specific workflows. You don't need, you know, Fable 5 to actually solve all of your clinical notes. We don't need frontier level intelligence for every problem. So we actually post train a lot of smaller models for different problems, such as … even to the granularity of different sections in the clinical note. And that lets us use much smaller models because it's a more specific problem and at much cheaper cost and latency," 18:17-19:07. (The frontier model named in the caption is transcribed as "Fable 5.")
- The data flywheel and the right to win: "we have this unique data set of a hundred million medical conversations a year. And as far as we know, no one else has such a large data set. So our key insight is having a right to win in training models. There [are] problems where the quality is already maxed out. And so you should train models then to reduce [cost] and latency. But there are other problems where the quality isn't maxed out and people say, 'Oh, the frontier model would just steamroll you.' Our key insight is we can actually potentially beat the rate of change on the frontier model if we have the right to win by having the right data that they may not have and the focus on a problem that they may not be focusing on. And that lets us still maximize quality," 19:08-19:48. (Captioned as "reduce quality and latency"; the sense from the surrounding argument is cost and latency, since this regime is the one where quality is already maxed out.)
- In-visit orders and the gating trick: clinicians "aren't big fans of pending orders, but often they'll mention orders during the visit itself, medication or non-medication orders. So what we have is while we're listening in the visit, as the clinician says [an] order, we actually queue it up in the background and let them actually sign it off in the EHR. But you can imagine if we did this in a very naive way, like every few seconds are just listening for orders, that would really break the bank. And so a lot of our tricks are like, how do we find the right events in the conversation to actually trigger heavier models that will actually do the order matching, because you need to match the order — not just is the order said, but does it match and references orders that are approved by the system and are relevant to the conversation. So we have a number of different gates that are cheaper and faster that let us trigger actually larger models and hand off to them for actually doing the end-to-end work," 19:49-20:45.
- Closing claim: "healthcare is a domain that needs frontier AI and actually puts it to the test at high stakes," offered against his own earlier worry as an engineer about whether healthcare technology actually works, 20:46-21:10.
