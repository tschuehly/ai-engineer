## Summary

Parth Asawa, a PhD student at UC Berkeley, argues that the field is not measuring continual learning at all, and presents a benchmark built to measure it. The objection is structural rather than about any particular leaderboard: "the way we evaluate these language models is we ask them to do one task and then completely independently we ask them to do another task and then another," which amounts to telling the model "imagine that every time you do something, you completely forget your memory" (00:43-01:31). If continual learning "doesn't look like point capabilities, we need to be measuring it the right way to optimize for the right objective as a field" (03:23-03:33). His working definition is "sample efficient online learning that is stable over long horizons" (01:51-01:59), and he explicitly declines to argue for any one mechanism among in-context learning, external memory stores, and parametric weight updates (02:38-03:18).

The measurement contribution is the **gain** metric. Every system is run through the benchmark twice: once normally, "allowed to maintain state across all of the instances" — which "might mean it's learning and updating its policy… updating its notes… just growing its context length" — and once as a **stateless baseline** where "we reset the system between every instance of a task" (09:26-09:57). Gain is the difference. The reason cumulative reward is not enough is that "total reward alone might confound continual learning ability with base model strength": a stronger system can post the higher total while showing no improvement over its own memory-wiped twin, in which case "it's just a better system to begin with" (08:05-09:16). Reward, gain, and cost are all reported on Pareto frontiers, because "there isn't one single metric that I think defines continual learning" (10:19-10:39).

Building tasks that can carry the measurement turns out to be the hard part, and he names three requirements: **headroom** (the task must actually require online adaptation — "if the model can improve on your tasks by just training offline… then it's not a good task for measuring continual learning"), **shared latent structure** across instances, and a **learning mechanism** in the environment, which "could look like scalar reward… error messages… textual feedback" (05:51-07:34). This is also why chaining existing benchmarks fails: their instances "are designed to be independent," so "you can't meaningfully expect them to improve from earlier experience in future instances" (05:13-05:51). Continual Learning Bench 1.0 spans six domains, and several tasks add a deliberate **concept drift** — in the database task, a migration that drops columns, renames others, and changes formats, so the system must "detect and discard stale experience and simultaneously update from new experience" (12:01-13:00). The headline result is that plain in-context learning tops the leaderboard, on reward and on both Pareto frontiers, beating the more elaborate and more expensive context-management systems (13:52-14:52). Failure modes land on either side of the stability-plasticity trade-off, illustrated by a forecast that discards its own first correction and a notepad that declares a relevant memory inapplicable (14:56-16:45). He closes on a hypothesis that the field is in a sunk-cost fallacy: continual-learning methods are being built on frozen checkpoints from models "never designed to be continual learners to begin with" (17:31-18:31).

## Notes

- The framing complaint, stated against the conference itself: "everyone is just talking about how smart these agents are. We're not actually talking about learning ability or how much these agents learn." (00:19-00:32)
- What today's evaluation implicitly assumes: independent per-task scores aggregated into leaderboards, which means "we've kind of told the models, imagine that every time you do something, you completely forget your memory… like imagine if your life was like that." (00:56-01:28)
- What a learning chart would look like instead: "across different instances, performance doesn't look like a scattered line, but it looks like as a function of prior experience, models are actually able to improve their performance in contrast to what they've done in the past." (01:34-01:49)
- Definition offered: "Continual learning to me is sample efficient online learning that is stable over long horizons," challenging because it requires "your ability to retain prior information without forgetting while simultaneously being able to update from new information and adjust your priors." (01:51-02:11)
- The deployment shape being challenged: data goes "in a box in some offline training process," then "we extract a frozen checkpoint," and "the weights don't really change after that in today's paradigm." (02:14-02:32)
- Three mechanism families named without preference: in-context learning ("put more information in the model's context and the model can simulate some amount of learning in context"), external memory ("a notepad that you then get to refer to every single time or a key value store"), and parametric ("ways to update the model's weights online"). (02:38-03:11)
- Scope statement: "today I'm not going to make a case for which one of these can solve continual learning, though I do have an opinion and I'll share it at the end. But what I am going to make a case for is that we're actually not evaluating it at all today." (03:11-03:24)
- What the existing literature does: train on task A, then B, then C, and check that "the model's performance not degrade on prior tasks while it's still able to learn the new tasks"; plus a factual-recall class asking whether "over these extremely long horizons, can models remember particular parts of the conversation and improve from that." (03:53-04:37)
- Three stated insufficiencies of that literature: "they don't consider sample efficiency a first order requirement. They don't necessarily always measure learning. And perhaps most importantly, they won't work for frontier language models," because frontier models "are pre-trained on vast distributions of the entire internet or economically valuable tasks we care about." (04:37-05:08)
- The chaining objection, which he says is the most common question he gets: "why can't I just chain existing benchmarks together?… why can't I just take my [AIME] problems and solve them in a sequence?" The answer: "benchmark instances… are designed to be independent. That means they don't have shared structure across tasks and as a result you can't meaningfully expect them to improve from earlier experience in future instances." (05:13-05:51)
- Criterion 1, headroom: "you need some sort of task that actually requires online adaptation or learning… If the model can improve on your tasks by just training offline and not actually require any online learning, then it's not a good task for measuring continual learning." (05:51-06:32)
- Criterion 2, shared structure: "there needs to exist some shared latent structure between the tasks… some sort of shared latent in the environment that your models or agents are learning over time and they're seeking to exploit these latent structures… to improve their performance on future tasks as a result of their prior information." (06:32-07:08)
- Criterion 3, a learning mechanism: "there has to be some realistic expectation that the models are able to learn as a result of their prior experience. This could look like scalar reward. It could look like error messages. It could look like textual feedback… Otherwise, it's not really a fair measure of continual learning." (07:08-07:34)
- Reward is defined per instance and is domain-shaped: "You could think of this as efficiency. You could think of this as profit. You could think of this as how close you were to the actual predictions." (07:42-08:04)
- The confound: "total reward alone might confound continual learning ability with base model strength." Worked against a two-system diagram — the red system has the higher cumulative reward and unsaturated headroom, "but you can kind of tell that in comparison to the black system, it doesn't necessarily improve over the stateless baseline. It's just a better system to begin with." (08:05-09:16)
- Gain, operationally: "for any system, we run it through the benchmark twice. Once in the normal way where it's allowed to maintain state across all of the instances… The second thing we run is a stateless baseline… we reset the system between every instance of a task so that the model isn't actually allowed to continually learn in any meaningful way." (09:26-09:57)
- What counts as state is deliberately broad: "It might mean it's learning and updating its policy. It might mean that it's updating its notes. It might mean that it's just growing its context length, but it's allowed to maintain state." (09:36-09:46)
- What gain answers: "if we're looking at gain on task five, how much did my prior experience on the first four tasks actually lead to an improvement in my performance on the fifth task. It isolates out what your benefit of from actually learning was versus your base model's initial capability." (09:57-10:19)
- No single metric: "reward, gain, and cost are all measured on Pareto frontiers. There isn't one single metric that I think defines continual learning because we still care about the base model strength. We still care about the ability to learn and we still care about the cost we're expending for these systems." (10:19-10:39)
- The database exploration task: answer a natural-language question via SQL queries against unfamiliar schemas. A human data engineer "learn[s] those schemas over time… how tables link together… the idiosyncrasies in the data," but a memory-reset agent "wouldn't learn any of that." Reward is efficiency: many queries at the start, "maybe let's say by the 10th instance of a task… it'll take you much less to actually answer the question." (10:39-12:00)
- The drift injection: "let's say there's a migration of your database… Columns get dropped. There's new columns with different names added. The data format changes." A human "learn[s] what to forget and… what's actually relevant to maintain over time," but the model "might struggle in its ability to detect and discard stale experience and simultaneously update from new experience." (12:01-12:50)
- Continual Learning Bench 1.0's six domains: blind spectrum monitoring (signal processing), codebase adaptation (software-engineering efficiency), cohort studies / epidemiology, exploitable poker (strategic game play, included "because my friends like to play poker"), database exploration, and sales prediction (a prediction-error task). (13:01-13:29)
- Task validation: "all of these task instances across domains we validate with domain experts to see is this learnable? Is this realistic? Is these the sorts of drifts you would expect and things you would expect to remember in an environment." (13:29-13:52)
- The headline result: "the in-context learning systems, this is vanilla in context learning where you just put the experience in the context and you don't do any of the fancy context management that some of these other systems do. It tops the leaderboard and it's not just on reward. It's actually also when we look at the Pareto frontiers, this kind of holds across reward versus cost and gain versus cost." (13:52-14:28)
- His own caveat, volunteered: "You could argue that these were medium horizon tasks and they didn't push the frontiers of the in-context learning systems enough and I would say that's fair and that's one of the things on our road map." He still calls the margin surprising: "it was still surprising that these more expensive context management systems perform a lot poorly compared to just vanilla in context learning on these sets of tasks where you have to do real learning." (14:28-14:52)
- The failure-mode claim: "most failure modes in continual learning fall on one side of the stability plasticity trade-off," where stability is retaining information usefully and plasticity is "your ability to actually learn from new information." (14:56-15:20)
- Stability failure, sales prediction over five years: the model is told it overpredicted, "revises its prediction downwards," is then told "its underprediction was too much of an underprediction," and instead of splitting the difference as "a human might naturally do," "the model kind of forgot that it had the over prediction to begin with at the start and it just re jumps back right to the over prediction." (15:20-16:12)
- Plasticity failure, epidemiology with a notepad system: the agent wrote "These seem to be cohort definitions from a different study schema that doesn't apply here" — but "the study schema did in fact apply here… It just didn't even recognize that this is something that's relevant to the task and use that to update its priors." (16:12-16:45)
- His stated preference, offered as opinion: "I'm personally quite excited about the parametric methods for continual learning that look at alternative architectures, data, and algorithms jointly to optimize what continual learning should look like." (16:48-17:07)
- The training stack as it evolved: "pre-training, then we did supervised fine-tuning, and then we do RLHF," now "mid-training, you have RL for different teacher models and you finish everything off with multi-teacher on-policy distillation." (17:07-17:31)
- The sunk-cost hypothesis: "A lot of the work in continual learning today is how do we take these models that are already trained and… that frozen checkpoint. How do we figure out methods for continual learning that work after the fact? But these models were never designed to be continual learners to begin with… we're operating in a bit of a sunk cost fallacy that because we've trained models the way we are today, we need continual learning methods that work on top of that." (17:31-18:00)
- The alternative shape: "In the purest sense, continual learning might just be one set, one phase of training for continual learning and everything after that is deployment. You're just… the model is interacting in the environment. It's updating its weights. There's only one phase of learning." (18:04-18:20)
- Roadmap items: "more domain specific and longer horizon tasks," "more of the OSS models and the parametric approaches," better "simulation for task environments outside of easily verifiable deterministic domains," and personalization to a user model — for which "I don't think we have models that are capable enough of simulating that right now to figure it out, but I think we should." (19:31-19:53)
- Closing takeaway: "continual learning doesn't look like point capabilities. We need to measure it the right way to optimize for the right objective as a field because that's a history of how machine learning has progressed." (19:53-20:04)

## Provenance and Caveats

- Academic work with named institutional funding, not a vendor pitch: "a collaboration across Berkeley, Snorkel, [UW], Madison," with thanks to "Snorkel AI for their… grant through the open benchmarks grant program and the [Laude] institute via their [Laude] slingshots program" (19:14-19:31). Snorkel's own benchmarking checklist is already in this wiki via [The Art & Science of Benchmarking Agents](20260604_iNkFlCiij0U.md), and the funder relationship is worth noting when reading the two together.
- **No numbers are reported anywhere in the talk.** There is no absolute reward, no gain value, no cost figure, no margin between systems, no error bars, no sample counts, and no number of instances per sequence. Every result in this note is an ordering or a qualitative statement.
- **The evaluated systems are not named.** The result is "vanilla in-context learning beat the context-management systems," but which context-management systems were run is never stated, so the finding cannot be checked against any particular product or paper.
- The stateless baseline doubles the cost of evaluating any system, since every configuration is run through the benchmark twice. The talk does not discuss this cost, and cost is one of the three reported axes.
- The speaker volunteers the most important limit himself: the tasks are "medium horizon" and may not have pushed in-context learning hard enough, which is exactly the regime where the headline result would be expected to flip. He calls that objection "fair."
- Parametric systems are described as "down the line too" in the results and as a roadmap item; the initial release evaluated mostly context-management systems, so the comparison across the three mechanism families is not complete.
- The speaker states an opinion favoring parametric methods and closes on a broader argument about third-party research institutions and open science, pointing at his own writing. The benchmark is presented as a public artifact, but no repository, URL, or leaderboard address is given in the transcript.
- Both failure examples are single anecdotes shown as slides, presented as illustrative of a class rather than as measured frequencies.
- Domain-expert validation is asserted ("we validate with domain experts") with no description of who, how many, or what the acceptance criteria were.
