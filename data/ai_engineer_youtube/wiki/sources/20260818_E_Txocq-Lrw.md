## Summary

Todd Fisher walks through a personal project that makes an electric guitar speak and sing, and the walkthrough is more useful than the premise suggests: it is a complete small AI product in which the model layer is commodity and every hard problem sits in the signal path around it. The build is a JUCE plugin dropped into Logic Pro like any other effect, fed by Piper or Apple text-to-speech, and later by a microphone → Whisper → local LLM loop that lets the room ask the guitar a question. The engineering content is in three places. Slicing synthesized speech into words defeated a single heuristic — energy-gap segmentation assumes silence between words and running speech often has none — so he combined it with a sonority-peak syllabifier and finished the residual with a manual drag-to-edit boundary UI. Singing needed YIN pitch detection on each fretted note, an ADSR-shaped synthesized tone, and a vocoder, plus a "clarity" lever mixing synthesized tone against voice. And because pitch-shifting VocalSet samples with WORLD is "a very heavy process… I can't really do that live," the sample set is pre-baked offline and each fret is mapped to a finished sample at play time. Live demos failed twice on stage and the output is openly "choppy"; the closing charge is that AI has made passion projects cheap enough that time "is not the big time suck that it once was."

## Notes

- Framing and lineage: the guitar's history is told as a sequence of signal-path additions — a pickup plugged into a speaker ("now we have rock and roll"), stomp boxes and effects pedals, then Peter Frampton's talk box, which takes "the sound of the guitar, put it through… an actual hose like a physical hose, put it in my mouth, and then basically play the guitar sound in my mouth, and inform some sort of words." The last two decades moved the same effects into software emulation (Pro Tools, Logic, Fruity Loops), to the point of a "hot take… that maybe you don't need all those physical effects pedals anymore." The question the project asks is "what is that next evolution of the guitar… with AI in the picture?" (02:48-04:02).
- Precursor project: for a decade he has played guitar on Halloween while handing out candy, and three years ago dressed as Eddie Munson and built an app that paints the *Stranger Things* alphabet on his garage door in response to played notes, including custom typed messages such as "Happy Halloween" (04:02-05:44). The next step was "how hard would it be to make my guitar speak? It sounds easy, maybe, maybe not" (05:46-05:50).
- Toolchain, stated plainly (05:59-06:30): **JUCE** — "really awesome for anyone building audio software" — plus the plugin formats for a digital audio workstation, with the definition "your DAW is effectively your IDE but for musicians and music producers"; **Piper** and "some built-in Apple stuff" for text to speech; and digital signal processing for the rest.
- Version one is a text field: type text → text-to-speech → audio clip → play the clip back whenever a note is played on the guitar (06:30-06:46). The plugin is demonstrated inside Logic Pro — "this is the plugin I made. So, it's just like any other plugin in Logic where you just plop it in there. It's just, you know, chaining all the effects together" (06:50-07:01) — and speaks a single word ("Developers", a Ballmer nod that gets the room clapping, 07:02-07:20).
- Version two slices the clip per word so a sentence can be played note by note ("Look at me. I can speak.", 07:22-07:53).
- Segmentation, the part he calls hard (07:57-09:00). **Energy-gap segmentation**: "if you look in any waveform… there's typically silence in between words. So, let's just cut it whenever the decibels are very much close to zero." It fails because "as I'm speaking right now, for example, there's actually no silence in between some of my words. So… it's not 100% foolproof." **Sonority-peak syllabifier** (captioned as a stumble, "sonority peak syllaba syllabifier"): "identifying the syllables of the audio signal and identifying that there's vowels in here. Vowels typically lead to syllables." The remedy is additive: "let's take the sonority peak, add it to the energy gap, and figure out if we could just make that work automatically."
- The combined result is demonstrated on a full sentence and is close but wrong in places — "it's working pretty well. Not quite as good as I want to" — so the shipped answer is a manual editor: "I settled on just the ability to go and actually I could drag this and manually edit some of these segments in here. So, worked okay" (09:00-09:37).
- Singing, first attempt (09:38-11:17). Any note contains many frequencies, so the note the player intends must be recovered: "there is definitely a fundamental frequency… but there's a bunch of other frequencies," detected with the **YIN** pitch algorithm (captioned "Yin pitch algorithm"). The detected pitch drives "a pitch sawtooth or aka a synthesized note" shaped by **ADSR**, "effectively the levers to figure out how to actually make the note sound in different ways"; that tone is pushed through the voice clip — the talk-box analogy, "we're kind of filling up the cavity of the voice" — and then a **vocoder**, "and it should sing."
- Live jam (11:18-13:32): he declines to play the song the talk title alludes to "because it's going to be posted online, I don't want to muddy up the waters with any copyright things," and plays chords that "may or may not sound similar to a famous song." Two takes fail — "technical difficulties… Live demos always the best" — before the guitar speaks over the backing track. The plugin exposes a **clarity** lever "balance or mixing the synthesized note with the actual voice that the AI is giving" (13:38-13:47).
- Conversational version (13:53-15:26): "speak in the mic, use speech to text so, **Whisper**, put that into raw text, run a **local model** on my computer. It doesn't really matter what LLM, just any local model. I have a conversation coming and then from that output go and plop it on the guitar." An audience member asks "What is reality?" and the guitar answers, word by word, "That's quite a question to start with. How does your music help you understand… that elusive concept of reality?" His assessment: "we have a choppy version, but it is working. So, that's a win."
- Singing, second attempt (15:30-17:35). He uses open-source singing recordings — **VocalSet** (captioned "vocal set"), "they had basically recorded a bunch of singers" — and the **WORLD** project (captioned "a project called World") for pitch shifting, then shifts each clip's pitch and maps it to the guitar. The constraint that shapes the design: "this is a very heavy process, and so I can't really do that live. I have to effectively pre-bake that. And then once it's already pre-baked, I could then go and jam out with it." Because pre-baking is slow he started with only the five vowel sounds, and the runtime mapping is "each fret or each note on the guitar to one of those samples that are pre-baked." Result: "not quite your opera singer, but getting closer."
- Close (17:36-18:16): he wants to move to "more of the AI heavier options" and asks the room for ideas on making a guitar sing, then repeats the talk's charge — build the passion project, "because nowadays with AI, we could build so many really cool things, and time is typically not the big time suck that it once was."
- Reading note on the AI/non-AI split: every model in this build is off-the-shelf and interchangeable by the speaker's own account (Piper or Apple TTS, Whisper, "any local model"), while everything he describes as hard is classical signal processing — segmentation heuristics, pitch detection, envelopes, vocoding, and the offline/real-time boundary. That split is the transferable observation from an otherwise light talk.
- Caption artifacts and non-signal: `&gt;&gt;` speaker markers separate the demo audio (the guitar's own speech) from the speaker; "the first shadow" (01:13) is *Stranger Things: The First Shadow*; "Yin pitch algorithm" is YIN; "vocal set" is the VocalSet dataset; "a project called World" is the WORLD vocoder/pitch-shifting library; the opening two and a half minutes are concert anecdotes (Slipknot, Broadway) with no technical content, and roughly two minutes of the run time are music demos and stage troubleshooting.
