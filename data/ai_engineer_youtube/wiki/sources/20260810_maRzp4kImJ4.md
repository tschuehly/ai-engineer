## Summary

Nan Jiang (Modal) makes an infrastructure argument about RL post-training that starts from a physical complaint rather than an algorithmic one: "a lot of RL discussion right now is about algorithm and the environments… but when you run those experiments at a scale the problem became more physical. Where are the GPUs? Are they in the same region? Do they have fast fabric? Can we get them right now?" The default RL loop demands all four at once — enough GPUs, one region, RDMA-class fabric, available now — and "any of these is manageable, but all four of them… are pretty hard to get at the same time." He sets the cathedral (one region, one fast interconnect, many GPUs wired together) against the bazaar (different providers, regions, prices, availability) and observes that "training capacity is not elastic in the way the inference capacity is": you cannot grow a trainer cluster halfway through a run because rollout wants more nodes, so if the whole loop must live in one cluster, rollout "inherits the hardest part capacity constraint."

The decomposition is the first contribution. Back-propagation stays: every training step has collectives, all-reduce, and model-parallel communication, and "that part actually wants one fast fabric." The rollout fleet is what can leave, and the movable unit he names is the **rollout serving island** — "a coherent endpoint or maybe a local group of endpoints… serving one policy version." Inside an island there can still be tensor parallelism and prefill/decode disaggregation; *across* islands "there is no global all-reduce — that's the most important thing," which reduces the global interface to policy version in, trajectories and metadata out. That leaves exactly one link holding the architecture together: the weight update. Shipping a full checkpoint is the wrong unit — a frontier-scale checkpoint is "like 500 gigabytes," takes "multiple minutes to hours," and even fully async training "still want[s] weight update latency to be as low as possible, like within seconds."

The mechanism is the talk's centerpiece, and it is a claim about *precision*, not about gradients. The bet is that fewer than 1% of **rollout-visible weights** — the weights in the served checkpoint, "not the FP32 optimizer states, not the Adam moments" — change between consecutive versions. Two ingredients produce that. The **floor**: the optimizer keeps high-precision master weights, but the next forward pass reads a BF16 view whose spacing is roughly θ/128 (a ULP, "the unit in the last place"), and an update must cross the nearest rounding boundary at about half a ULP, "roughly θ over 256… for weight around one" — an absolute 0.0039. The **push**: ignoring weight decay, the Adam per-parameter update is the learning rate times a normalized direction (Adam divides by running gradient statistics, so raw gradient magnitude does not carry through), and a cited paper bounds the step at a constant times the learning rate; at RL post-training learning rates "a typical Adam step here is around 3 millionths," which is "more than a thousand [times] smaller than the boundary." Plotted with weight magnitude on the x-axis and update magnitude on the y-axis, the red floor sits above the green push for most weights: small weights on the left can move, "large weight on the right… will be absorbed." He calls this **Adam absorption**, and it is why the served update is sparse while the master update is not.

He then closes off the misreading directly: "a common misconception there is like it works because our gradients are sparse. They are not. The paper reports the gradients are dense. About 99% of the parameter gets non-zero gradients. The FP32 master update is also dense. It's just small." The measurement backing the claim is deliberately narrow — cast weights to BF16, compare consecutive versions bitwise, count what did not change — and reports around 99% bit-identical per step across a model family, surviving staleness ("even when the rollout lags, the changed set remain very small"). The shipped object is a bitwise-lossless patch (changed positions plus replacement bits plus metadata, encoded as selective overrides or XOR), explicitly "not a floating point addition. So there's no additive delta drift," so a correctly applied patch reconstructs the served version bitwise. LoRA is contrasted as small for a different reason: "full parameter delta is small by absorption and the LoRA updates are small by construction." Lower serving precision makes the effect *stronger*, since for a fixed float format the floor is roughly θ/2^(mantissa+1) — FP4's floor is higher than FP8's, which is higher than BF16's — with group-scaled formats (INT4, MXFP4, NVFP4's hierarchical scales) needing their own encoding treatment. One internal run serving GLM 4.7 Air in FP8 saw 0.15% of weights change on the first high-learning-rate step and settle near 0.05% per step once Adam stabilized.

The architecture that falls out is a **bulletin board**: after the optimizer step the trainer publishes an immutable rollout-weights version; engines living outside the training cluster — no RDMA to the trainer, different regions, different providers — pull and materialize it locally in checkpoint layout as Hugging Face safetensors, "accepted widely by many rollout engines such as SGLang and vLLM," and the engine chooses how to load and shard it but "does not choose a different served version." Requests carry which version they are sent to and which versions are acceptable; responses come back with the version plus "tokens, log probs, router replay information and many more metadata" — "exact same information as if they are in the same cluster as the trainer." A **sidecar** makes an ordinary rollout engine version-aware with three cases: proxy the request if the engine is already at an acceptable version, apply the missing transitions if it is behind but can catch up, and "simply return not ready" if it cannot. Transfer drops from ~500 GB to ~500 MB, which he argues makes cross-region sync a seconds-scale operation. Modal's implementation of the protocol is called **Stitch** — framework-agnostic about trainer, engine, and transport, "async first and also agentic first" — and the payoff line is that "scattered inference capacity became one elastic rollout fleet… inference capacity can now become RL capacity." Three open questions close the talk: whether the sparsity holds for **Muon** (everything above is Adam-only, and "a lot of model providers such as Moonshot and also DeepSeek… are adopting Muon in their post training"), how scalable fully async RL is at this scale, and whether the paradigm generalizes to pre-training, mid-training, and SFT.

## Extracted Concepts

- [RL Post-Training Demands Four Scarce Compute Properties at Once](../concepts/rl-post-training-demands-four-scarce-compute-properties-at-once.md) - Names the conjunction (enough GPU, same region, fast fabric, available now) that makes the default RL cluster shape the hardest thing to buy, and states the training/inference elasticity asymmetry.
- [The Rollout Serving Island Is the Movable Unit of an RL Run](../concepts/the-rollout-serving-island-is-the-movable-unit-of-an-rl-run.md) - Draws the split at the absence of a cross-island all-reduce rather than at "training vs. inference," and reduces the global interface to policy version in, trajectories out.
- [Synchronize Rollout Weights With a Bitwise-Lossless Patch, Not a Checkpoint](../concepts/synchronize-rollout-weights-with-a-bitwise-lossless-patch.md) - Argues the full checkpoint is the wrong unit of synchronization and specifies a patch that reconstructs the served version bit-for-bit with no additive drift.
- [Adam Absorption Hides Most Weight Updates From the Served Model](../concepts/adam-absorption-hides-most-weight-updates-from-the-served-model.md) - The mechanism: a bounded Adam step at RL learning rates is ~1000× smaller than the serving format's rounding boundary, so most master-weight motion is invisible to the rollout engine.
- [A Sparse Served-Weight Delta Is Not Gradient Sparsity](../concepts/a-sparse-served-weight-delta-is-not-gradient-sparsity.md) - The explicit misconception correction: gradients are ~99% dense and the FP32 master update is dense too; sparsity appears only in the quantized served view.
- [Lower Serving Precision Shrinks the Weight-Sync Patch](../concepts/lower-serving-precision-shrinks-the-weight-sync-patch.md) - The counterintuitive corollary that FP8 and FP4 rollout serving produces *fewer* visible weight changes than BF16, with the group-scaled-format caveat.
- [Publish Immutable Weight Versions to a Bulletin Board](../concepts/publish-immutable-weight-versions-to-a-bulletin-board.md) - The pull-based architecture and the version-pinned request contract that let rollout engines run outside the trainer's cluster.
- [Make a Rollout Engine Version-Aware With a Sidecar](../concepts/make-a-rollout-engine-version-aware-with-a-sidecar.md) - Three-case proxy (at version / catch up / not ready) that turns an unmodified serving engine into an elastic RL rollout worker.

## Provenance and Caveats

- This is a vendor talk. Modal sells the compute platform whose scarcity argument the first four minutes establish, ships the named implementation (Stitch), and closes with a hiring pitch ("come work with us"). The protocol is described as general and framework-agnostic, but no non-Modal implementation is cited.
- **The load-bearing evidence is second-hand.** The Adam step bound ("at most B times the learning rate"), the ~99%-bit-identical-per-step measurement across a model family, the staleness-survival result, and the ~99%-dense-gradient counter-claim are all attributed to a paper that is never named on screen or in the video description ("the paper [I cite] there"). None of it can be checked from the talk, and no model, size, step count, or task is given for the cross-family measurement.
- **One first-party number, from one run.** The only measurement presented as Modal's own is a single internal run serving GLM 4.7 Air in FP8: 0.15% of weights changed on the first step, settling near 0.05%. One model, one precision, no task described, no comparison against a second optimizer or learning-rate schedule.
- **No end-to-end result.** Nothing in the talk shows a cross-datacenter RL run reaching the same reward, sample efficiency, or wall-clock as an in-cluster one. The argument is that the served weights are bitwise identical, which makes correctness an equivalence claim rather than a quality measurement — but there is no reported run at all, successful or otherwise.
- **"Extremely fast, in seconds" is derived, not measured.** The 500 GB → 500 MB ratio is stated; no cross-region transfer latency, bandwidth, or egress cost is reported, and no comparison is made against the trajectory traffic flowing the other way, which for long agentic rollouts may be the larger volume.
- **The trainer-side cost of building the patch is never priced.** Producing it requires casting the full parameter set to the serving dtype and diffing against the previous version every step, on the trainer's critical path or beside it. No overhead figure is given, and neither is the storage cost of retaining prior versions on the bulletin board so a lagging engine can "apply the missing transition[s]."
- **The mechanism is a statement about learning rate, and the talk's own numbers show its sensitivity.** The push/floor ratio quoted is ~1000× (3e-6 against 3.9e-3); the GLM run's first, high-learning-rate step changed 3× as many weights as the stable regime. The technique is therefore cheapest exactly when training has settled and most expensive during early or aggressive-learning-rate phases. This reading is derived from the numbers as stated; the talk does not draw it.
- **Optimizer scope is explicitly narrow and explicitly at risk.** Everything rests on Adam's normalized, bounded step. Jiang raises Muon himself and names Moonshot and DeepSeek as adopting it in post-training, so the mechanism may not survive the direction the field is moving. No analysis of Muon's step-size distribution is offered.
- Nothing is said about trust: running rollouts on "different providers" means placing served weights and trajectory data on hardware you do not control, and no threat model, encryption, or attestation appears anywhere in the talk.
- The talk has no Q&A; the recording ends at the thank-you slide (19:35).
