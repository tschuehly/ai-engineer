## Summary

Ashish Kamra (senior manager, performance engineering) and Yuchen Fama (product manager, Red Hat Inference, and a vLLM/llm-d contributor) open by disqualifying the numbers most teams plan against: "when you look at public inference benchmark results you are typically looking at very steady state isolated highly sanitized numbers," and what those hide is "the chaotic reality of multi-turn interactions, massive context fluctuations which are very typical of agentic workloads" (00:43-01:10). The evidence they put in its place is a trace profile from SWE-bench-style workloads and real Claude Code sessions, which "fundamentally break many assumptions we made with classic LLM serving": turn counts running "from a few turns all the way to 3,000 turns"; cache hit rates "oftentimes well exceeding 90%" because agents reuse the system prompt and tool definitions; and an input-to-output ratio "oftentimes over a 100 ratio and even higher" (03:16-04:12). The variance is the operational point — "we can't just simply take the average and oftentimes we need to look at the distributions and the P90 numbers especially when you do capacity planning" — and to let others reproduce this they contributed a trace-replay tool to `inference-perf` with Google and IBM (04:12-04:58).

The regime change they draw from that is who owns the cache. "We're no longer chasing this raw throughput in a steady state… KV cache management becomes super volatile because the context is client determined," producing "frequent evictions and rewrites," which in turn forces the engine (vLLM) and the upper scheduling/routing layer to coordinate "especially when latency becomes a primary scheduling metric rather than a secondary or afterthought," and forces a metrics change: "we need to measure cache throughput separately." The economic justification is one slide of Anthropic API pricing — "there's 10x cost difference between cached and non-cached tokens… 10x difference on your token balance sheet is pretty serious impact on your business" (05:04-06:28).

Lever one is KV-cache-aware routing. The llm-d router carries pluggable **endpoint picker** (EP) plugins that "continuously probe each pod's vLLM metrics to score each pod on running and waiting requests, the KV cache utilization, also prefix cache availability," then "schedule requests to the optimal pod with the lowest load and also highest possibility of a cache hit" (06:28-07:12). Beneath the router, the cache management layer is where hot/warm/cold tiering lives: offloading to NVMe SSD and the XFS filesystem, KV-centric stores such as Mooncake, and "smarter and session-aware eviction policies such as priority and also session pinning to ensure this really important context persists exactly when and where it's needed" (07:12-07:44). The demo makes the routing behaviour legible in four turns: the first request populates the cache and takes "roughly 3 seconds" with no cache hit; the second turn with the same system prompt reuses the cache on "exactly the same" pod address and takes "about one second"; a third turn with a *different* system prompt lands on a different pod with no cache hit and pays "about three seconds" again; changing only the user prompt while keeping the system prompt returns to the cached pod and "roughly about one second" (07:44-08:53). Fama's summary of scope is the sentence to carry: prefix/KV-cache routing "helps you solve the TTFT problem and of course you'll improve your throughput, but oftentimes for agentic workload it's not just a TTFT… it's about your inter-token latency. How do we solve that? So prefill-decode disaggregation" (08:53-09:19).

Lever two is that disaggregation, and Kamra argues it from hardware physics rather than from architecture taste. Colocating both phases on one GPU "creates something called phase interference": prefill "wants high compute… is highly bursty… utilizes GPUs at high FLOPs and thrives on large batch parallelism," while decode "is generating one token at a time and it's more memory bandwidth hungry… highly latency sensitive and requires heavy cache residency," so "if there's a sudden influx of a long prefill prompt, it will completely stall the ongoing decode token generation process causing massive problems and jitter in user streaming latency" (10:20-11:56). In llm-d the mechanics are: the gateway router evaluates cluster state through the endpoint picker and "schedules the request to use PD disaggregation, selecting the optimal prefill and decode workers"; the prefill worker "processes the prompt, constructs the initial KV cache of the prompt and outputs the standard KV transfer metadata"; and "the target decode worker actually pulls the computed KV caches across the network fabric utilizing the KV transfer metadata" (11:56-12:55). Around that sit llm-d's other pieces: a CNCF-hosted, Kubernetes-native (and now also non-Kubernetes) distributed inference framework with workload APIs — LeaderWorkerSet and DisaggregatedSet — "that orchestrate complex multi-node model execution," plus autoscalers that "monitor capacity bounds and real-time traffic mixes to independently scale up and scale down your pods" (09:28-10:20).

The measurements are internal Red Hat results on gpt-oss. On 16 H100s (aggregated: 4 replicas at TP4; disaggregated: 2 prefill + 2 decode, all TP4) against "a highly multi-turn workload with a 10,000 token prefix and 128 tokens for every turn," P99 inter-token latency falls from "roughly around 900 milliseconds" with visible fluctuation to "almost nine times better at 100 milliseconds," and "much smoother" (12:59-13:52). A concurrency sweep on the same setup separates the two levers: the bottom line is aggregated serving under default Kubernetes scheduling, the middle line is *still aggregated* but with llm-d KV-cache-aware routing — "you can almost see the gains just based on the routing" — and the PD line "is very similar to the aggregated config at the lower concurrency regimes and very similar at the higher concurrency regimes, but it's actually the middle part of the concurrency regime that PD actually shines" (13:52-15:07). A second config on 64 H100s (aggregated: 8 replicas TP8; disaggregated: 3 prefill + 5 decode, TP8) with a prefill-heavy 5,000-token average input and 500-token output shows the PD Pareto curve dominating "across the entire interactivity spectrum" (15:07-15:35).

Kamra then refuses the generalization: "I don't want to leave you guys that PD is the answer to everything and it's a magic bullet… it's essentially a phase separation trade-off." His matrix says use PD for long context with high ISL:OSL ratios, large models where rich model-parallelism applies, the middle concurrency regime, and "strict ITL streaming requirements" — but "it requires transfer of KV caches from your prefill workers to your decode workers, so you must possess an advanced high-speed network fabric like RDMA or RoCE." Stay aggregated for short-to-moderate context, any model size, low concurrency, strict TTFT requirements ("because you can actually tune them on aggregated serving"), and — "the biggest point" — if you do not have the fabric (15:39-17:04). His takeaway is that the scheduler must "constantly evaluate SLO targets, QPS, KV cache locality metrics, PD ratios and network topologies," while the PD design space needs "dynamic PD rate matching… because you can start with a static PD ratio but it needs to evolve with the autoscaler as the traffic changes," independent autoscaling of prefill and decode pools, and continual tuning of tensor and data parallelism against SLOs (17:04-17:55).

The closing case study anchors all of it on hardware customers actually own: "You probably have seen tons of impressive numbers of GLM 5.2 on B200. When we talk to our customers… they usually don't have the luxury of B200. They have a lot of H200" (18:01-18:30). The design uses a prefill pool of "up to three workers optimized for high throughput" and "one dedicated worker" for decode "optimized for low latency," NIXL for KV transfer between pools, and each worker as a LeaderWorkerSet group at TP1/DP8/EP8. The modularity claim is the payoff: "you can actually scale the throughput by simply adding prefill workers without reconfiguring the decode" (18:30-19:28). Two findings ride along: BF16 KV cache "actually is faster than using FP8 KV cache for longer prefill," reported as a fun fact from "a couple days ago" and still being explored (19:28-19:52); and on an agentic dataset with a 45:1 ISL:OSL ratio where "prefill is really the constraint," they report "4x faster TTFT and 60% more requests," with the work explicitly in progress and the next step being more prefill replicas (19:52-20:20).

## Provenance and Limits

- Every performance number is Red Hat's own internal result for its own project, presented without error bars, run counts, or a reproduction recipe. The talk is candid that this is vendor benchmarking ("this is some of our own internal results at Red Hat"), and the GLM 5.2 case study is stated twice to be ongoing work.
- The trace profile that motivates the whole talk (1→3,000 turns, >90% cache hits, >100:1 input:output) is given as a range and a qualitative "oftentimes," with no distribution, sample size, or split between SWE-bench-style workloads and real Claude Code sessions. The P90 advice is stated but no P90 figure is shown.
- The model is captioned as "gpt-oss 12B." The publicly released gpt-oss sizes are 20B and 120B, so the parameter count is likely a caption error; the GPU counts and parallelism degrees are self-consistent (4×TP4 = 16, 8×TP8 = 64, 3+5 at TP8 = 64) and are more trustworthy than the model size.
- The "middle concurrency regime" rule and the "dominates the entire interactivity spectrum" result come from *different* configurations (2P2D on 16 GPUs versus 3P5D on 64 GPUs, and a multi-turn 10k-prefix workload versus a prefill-heavy 5k/500 workload). The talk does not reconcile them, so the middle-band claim should be read as one config's shape, not a law.
- The 9x P99 ITL improvement is measured at one point, not across the load curve; the concurrency sweep that does span load is presented qualitatively with no axis values read out.
- The 10x cached-versus-uncached cost gap is a screenshot of one vendor's public API pricing (Anthropic), used to motivate self-hosted cache work. It is not a measurement of Red Hat's own serving economics, and no self-hosted cost-per-token figure appears anywhere.
- The GLM 5.2 headline conflicts with its own slide description: the architecture is described as up to three prefill workers to one decode, while the quoted result is "with 2P… 1D." The video description states three-to-one. Both numbers are recorded here because the transcript supports the lower one.
- The BF16-faster-than-FP8 KV cache observation is offered as a days-old anecdote with no workload definition, no measurement, and an explicit "we continue to explore." Treat it as a prompt to measure, not a finding.
- The demo's 3s-versus-1s figures are wall-clock request times in a staged four-turn demo on an unstated model and hardware. They illustrate pod affinity and cache reuse; they are not a latency benchmark.
- Nothing is said about what KV-cache-aware routing costs: the EP continuously probes every pod's metrics, and neither the probe overhead, the staleness window, nor the behaviour when the cache-richest pod is also the busiest is quantified — the scoring function is described only as balancing the two.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "KV cache away routing" → cache-aware (00:28); "VLM" → vLLM and "AMD"/"LMD"/"LNMD" → llm-d throughout; "sweet bench" → SWE-bench and "cloud code" → Claude Code (03:33-03:44); "guide LLM" → GuideLLM and "hugging phase" → Hugging Face (02:41-03:07); "moon cake" → Mooncake and "file system XF" → XFS (07:21); "preview"/"pre-fill"/"pre-filled" → prefill and "decoup" → decode throughout; "RDMMA or rocky" → RDMA or RoCE (16:31); "parita curves" → Pareto curves (14:54); "Nixo" → NIXL (19:07); "leader worker set"/"disagregated set" → LeaderWorkerSet/DisaggregatedSet (09:58); "QEPs" → QPS (17:22); "Andrew Ning" → Andrew Ng (02:14); "PB and DB and EPI scale" → PD, DP and EP at scale (19:30). Left unresolved: "the case of projects" in the contributor list (02:52, plausibly KServe), "Red Hat AI arc" as the Hugging Face org name (03:09), "sub Asian panel" as an observed agentic pattern that "further complicates scheduling" (04:28), and "program award scheduling" in the roadmap list (20:34, plausibly prefix-aware scheduling).
