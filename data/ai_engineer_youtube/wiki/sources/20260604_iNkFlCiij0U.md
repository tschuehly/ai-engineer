## Summary

Vincent Chen (research fellow and co-founder, Snorkel AI) distills a framework for building effective open benchmarks, drawn from reviewing 120+ applications for Snorkel's $3M Open Benchmarks Grants. The motivating asymmetry: our ability to build agents has outpaced our ability to measure them (ARC-AGI 3 launched weeks earlier with every task human-solvable but frontier models under 1%), so closing the evaluation gap needs a toolkit in which open benchmarks remain critical — and the best ones are forward bets that *define and shape* the field, not backward snapshots. He splits benchmark design into "the science" (four properties of a good empirical measuring stick: individual task quality, distributional diversity, difficulty/model headroom, and robust eval methodology) and "the art" (three differentiators of field-shaping benchmarks: a thesis/bet on where capabilities are going, the ability to produce research roadmaps, and first-class researcher/builder UX). He closes with three axes Snorkel believes the next generation of agent benchmarks must cover: environment complexity, autonomy horizon, and output complexity beyond plain text.

## Notes

- Framing asymmetry: capabilities (hill-climbing model cards, vibes shifting especially in coding) are improving, but enterprises hesitate to deploy in high-stakes settings (finance, insurance, healthcare) because measurement is falling behind capability, not because capability is absent. (01:52-02:44)
- The evaluation gap needs a *toolkit*: field deployments, red teaming, private human evals, crowdsource labeling, and — the piece Snorkel feels most strongly about — open benchmarks, which "aren't just about taking a snapshot of progress looking backwards" but about defining progress and setting goalposts. Named guideposts: Terminal Bench, METR's long-horizon benchmark, ARC-AGI. (02:48-04:11)
- Open Benchmarks Grant: $3M deployed ~a month before the talk; 120+ applications reviewed across academia and industry labs. (04:16-04:52)
- Science axis 1 — individual task quality: tasks need rigorous validation, real-world complexity, well-posed instructions, and verifiable solutions validated by domain experts. GPQA's appendix contribution is an adversarial quality-control mechanism: tasks must be *tractable for other experts* to solve, enforced by a multi-reviewer protocol (original author, reviewers, adjudicators, revision opportunity) plus new incentive mechanisms (payouts tied to agreement, inspired by peer review). (06:34-08:23)
- Science axis 2 — distributional diversity: define a clear taxonomy for the domain and distribute tasks intentionally across it — either to represent a captured real-world traffic distribution, or to deliberately taxonomize rare-but-disproportionately-important failure modes (self-driving analogy: yellow lights, pedestrians, motorcyclists are rare but critical to get right). MMLU's 57 academic/professional domains across STEM and humanities is cited as a thoughtful taxonomy. (08:27-09:49)
- Science axis 3 — difficulty/model headroom: a benchmark must stay unsaturated, expose real soft spots, and reliably separate models at the frontier. ARC-AGI 2 stayed unsaturated for months/years, then the reasoning push (~18-24 months earlier, o1-style) produced a leap that corresponded to a real capability leap; ARC-AGI was designed to capture an efficiency humans have but models lacked. ARC-AGI 3 launched with every task human-solvable but frontier models under 1%. (09:52-11:24)
- Science axis 4 — robust eval methodology: go beyond accuracy to capture cost, latency, reasoning-trace quality, intermediate steps, and tool use as reward/supervision signals; "measuring what it claims to measure" is non-trivial for reproducibility. τ-bench evaluates multi-turn agents on both task completion (via a user simulator) *and* adherence to policy constraints — a model that books the right flight but violates fare-class rules still fails. (11:27-12:52)
- Art differentiator 1 — thesis/bet: great benchmarks pose a research question about a subspace of capabilities and bet on where the world is going. Terminal Bench bet on the CLI not just for coding agents but for general-purpose computer use — "a largely correct and consequential bet" as Claude/Codex teams built enterprise capabilities on CLI-based tools; measuring those capabilities early helped accelerate the field. (13:23-14:34)
- Art differentiator 2 — roadmap for the field: SWE-bench was a simple idea (leverage coding capability via PRs) that spawned a family (Lite, Verified, Pro, Multilingual, Multimodal) and evolved how the field thinks about coding agents, inspiring many follow-on benchmarks. (14:36-15:55)
- Art differentiator 3 — researcher UX (called "severely underrated"): make it simple to run models/agents against the benchmark, simple to contribute new tasks, and simple to leverage its signals for RL or post-training tuning, because a benchmark's core users are other builders/researchers. Stanford CRFM's HELM pioneered a standardized modular harness for reproducible scenario/model evaluation; Terminal Bench 2.0 shipped with Harbor, a de-facto harness and eval infrastructure for teams building agents. (15:59-17:40)
- Next-gen axis A — environment complexity: how complex/realistic/dynamic the operating environment is. A real codebase has org-specific policies, Slack context, screenshots, flaky toolchains, distributed CI, human reviewers with preferences in their heads, and many parallel contributors; today's benchmarks capture only a fraction of this, and the gap is where agents fail. (19:57-20:47)
- Next-gen axis B — autonomy horizon: how long an agent can operate before reliability breaks down, capturing different points on the co-pilot↔fully-autonomous slider. A customer-experience agent may lose track of context delivered weeks ago, see integrations/specs change, or have reorgs shift priorities midstream — long-horizon continual-learning settings with changing state/environment. (20:47-21:37)
- Next-gen axis C — output complexity beyond plain text: produce more complex, representative work and nuanced signals usable for both eval *and* reward during training. It is subjective and non-trivial to define what is verifiable about a good strategic recommendation/proposal/roadmap (needs organizational context and human judgment). Also "trustworthy outputs": agents that capture their own uncertainty and stop or ask for more information rather than always emitting a plain-text answer. (21:37-22:35)
