## Summary

Ben Hylak (CTO and co-founder, Raindrop) argues that mainstream eval advice is still written for the chatbot era, "back when you knew the answer to most questions your users would ask" — "at least like 80% of them or 90% of them" — and that it transfers badly to agents. The build-a-thousand-example-suite prescription fails on two counts he states plainly: "nobody's doing that. Um, very few people anyway," and the suites that do exist "break as soon as you have a new model, as soon as you like switch harnesses" — write a tool-call eval, move to Claude Code CLI, "and now 80% of your evals suck." His reframing is that "what issues does your agent have?" is the wrong question, because an agent "will have an infinite number of problems"; the question is which of them are worth acting on. That turns on the gap between the **ceiling** (the "craziest capability, emergent capability that your product or agent is capable of") and the **floor** ("the worst thing your agent can do" — "recommend a competitor," "delete a bunch of data," "accidentally send a AI slop email to a customer because it like technically had access to like your email"). The floor "is the thing that breaks user trust," and he places the well-known societal failures there rather than on the capability axis: "a lot of it is more on like the floor side rather than the capability side."

The operational core is two numbers per issue: **when it started** and **what share of users it hits**. Onset is what converts a report into a question you can act on — "if I tell you like, 'Hey, look, this issue started yesterday'… suddenly like your mind starts turning and you're like, 'Oh, what did I do? What changed? Did we change model?'" — and share is what decides whether to care at all: "knowing that it happened to three users versus 100,000 users just is critical." A prior question gates both: "the first question I always ask people is like, 'How many users do you have?'" Raindrop has customers with millions and customers "with like five," and the available method changes completely across that range — at 10, 20, or 100 million messages a day "experiments become extremely valuable" and you can run them "on a very small sample of your free tier," while at five or ten users "I would not recommend experiments or AB tests." Small user counts do not imply low stakes; a five-user internal enterprise app "giving like very critical information" can matter more per interaction than a consumer product.

Three tactical lessons close the talk, all framed as first-principles findings from running Raindrop ("three things that I've never heard anyone talk about"). **Clusters are not issues**: clustering all your traces is "useful for one-off analysis, but it just doesn't really scale well," because temporal tracking is unreliable, "you also just like don't have control of boundaries," and what counts as the same issue "is actually very, very unique to every company" — a cluster named "price issues" quietly merges a wrong quote and a wrong refund that "could have like extremely different root causes," and it supplies neither of the two numbers you need. **Code mode scales to traces**: "you've heard about code mode in the context of MCPs… I highly recommend just trying to apply this to traces. Like you can just write these classifiers and you can run them in a sandbox and you can run them at production scale." **Agents cannot spot anomalies but can investigate them**: "don't ask your agent to find anomalies. Ask it to investigate anomalies you've already found" — surface something deterministic first, "like keyword frequency," where a spike "doesn't necessarily mean that there's an issue, but… it's like something more tangible tractable that you can have an agent actually investigate." Underneath all of it is the claim that the unit under test has changed shape: "the prompt is actually like the whole thing now. It's like all the code. It's your whole harness," so evals "should look a lot more like code… a lot more like tests," kept in the repo rather than in a managed prompt playground.

## Provenance and Caveats

- **Vendor talk, and the tactical section is aimed at competitors.** Hylak is CTO and co-founder of Raindrop, a hosted agent issue-detection product ("Think of it like Sentry, but detects issues for agents"), and he introduces the three lessons with "any competitors in the audience, please pay attention." Two of the three map onto product surface: the code-mode lesson comes with "we have a feature that makes this easier," and the clustering critique is aimed at what "customers or also, sometimes competitors" do. The ideas can be adopted without the product, but the selection of which three lessons to present is not neutral.
- **No figures from Raindrop's own book of business appear anywhere.** There is no issue count, no detection rate, no accuracy number, and no measured comparison of clustering against whatever Raindrop does instead. The clustering critique is argued structurally and by one hypothetical example ("price issues"), not demonstrated.
- **"80% of your evals suck" is an illustrative estimate, not a measurement.** So is "80% or 90%" for chatbot-era answer coverage. Neither is sourced, and no methodology is offered for either.
- **"Agents are very, very bad at anomaly detection" is asserted with no evidence.** No model is named, no baseline is given, no task is described, and no alternative is measured against. The recommendation that follows is nonetheless cheap to adopt and easy to test locally.
- **The market claims come from a self-selected customer base.** "Nobody's doing that" about thousand-example eval suites, and "I don't know many companies that use some sort of like managed prompt like in the cloud anymore," are impressions from the customers of an agent-observability vendor — a population already biased toward teams that concluded offline suites were insufficient.
- **The talk was cut short.** Slides were skipped twice ("I'll kind of skip this," "I'm going to skip through the rest because we're tight on time") and the Q&A that the first two minutes were built around never happened, so the discovery half of the three-part frame ("discovering all these like unknown issues") is stated but never developed.
- **Same speaker, same company, thirteen months earlier.** The wiki already carries [Building AI Products That Actually Work](20250724_eSvXbb2EBYc.md) (2025-07-24), where Hylak recommended clustering explicit and implicit *signals* together with user intent. This talk's "clusters are not issues" is best read as a refinement of that position — the object being clustered has moved from signals to raw traces, and the missing pieces are named — rather than as a reversal, but the two pages should be read together.
