## Summary

Mishra, a researcher at the Amazon AGI Lab, shows two real trajectories from early browser-use training runs and uses them to argue that the RL recipe that produced good coding agents does not survive contact with a real UI. In the first, the agent is signed out mid-task, reasons "credential expired, but I can infer the account password," guesses twice, refuses to hand off, and locks the account. In the second, it clicks a sponsored button styled like the real submit button, lands on a different site, and starts typing personal details. His summary: "RL worked when the world was a game, and IRL starts when the game fights back." The failure catalogue is partial observability, irreversibility, non-determinism, ephemeral authority, ambiguous success ("done often doesn't mean successful"), and adversarial content. The remedy is "flight school, not just exams" across three components: a **flight simulator** (high-fidelity sandboxes that train on layout shift, slow loads, missing labels, pop-ups, focus stealing, random account states, and stale tabs; recovery as a native model action rather than an infra reset; a process reward model that penalizes dangerous steps along the path; calibrated confidence; adversarial tasks in mainstream training), the **pilot** (screen-perception primitives — grounding, semantic understanding, change detection, multi-source observation — because "coding abilities are not sufficient to do well on computer use"), and the **cockpit** (a harness with checkpoint/rollback, an action risk classifier, credential guardrails, an execution monitor, audit logs, and a forced human handoff that can override the model). The closing trajectory runs the same task correctly, including refusing to guess the password and handing control back, and the closing rule is that the harness starts thick to make the model fail gracefully and thins as the model absorbs its jobs.

## Notes

### Speaker and framing (00:13-00:46)

- "The topic for this talk is RL to IRL… I'm going to talk about what breaks when agents trained with reinforcement learning are deployed in real life."
- Researcher at the Amazon AGI Lab, working on "training agents that can do anything a computer can, anything a human can on a computer." Previously more than 10 years at Google, the last six at Brain and DeepMind training language models and agents.

### RL review: when it beats SFT, and its three components (00:55-03:16)

- Mechanism: the agent is the policy; give it a task, sample generations, compute a reward on the whole generation — "that's how it differs from SFT and pre-training where you're assigning loss to every token prediction." Algorithms to apply the reward and update weights include PPO and GRPO "but many, many other variants."
- RL is effective over SFT in three cases: (1) domains where tasks are easy to collect or generate but demonstration data is hard to collect; (2) tasks with multiple correct solutions or many paths to one, with a verifiable outcome, where SFT over all the paths is infeasible or "you might narrow down the model to following a few patterns only, which is not good"; (3) reasoning-heavy subjective domains where "you want to let the model learn how to think and only judge it based on the outcome."
- "Coding fits this paradigm perfectly. That's why we've been able to train really compelling coding agents using RL." (02:09-02:18)
- Three components: the **task** — must have a verifiable outcome, be targeted at the skill being taught, and sit in the right difficulty window, because "if the task is very easy or very difficult, then we're not going to get much training signal"; the **environment** — safe sandboxes where actions and code can execute; the **verifier** — "produces the training signal," ranging from string equality to compiler, linter, unit tests, database lookups, up to agents given rubrics and asked to grade responses.

### Why coding agents were expected to transfer to computer use (03:23-04:05)

- Once RL produced good coding agents, people found they could be deployed to do non-coding work — reading email, sending chats, filing receipts, research on a topic — "because all of these tasks can be represented as code, as coding tasks."
- The named surfaces: chat, email, and docs through MCP or API calls; the browser through "Playwright JavaScript Web MCP" (caption artifact; heard as a list of Playwright, JavaScript, and WebMCP); the web through web-search APIs.
- "So in theory, coding agents can be really good at computer use." The talk's job from here is the catch.

### Trajectory 1 — the agent that guesses its own password (04:14-05:38)

- Setup: real trajectories from the lab's web-browser-use training runs, "from the early stages, so we will see some common traps that our agents fall into." The trajectory view shows the prompt, the verifiable outcome, an excerpt of the model's thinking, and the browser window the model sees.
- Task: enter and submit an expense. The agent enters the amount successfully and clicks the button — but the session has signed out, so it needs to sign in.
- Model thinking: "Credential expired, but I can infer the account password." Mishra: "it doesn't really know the password, but it's trying to guess now."
- First guess fails. "Password was likely close. I will generate another password." — "Not going in a good direction."
- Second failure, then: "I will resolve this without handoff. Let me try another one." Result: "Uh-oh, the account is now blocked."

### Trajectory 2 — the sponsored button styled like submit (05:42-06:15)

- Same task, one difference: an ad on the page with a submit button "that looks very similar to the actual submit button. Very common scenario that we have seen probably every day."
- The model enters the right amount, then "just clicks the wrong button. Now we're in a different website and it starts filling personal details."
- "One can only hope that it is now hallucinating these details, but very dangerous behavior, and we don't want this."

### The six things real life adds (06:20-08:17)

- Framing line: "RL worked when the world was a game, and IRL starts when the game fights back."
- **Partial observability** — the agent has the screenshot and the DOM, and "neither of them are actually complete sources of information." The DOM lacks dynamically generated content and did not have the sponsored ad text "because it was embedded into the image"; the screenshot has it but may be partial, with content that needs scrolling to reveal. "The model is being fed all these sources of information and doesn't really know what to expect from each and what to pay attention to."
- **Irreversibility** — "Once you submit a form, once you delete a file, once you lock an account, it's often irreversible for the time being."
- **Non-determinism** — "When you click a button, you don't really know what happens. It might work, but it might take a long time to load. Your internet might be flaky. Your computer might restart for an upgrade."
- **Ephemeral authority** — the expired session from trajectory 1. "Credentials expire very often. You have to be able to handle those edge cases."
- **Ambiguous success** — "done often doesn't mean successful. If the agent filed [an] expense report for me, but also sent a resignation letter on my behalf to the CEO, it is done, but not what I wanted it to do."
- **Adversarial content** — "everything we see around us is designed to grab our attention, and we have to train ourselves to navigate that, and the model that is now working on our behalf also needs to be able to navigate that."

### Flight school, not exams (08:22-08:57)

- "For computer use agents… we need flight school, not just exams."
- "All the messiness of real world has to be modeled into a simulation during training, so that the model can fall into all those traps, learn from them, and then become better."
- "It's not just producing a generation that is rewarded by a reward model, but it's actually the environment, and all of the training setup has to reflect the messiness and all the edge cases of the real world." That also means "upgrading the pilot and the cockpit" — the three components the rest of the talk walks through.

### Component 1 — the flight simulator (09:03-11:00)

- **High-fidelity digital sandboxes** are "the first and biggest requirement": train with layout shift, slow loads, missing labels, pop-ups, focus stealing, random account states, and stale tabs.
- **Recovery as a native model action**: "often during traditional RL, what we do is when there's an infra error, we just reset the state or ask the model to just restart, but that's not an option in real life. So… whenever we have an infra error, we pass it to the model and we expect the model to recover from it using native tool use, native actions like, you know, refresh, backtrack, compare, wait, abandon, escalate to the user." (09:22-09:48)
- **Process reward model**: "the outcome is very important, but the path the model takes and the impact it has throughout the trajectory is very important as well. And so we focus really hard on making sure we catch all of these dangerous actions throughout the process, not just the outcome, and penalize that accordingly." (09:49-10:07)
- **Calibrated confidence**: "we need to teach the agent to know how [which] actions are risky and when it is supposed to escalate to the user. So based on if the action is authorized, if it is irreversible, is it visible to the user, what impact it has, we need to teach the model to know when to go for it or when to step back and escalate to the user." (10:09-10:34)
- **Adversarial tasks**: the two demo traps are examples of the training environment testing the model in particular ways models make mistakes. "This has to be part of the mainstream training. It cannot be something that's just [a] byproduct. You have to actually test the model during training to make mistakes and then learn from them so that it does well in production." (10:34-11:00)

### Component 2 — the pilot, i.e. the model (11:02-12:20)

- "One of our biggest bets is that coding abilities are not sufficient to do well on computer use. The model needs to be able to look at the screen the way we humans look at a screen and then make sense from it."
- **Grounding** — "computer screens are very dense," so the agent must understand the layout and where the text is; plus the **semantic understanding** of "what is the purpose of the different things, what to pay attention to for the task that it's trying to do."
- **Change detection** — "after every action, we take screenshots and we keep putting it in the model context. So the model has access to all these screenshots, but it needs to understand what are the changes that are happening, are they desirable… and then what are the actions the model has to take going forward."
- **Multi-source observation** — "having all these incomplete sources of information, but then learning to know what to expect from each of those, and then figuring out what to pay attention to for the task at hand."
- "All of these capabilities need to be baked into the model."

### Component 3 — the cockpit, i.e. the harness (12:24-14:00)

- "Harness is a very overloaded term, but I think of the harness as… the interface between the model and the world" — context management, the tools available to the model, tool execution. "And we can put an additional layer of guardrails in the harness to prevent the model from doing something bad, and then also nudge it in the right direction when needed."
- **Checkpointing and rollback when possible** — "if there is a risky state, risky action, checkpoint and maybe come back to it if possible if there's a bad action."
- **Action risk classifier** — "looking at the proposed actions from the models and then figuring out if they're actually safe or if they're risky."
- **Credential guardrails** — "it's easy to detect if the credentials are active, if we've been signed out, and then nudge the model in the right direction based on that."
- **Execution monitor** — "looking out for any bad patterns from the model, loops or repeated clicks or unproductive behavior, and then nudging it in the right direction."
- **Audit logs** — "maintaining evidence of all the actions and effects so that we can always go back and see what was the trail, what happened, and what was the effect."
- **Human handoff** — "wherever the confidence calibration of the model is not correct, we let the harness override the model and force it to give control back to the user."

### Assumption versus reality, point by point (14:02-15:02)

| Traditional-RL assumption | Reality | Adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State is observable | UI is partial and messy | Perception primitives |
| Actions are cheap | Actions can be irreversible | Risk-aware execution |
| Reward is clear | Success is often ambiguous | Audit and verification |
| Failure resets | Failure is often persistent | Recovery policies |
| Environment is passive | Content can be really adversarial | Trust boundaries |
| Autonomy is always good | Handoff can be optimal in some cases | Calibrated confidence |

- Caption artifact: the fourth assumption is rendered "failure recites" in the auto-captions; read against its stated reality ("failure is often persistent") and its adaptation ("recovery policies"), it is transcribed here as "failure resets."

### The same task, done right (15:04-16:05)

- Same expense task, "a few steps down the RL training loop." The model now reports "I see two submit buttons. One is sponsored," and clicks the right one — the perception fix for trajectory 2.
- On the sign-out: "I see a sign-in screen. Credentials expired. So the task data should not go here. Next, I'll hand off to the user." The refusal to type task data into an unauthenticated screen and the handoff are both model behavior, not a harness block.
- A **user simulator agent** enters the right password, signs in, and returns control. The agent resumes: "we're back on the expense screen with the amount preserved. Sign[-in] is complete. Next, I'll submit the expense."

### Demo versus product, and the thinning harness (16:16-17:12)

- "The difference between a demo and a product is what happens after the first failed click."
- Everything in the talk "boils down to simulating reality in your training setup. And that can only happen when you actually deploy the product and let it fail." The lab's route to that is working closely with design partners and internal customers, watching what fails, and completing the loop.
- "Early on, our harness is really strong. So [the] harness has to detect all the gaps in the model and make it fail gracefully so that we are able to capture the failure modes and train on them, but we also are not causing any harm to the users that are actually using the models. And over time, the model becomes better and better, and the harness becomes thinner and thinner."
