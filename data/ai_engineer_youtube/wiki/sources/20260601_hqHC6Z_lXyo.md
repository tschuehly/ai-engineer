## Summary

Bertrand Charpentier (cofounder and chief scientist at Pruna AI, an efficiency/compression company) dismantles the idea that any single model holds the "state of the art" title and argues that efficiency is a dimension of state of the art, not a footnote. He critiques the two methods people use to pick a "best" model — reading a public leaderboard and running an internal benchmark — and shows both are unreliable when applied naively: leaderboards disagree with each other, are built on small samples, and report win rates where most models lose ≥40% of battles, while internal evaluation is biased by personal preference, small sample counts, and metrics nobody understands. His answer is the quality–efficiency Pareto front: plot a quality score against latency or cost, find the frontier, and you get multiple state-of-the-art models (3–4) clustered tightly in quality but varying up to ~20x in efficiency — which tends to surface small specialized "performance models" rather than large foundation models. A running cost example anchors the thesis: the same image-model evaluation (26,000 battles at ~62s/image) costs 20 days of compute, $5,000, 556 kWh (~400 marathons of energy) on the slow model versus 7 hours and $265 on a fast compressed model.

## Notes

- Two common ways people decide "what is state of the art": (1) check a public leaderboard and pick the top model; (2) run an internal benchmark. Both default to a "lazy solution" — just use a large foundation model (00:46-01:26).
- Leaderboards disagree. Three image-editing leaderboards (LM Arena now "Arena", Design Arena, Artificial Analysis) rank models differently; the "Human" model is rank 10 on Artificial Analysis but rank 5 on Arena (02:33-03:05). Elo/quality scores span different ranges (~1,100–1,300 on some, a completely different range on others), so cross-board relative strength is unreliable; there are also duplicate entries and models present on some boards and absent on others (03:38-03:47, 03:11-03:23).
- Solution: don't trust a single leaderboard, look at multiple; large ranking disagreement means some models are approximately equivalent, so being top-1 on one board does not mean best overall (03:52-04:15).
- Aggregate scores hide per-sub-task variance. You can build per-use-case leaderboards (removing objects, changing background, editing text) and rankings differ per sub-task; ChatGPT image is never top-1 on the per-sub-task rankings; no model consistently outperforms because models are trained more on some tasks than others (04:24-05:21). Target the leaderboard that matches your end use case (05:30-05:44).
- Statistical significance: leaderboards are built on a few thousand samples — low compared with real inference load. Pruna has models with "millions of [requests] per day," so they get more information by evaluating on their own API traffic than from a leaderboard (05:55-06:30).
- Win-rate insight: no model is near 100% win rate; most models lose at least 40% of their head-to-head battles, so if your use case falls in that 40%, picking the top-ranked model gives you the wrong model for nearly half of real use cases (06:30-07:05). Average on many samples, under conditions close to the final use-case setup (07:10-07:24).
- Manual inspection is biased. Audience demo: people preferred different images and some changed their minds; you are doubly biased — by your own preference and by the few specific samples you look at. Use it for a feeling but never trust it alone; ask many people and scale human evaluation properly (07:37-10:25).
- Automated metrics can be inconsistent. CLIP score (text-image alignment, 0–1 / 0–100) ranking 8 models changed across datasets with tiny between-model variation, so the "best" was unclear; for a known use case like text rendering, use text-rendering metrics, which gave consistent rankings with significant variations. Understand what a metric measures, and use multiple metrics rather than stopping at "I ran my benchmark" (10:28-12:18).
- Quality is driven by compute (12:18-12:30). The 26,000-battle image evaluation (~62s/image) cost 20 days of compute, $5,000, and 556 kWh ≈ ~400 marathons of energy on a slow model; the same evaluation on a fast compressed model (sub-1-second generation) took 7 hours and $265 (~4 marathons) (12:35-14:07). So look at efficiency, not only quality — the marginal quality gain often isn't worth the compute cost (14:13-14:27).
- The answer to "what model is state of the art" is that there are multiple. The preferred tool is a Pareto plot: x-axis an efficiency metric (latency or price per image), y-axis a quality score (Elo); the Pareto front holds 3–4 models, clustered tightly in quality (~1,100–1,200 Elo) but differing up to ~20x in efficiency. Even better, draw the front using a use-case-specific quality metric (e.g. text rendering) rather than general capability — Pruna optimized Flux 2 / Flux models (with BFL, Black Forest Labs) to be far faster while staying on the text-rendering Pareto front (14:27-16:02).
- Benchmarking is not dead — done properly with these rules, evaluation surfaces "a lot of small performance models" that are very good for your use case rather than one large foundation model (16:02-16:27). Takeaways: evaluate on many samples, match user use-case conditions, use multiple benchmarks, and weigh efficiency, not only quality (16:27-16:41).
- Pruna builds "performance models" served behind endpoints (fastest image models, video models running 1–5s) and ships open-source compression tooling and materials on efficiency research (16:41-17:18).
- Q&A — compression methods: many families. Quantization (a different quantization per module of the model — "super important"), pruning (remove unimportant components), and for image/video diffusion, attacking the denoiser steps: generation normally takes 20–50 steps, reducible via distillation or caching to ~20x or even ~4x fewer depending on aggressiveness; Pruna ships open-source caching algorithms plus internal ones for served models (17:52-19:15).
