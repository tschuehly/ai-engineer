## Summary

Denys Linkov (WiseDocs) gives a retrospective audit of a decision most teams face and few report on afterwards: his team spent six months collapsing more than ten repositories into a monorepo, and the talk asks "should we have waited a year to do this refactor as models and harnesses continue to get better, or did it make sense to do it at the time?" The setting is a document pipeline of ML models processing complex medical claims that arrive as PDFs "more than 10,000 pages in size. Some of these files are bigger than video files," where three problems converged: "we're too slow to meet customer demand," "this AI pipeline that we've built is too complicated to update," and a legacy estate of "more than 10 repos, nobody actually wants to touch the code. It's not a fun experience." The framing is tech debt as financial debt that "compounds in mysterious and sometimes unexpected ways," where the discipline is that debt taken on to ship a feature or land a customer must clear an ROI bar, because "if we introduce additional complexity into our code base, we can very quickly outrun the ROI we've generated." He sets that against an industry claim he does not accept at face value: after an audience poll on whether technical products improved over twenty years, five years, and the past year, he argues "we're going faster and faster through the technology life cycle, but we've lost something" — product focus, maintainability, and reliability have all degraded, illustrated with deliberately anonymized uptime dashboards from two leading companies sitting "below a three-nine or even four-nine reliability." **The talk's recurring instrument is a single real refactor task, re-run as each model generation shipped.** With o3 it "took 3 hours of back and forth chatting with [Cursor], but it made 10 major mistakes," and remained "a very manual process where you had to intervene and actually guide the model and manually edit or delete code." Re-run later, "Sonnet 4.6 with one additional iteration was able to solve the task" and Opus 4.8 "was basically able to one-shot this problem," so rebuilding the same task now "would take around 1/5 of the time." Two secondary observations travel with that: the *shape* of the interaction changed, since with o3 "there weren't substantial tool calls on certain categories" while in modern harnesses "we get sub-agents, we get some of those plan calls, we get different shell commands, and we get different verifications"; and the trade was priced, since "even though the model execution was a little bit more expensive, it was a lot less manual." **The counterweight is the zero-shot experiment.** Handed the whole job — the goal plus the names of the repositories, the underlying models, and other components — GPT 5.5 extra high "completed its goal in 10 minutes and 22 seconds" and "only wrote 2,000 lines of code, which was a little bit fishy. So I dug deeper. And it actually just implemented a bunch of scaffolding and didn't implement the models," a gap the model itself had written down in its own output: "I did not add a Ray Serve deployment or bootstrap command yet." His reading is that "we're still not there where models can self-validate and just one-shot these kinds of problems, but we're getting close," with a prediction that "in 6 months, we'll get to the point that we can complete pretty substantial refactors… consistently across the board." **The most transferable prescription is about how to read the METR task-length curve.** "Typically this graph is shared with the 50% accuracy rate, but I think it's much better to actually look at the 80% accuracy rate or higher," and better still "at 90% or 99%," because at those thresholds "we're no longer claiming that models can accomplish tasks that would take a human 18+ hours" and because the higher threshold is where delegation actually pays: "you construct a plan, you create a spec, you hand it off to an agent, and you're pretty sure that it'll get things done… You don't want to be creating a plan or a spec and then have a 50/50 chance of coming back and knowing that you wasted compute and your attention span." The arithmetic is blunt — "if you're kicking off a process that is going to take an hour and it has a 50% chance of completing, there's a very high chance you just wasted that hour and you could have been doing something different" — and it is grounded in a poll the room answered with raised hands, on having launched an agent and only then realized "the prompt, the plan, or the requirements were incomplete or missing." He adds that duration is not a single ordering: on METR's frontier-model results, success "starts to decline significantly at that 4-hour mark, but even before then at the 15-second mark or even before the 15-minute mark, there are certain tasks that [the model], in all its glory, cannot complete effectively and consistently." **The other half of the talk is what the six months bought.** The pre-work included roughly two months evaluating orchestrators for the AI pipeline across five open-source projects "before deep research came out as part of Google and OpenAI," done manually into a Confluence doc against 17 criteria and validated by building proofs of concept with a team of three; he now believes "we could do this 90% faster" with an agentic workflow — deep research, matched against the problem statements, sub-agents per criterion and per product, then POCs and evaluation — with one guardrail preserved: "it's very easy to undergo AI psychosis, where you look at a deep research report that's 20 pages long and you say, 'Wow, this looks good.' And then those features don't actually exist in the product, and you've set yourself back." The results are reported as velocity and reach rather than quality. Against previous repos that "had been around for more than six years" and moved "pretty slow," within "the first six months of the rebuild when we got to parity that we had before, that steepness of that curve is immense. And it didn't slow down after," with the team continuing to add features past the parity line; commit rate rose, more developers joined the contributions, and "now almost every developer within the company is committing to this new mono repo even though it might not be their area of expertise, but they might need to make changes to schemas, API calls, and other parts of the stack." Business outcomes claimed: pipeline time reduced, costs reduced, larger files supported, and features "that would take multiple months in under a week." The social result is offered as evidence in its own right — "beyond just shipping velocity, developers actually want to work in this codebase… 'Hey, can I work in this codebase? It's much cleaner compared to the other ones'" — and "a lot of the patterns we have adopted here have spread to other repos within the company." He states the opposing case fairly: models, tool calling, sandboxes, and monitoring frameworks keep improving, so "taking on technical debt and refactoring later is getting exponentially easier as the days go by." His answer is that the pile you would hand the future model is growing too, because "when you build a lot of code and you do this kind of development in an AI-native world, it starts looking like some of the legacy code we've seen in the past. There's a lot of code written. It's written with low performance or quality, and the broader problem is people don't actually understand what's happening there" — so change against customer requirements gets harder, and "you do have to make sure that there are appropriate guardrails, whether or not you do a full refactor or only a partial one." Verdict: worthwhile, with the closing note that "the AI delivery system is a layered approach. You can isolate different parts of your codebase to avoid a full refactor," and "sometimes it's good to pause, build a monorepo, and forge ahead." The Q&A supplies the sharpest single technical finding: asked why consolidate at all, he answers that "models are much better at navigating multiple repos. So, if you put it into a higher-level folder… they could navigate the file directory. But, for doing that end-to-end testing and verification and deployment, it's still much harder to do with multiple repos. And if you're building a sandbox environment to run sort of a full AI factory, it also takes more time to clone repos and get everything set up." He also reports 15 of 17 requirements right going into the refactor, all-human PR review throughout ("PRs were really good way for us to build context for that repo… we wanted to make sure people understood what had gone into the refactor") alongside skills run as local review checks, plan mode adopted mid-project when it "was just barely coming into [Claude] Code and didn't exist in Cursor," and a closing prediction that the wrapper around the edit — research, POC work, validating code quality, checking hidden assumptions like an open-source feature that "was actually in a beta" — is what will speed up most, "on top of the standard refactoring of… here's a file, rewrite it to match this set of requirements."

## Extracted Concepts

- [Read the Task-Length Curve at the Success Rate You Would Actually Delegate At](../concepts/read-the-task-length-curve-at-the-success-rate-you-would-delegate-at.md) - the 50% time horizon is the wrong number for deciding whether to launch an hour-long agent run, because a coin flip costs you the hour and your attention.
- [Audit a Refactor Against Having Waited for Better Models](../concepts/audit-a-refactor-against-having-waited-for-better-models.md) - deferring genuinely gets cheaper every month, but AI-native development manufactures the same illegible code faster than the discount arrives.
- [Multi-Repo Cost Has Moved From Navigation to Verification](../concepts/multi-repo-cost-has-moved-from-navigation-to-verification.md) - models now read across repositories fine; what still breaks is end-to-end testing, deployment, and standing up the sandbox.
- [Re-Run One Remembered Hard Task on Each New Model](../concepts/re-run-one-remembered-hard-task-on-each-new-model.md) - a single real task you already solved gives a longitudinal capability reading measured in human interventions, so it needs no scorer.
- [Validate a Research Report by Building the Thing It Recommends](../concepts/validate-a-research-report-by-building-the-thing-it-recommends.md) - a fluent twenty-page comparison can describe product features that do not exist, and only an instantiation catches it.

### Provenance and Limits

- A ~15-minute practitioner talk plus roughly three minutes of Q&A, covering one company, one team, and one refactor. No Q&A questions are audible; the speaker restates each before answering.
- The talk's central complaint is that shipping speed rose while quality fell, but every reported result of its own refactor is a velocity, reach, or cost measure — commit rate, lines of code (self-flagged as "not a great metric"), contributor count, pipeline duration, cost, feature lead time. No defect rate, incident rate, uptime, or static-analysis figure is given for the new monorepo, so the talk does not test its own thesis on itself.
- Charts are shown and described but never read out numerically: the commit graph is a log-scale plot with a dotted parity line, and no axis values, absolute commit counts, or team sizes appear in the transcript.
- The "90% faster now" figure for the orchestrator evaluation is an estimate of a counterfactual, not a re-run. The redesigned agentic workflow is described as what they *could* build, not as something executed and measured.
- The model comparison is one task. "10 major mistakes" is not scored against a rubric, "one additional iteration" is undefined, and the harness is held constant only loosely — o3 ran "in Cursor" while the harness used for the Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.8 re-runs is never named, which matters because the talk simultaneously credits "modern harnesses" for the improvement.
- The zero-shot GPT 5.5 experiment is a single run from a single prompt, with no retry, no variation of the prompt, and no report of what a second attempt would have produced.
- The Spotify and Stripe case studies are invoked as evidence of industry progress with no numbers, and the anonymized uptime dashboards are presented as symptomatic of AI-accelerated shipping without any causal evidence linking the two.
- The METR material is a recommendation about how to read someone else's chart, not new data. The frontier-model evaluation is recalled from "roughly a month ago" with only the 4-hour decline point and the short-task failure observation quoted; the model's name is unclear in the captions (see below).
- "In 6 months, we'll get to the point that we can complete pretty substantial refactors… consistently across the board" is a prediction with no stated basis beyond the observed trend.
- The 15-of-17 requirements figure is given without saying which two were missed or what it cost to be wrong about them.
- The refactor's own cost is unpriced. Beyond "a team of three" for the POC phase, there is no engineer-month count, token spend, opportunity-cost estimate, or account of what did not ship during the six months — which is exactly the input a reader would need to run the same now-versus-later comparison.
- Verification during the refactor is described as human PR review, skills used for local review checks, and plan mode. No test suite, coverage target, CI gate, or regression check on the legacy behavior is mentioned anywhere, despite parity with a six-year-old pipeline being the milestone.
