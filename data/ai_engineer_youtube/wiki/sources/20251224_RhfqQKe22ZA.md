## Summary

Joel Becker reconciles METR's rising long-task benchmark curves with a field study where experienced open-source maintainers were slower when AI tools were allowed. The durable lesson is that benchmark capability, especially on low-context contained tasks, does not automatically imply productivity on high-context repository work where reliability, mergeability, task interdependence, and developer context retention dominate.

## Notes

- Conventional benchmark scores can be difficult to interpret because the distance from random performance to human baseline or 100% does not directly answer whether a model is useful in real work. (01:47-02:50)
- Benchmarks increasingly have a short window between first signal and saturation, which motivates measuring task difficulty by the human time required under matched conditions. (02:52-06:04)
- METR's time-horizon method fits model success against human task completion time and calls the 50% predicted-success point the model's time horizon; Claude 3 Opus was described around 4 minutes and o1-preview around 15 minutes on the measured distribution. (04:34-06:22)
- The time-horizon curve appeared close to exponential over calendar time, with a cited doubling interval around six to seven months, but the talk cautions that even this benchmark family can eventually saturate. (06:22-09:02)
- Field experiments were proposed as a more externally valid complement because they measure high-context developers doing real tasks in mature repositories rather than low-context experts attempting contained tasks. (09:36-10:36)
- The RCT involved 16 experienced developers, roughly 16 real GitHub issues each, random assignment to AI-disallowed or AI-allowed conditions, and Cursor Pro access in the AI-allowed condition. (10:40-11:55)
- The studied repositories included large mature projects such as GHC, scikit-learn, and Hugging Face Transformers; participating developers averaged top-contributor status and about five years of repository contribution history. (11:58-12:29)
- Experts expected roughly 40% time savings, participants expected roughly 24-25% beforehand and perceived roughly 20% afterward, but measured elapsed time showed a 19% slowdown when AI was allowed. (12:29-13:24)
- Proposed contributors to the slowdown include overoptimism, implicit repository context, high developer familiarity, large complex repositories, and low AI reliability requiring verification and correction. (14:14-15:34)
- Caveats include narrow generalization to expert maintainers on complex long-lived open-source repositories, the March 2025 study period, small study size, possible tool-learning effects, possible incentive effects, and continuing follow-up work. (15:56-18:10)
- Reconciliation hypotheses include reliability thresholds, differences between unit-test-like scoring and mergeability, low-context versus high-context human baselines, task distribution messiness, capability elicitation differences, and task interdependence where delegating a subtask can harm context for later work. (18:22-20:22)
