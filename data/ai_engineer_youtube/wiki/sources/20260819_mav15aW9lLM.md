# Why Your Enterprise Tech Stack Isn't Ready for AI Agents — Christopher Lovejoy & Saul Howard

## Summary

Christopher Lovejoy (forward deployed engineer, Anterior) and Saul Howard (VP of engineering, Anterior) argue that a successful enterprise AI proof of concept usually dies on questions the POC's architecture cannot answer — the audit trail, the data lifecycle, the escalation mechanism, and the evals — and that the fix is to take those constraints as the foundation and rebuild toward the POC's accuracy on top of them. They present three primitives that answer the first three questions and make the fourth fall out for free: an immutable append-only event log as the single source of truth (so auditability is a property of the storage model rather than a reconstruction), schema-driven object storage sitting *beside* the log with events holding only references (so developers can retrace an agent without seeing PHI, and zero-trust tokens plus process segregation constrain the lethal trifecta), and a platform-level definition of "agent" wide enough to include humans (so any action an LLM can take a human can take, at any point in the chain, with downstream steps indifferent to which one acted). Evals then emerge from those three: replay for exact counterfactuals, human-versus-agent difference as the eval score, and eval runs on production data inside the customer's own environment. The framing is healthcare-specific but explicitly generalized to finance, defense, and government — anywhere process compliance is mandatory.

## Notes

- Speakers and scope: Lovejoy is "a member of technical staff at Anterior" working "as a forward deployed engineer. So I embed within enterprise organizations and help them get value from using AI agents"; Howard is "VP of engineering at Anterior. We're a New York based company selling … agentic AI to US health insurance companies," 00:14-00:43.
- Why healthcare, and what transfers: it is challenging "because of the requirements around process and … compliance, the regulatory requirements," and "because of the … direct real impact that your work has on people's lives, which is of course also what makes it so rewarding"; but "a lot of the learnings you can take from working in enterprise for health care, you can take to enterprise in other regulated industries. Like finance, defense, government work, anywhere where process is so important and has to be followed," 00:50-01:37.
- The POC setup: identify a customer and a priority use case (a large health system, "some sort of administrative healthcare workflow"), scope the POC, "define the metrics that you are going to care about," allocate two engineers, spend four weeks building, 01:51-02:27.
- What the build actually touches: "An enterprise stack is very complicated… generally you can have an application layer, a control plane layer, the data plane," plus model-provider access, and "your POC is going to need access to data across all of these different planes. It may be some in the data lake, some directly from the application layer" — some offline pulling, some online, 02:32-03:12.
- The happy meeting: results hit the performance metrics, it is fast and relatively cheap; finance wants next year's budget impact, the chief medical officer wants to tell colleagues how accurate the AI is, the head of sales asks "when can we put powered by AI … on the websites?" — "but the problem is that everyone here is assuming that the … hard part is done, that the AI was … the challenging part. But actually, as we know, often getting things into production is really where the challenge lies," 03:14-03:56.
- The next day's questions, in order: the audit trail ("for compliance, it's critical that we can see every step, every action that the agent takes, every piece of data that it accesses"); sensitive-data handling ("we have a very strict boundary around where our data can go and where it can't go… is this respecting that?"); approval and escalation ("in certain scenarios, we have to escalate to a clinician who will then … approve or … not agree with what the agent is saying… what's the mechanism for that?"); and then more — "can untrusted data manipulate the model? How do we know that the agent continues to perform well? How do we deal with integrations? How do we connect to Epic, to Salesforce…", 04:00-05:09. The talk answers four and explicitly defers integrations and continued-performance monitoring for time, 05:09-05:18.
- Audit-trail definition: "for programmers, an audit trail sounds very like a typical developer log you might have in DataDog," but under "SOC 2, HITRUST, HIPAA … it has to contain a complete record of absolutely every action that the agent took … all of the places where the agent accessed data, all of the authorization by which the agent did something," 05:23-06:07. The test is legal: "say our agent's decisions came up in a court of law. Could we show a justifiable chain of evidence for why the particular actions were taken by a decision? And that's something that could easily happen within the health care context," 06:09-06:27.
- Architecture as constraint selection: "when I'm choosing my constraints, I'm saying, 'Okay, these are the things I want my system to be easy' and let that drive the trade-offs," 06:29-06:45. The borrowed pattern is the finance transaction log: "an immutable record of events that store all of the transactions that happen throughout the system," append-only and timestamped, complete ("this is your source of truth for all of the data of the system") and unified ("there's only one source of truth across all of the different agents that you might have running in parallel"), 06:47-07:21.
- The payoff and its price: "auditability becomes trivial. It falls out of your data storage paradigm… It sort of is impossible not to be able to roll back time and … see exactly the state of the system at a … particular point in time," 07:21-07:44. The event-sourcing tradeoff: "writes become very easy. So, you just drop an event. Reads become more difficult because you have to read through all of the events in order to reconstruct a view of what happened," softened by "caching and snapshots" but "there always is more effort there," 07:46-08:14.
- The unexpected upside in healthcare: "you're going to want different interpretations of the raw data that your agents recorded after the fact… it might be that more events happened and that changes the interpretation of the healthcare journey, and you want a different view of the … source of truth at that particular time. And this pattern makes that easy because all of your views of the data are ephemeral computed projections of the event log," 08:14-08:46.
- Data lifecycle: PHI "has legal restrictions around it. Not just HIPAA, but other legal restrictions about the use of people's data. You cannot have your agent, just as you cannot have humans, accessing and reading and utilizing healthcare data that they don't absolutely have a necessity to use at that … point in time for that particular journey," 08:47-09:27.
- Shape of healthcare data, which drives the storage choice: complicated, "doesn't follow strict hierarchical … relationships," sometimes unstructured and sometimes structured, potentially very large ("one piece of health care data can easily be over a megabyte in size or … much more"), under strict role-based access control "both for humans and … then for agents downstream of that," and sometimes not permitted to leave the customer's environment at all — "I've seen customers where they're not willing to have their health care data leave their own environment, leave their on prem VPC," so "we have tangential access to their … data," 09:28-10:25.
- Object storage beside the log: schema-driven object storage "matches well with the choice of using event logging because you can separate the two. So, the events … only contain references to the schema driven blobs that are the storage of the actual health care data itself," and the data is stored immutably "so that you can always go back in time and reconstruct what data the agent had access to at that particular point in time," 10:25-11:03.
- Debugging without exposure: developers "go back and debug and have observability over what happened, what particular steps the agent took, why it did that, and … retrace the agent's steps without having access to the personal health information itself. Although because of the schema driven, they can see the shape of that data" — and often "won't be able to be given access to that health care data." The general form: "you can separate out observability and orchestration and instrumentation from the health care data itself," 11:04-11:52.
- Zero trust and the lethal trifecta: "the object storage becomes a place where you can apply zero trust principles. Your agents can bear tokens and use those tokens to access the data at the point of use and not allow data to flow around the system as it likes," 11:52-12:14. The trifecta is treated as a solvable constraint: "if I have an agent at point A with access to this data, is it possible within my architecture for the agent to be also accessing data over here?" — tokens borne by agents plus "object storage segregated from the event stream that has your orchestration logic gives you a place to be able to solve for that constraint. It won't be possible for the agent to access data within the same process that … you've given it the … previous data," 12:15-12:56.
- Escalation's two difficulties: it is dynamic and unpredictable — the agent may escalate when unsure, or a rule may fire ("in a medical context, the treatments going above a certain threshold means that it needs to be escalated … for an approval") — and "humans and LLMs ultimately process context differently. You know, LLMs will have no problem if you give them massive massive amounts of text, but humans that's not the case," 12:57-13:47.
- The equivalency primitive: "if in your platform you enforce … a wider definition of agent which encompasses both LLMs and humans, then you can make it such that any action that can be taken by an LLM could also be taken by a human… at any point in the kind of chain of actions that your agent is taking, it can escalate to a human, the human could perform that action, and then any step downstream doesn't care about whether it was a human or an LLM that did those actions upstream," 13:47-14:16. On context: "you can define methods that take the context, which has some kind of shared definition of context, which is irrespective of whether it's a human or an LLM that's going to be accessing it. And you can take those methods to then map into something that's agent friendly, like a prompt, or into something that's more human friendly, for example, a UI," 14:19-14:41.
- Why evals are hard here: LLMs "are not deterministic, so it can be quite tricky to pin down the precise change that led to some sort of change in outputs"; offline data "might not necessarily represent production data … maybe you sampled from data, but actually that sample isn't truly representative"; and "you also have drift of data over time, so maybe your offline data set is now out of date," 14:46-15:32.
- Evals as byproduct: the immutable ledger means "you can replay your actions… you can see the complete state of the system at that point in time. And if you wanted to, you could then make very specific tweaks. So you could tweak a prompt, you could tweak a model, you could tweak the code, and you can see the exact direct impact of that because you have all of that context," 15:36-16:15. Human-agent equivalency means "for any task, you could get both the agent, the LLM agent, and the human to perform it, and your difference is your eval, that gives you the eval scores," 16:16-16:29. Object storage means you can "run these evals on production data including inside your customer's environment without actually ever exposing that data. You can get your eval results without the sensitive data ever needing to come to where your agent is performing the work," 16:30-16:46.
- Closing recap of the four principles: "the immutable ledger of actions, the orchestration adjacent object storage, the human agent equivalency, and the way that with these three principles evals can emerge as a first-class property of the system rather than as something you attach onto the side," 16:48-17:15.
- The architectural stance: "I like to think about architecture as taking your constraints very seriously and thinking about what you want to be simple within the system and then choosing the trade-offs for that. And of course, alongside that, some things will become hard, but it's the things that are simple that are most important to you," 17:16-17:37. And the patterns are not new: "there are patterns that already exist across enterprises that solve for a lot of these things. And sure, with AI, we need to combine them in new, sometimes radical ways and bring in … other pieces, but there are patterns that have worked very well within finance, within defense, within big tech," 17:37-17:56.
- The failure and success shapes: "where I've seen it go wrong is taking that initial POC … that showed the high accuracy … and then trying to build up from it, strapping on the enterprise requirements as you come across them. Okay, we need eval, we need … security, we need auditability, and bolting these on as additions to the … foundations of the POC. You end up with something very brittle, something very hard to … externalize and to generalize across different use cases." Where it goes well: "take the constraints of a production-ready, scaled enterprise … system seriously from the beginning and treat those as the architectural principles that you're going to build everything upon and then build back up towards that POC accuracy using your new primitives," 17:56-18:52.
