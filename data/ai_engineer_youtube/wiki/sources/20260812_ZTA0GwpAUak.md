## Summary

Sam Denton, who leads the platform research team at Applied Compute, gives the wiki's most operational account of weight-level continual learning for enterprise agents. His organizing device is a grid built from two axes that are usually collapsed into the single word "online." The first is how on-policy the *traces* are — a one-time dump of production traces, a daily batch, or the "holy grail" where a model serves production traffic and each rollout immediately updates it. The second is where the *hint* comes from: the privileged information used to build a teacher smarter than the student, either a static prior held before any rollout ("a customer support agent that is too willing to give refunds") or a hint constructed dynamically against what the on-policy model just did. Applied Compute works two corners. Offline hints on offline traces need no replayable environment, which is what lets them "improve for free today" from a data dump; online hints on online traces have the higher ceiling because one judge adapts to whatever the model does in production. The constraint held throughout is that none of this may assume a golden answer — "a lot of distillation work is done assuming you have some kind of golden answer that you can distill into the model. And this is often not the case."

Two results carry the talk. On SWE-bench, a Qwen thinking model was taking up to 80 turns to submit; the goal was wrapping up by turn 40. Task-complete tool-call rate went from about 22% to 60% with test pass rate flat (slightly up). The mechanism is the interesting part and Denton flags it as surprising: because the rollout was conditioned on an old production trace that never called the tool, the teacher never touched the tool-call tokens at all — it moved the reasoning path toward the call, and the call followed. The second case is a customer whose harness required an unusual hyperlink format, "very, very out of distribution for previously post-trained models." Rewarding the format directly and SFT on correctly formatted traces both degraded overall coding-agent performance; a hint written against each individual rollout ("in your prior rollout, you'd formatted hyperlinks like this. Next time, make sure to format hyperlinks in this way instead") took correct formatting from about 15% to about 80%, while the same hint applied uniformly to every rollout "climb[ed] the behavior a little bit, but far less."

Two implementation practices close the talk. Per-step hinting: rather than putting the hint at the start of a rollout, a judge decides where in the rollout it belongs, and distillation runs only on the next step or a few steps forward, because the KL learning signal visibly decays with distance from the hint. And relevance-masked self-distillation: an LLM judge selects which teacher tokens are learned from, because "the teacher model has preferences of certain connector words that are not really relevant to actually what we're trying to teach the student" — which they report both improves learning of a very out-of-distribution behavior and reduces catastrophic degradation.

## Notes

- Speaker and scope: "my name's Sam Denton. Uh I lead the platform research team at Applied Compute." The agenda is the distillation spectrum, where value accrues along it, data from enterprise deployments, tips and tricks, and lessons. He assumes the audience already knows what distillation and self-distillation are — "I figured by 3:00 p.m. on a continual learning day, you'd had enough of it." (00:12-01:15)
- The trace axis, stated as a spectrum with three named points: at the offline end, "you get a single batch of traces from a production agent, and you're meant to just do something with it… you want to learn via hindsight, you want to learn via the mistakes the agent made"; in the middle, "a daily batch of production traces"; at the online end, "this sort of unified engine of putting inference and training together… I have a model that's serving production traffic, it does a rollout, it creates a trace, we figure out how to learn from that trace, we update the model, and then we serve the next production request." He calls the last one "the holy grail of continual learning." (01:24-02:48)
- Where customers actually are: "on the offline side of traces, this is sort of where a lot of enterprises are today… we have a bunch of production traffic, we have a bunch of traces, like figure out a way to make our agent better. It's clearly doing something, but it clearly can be better." Applied Compute's stated goal is "to meet enterprises where they are." A smaller set is "willing to sort of start getting something into production, which looks like this fully online continual learning." (02:50-03:39)
- The second axis and why it exists: "the whole point of distillation is we have some kind of information that allows us to create a teacher model, which is smarter than the student model or the on-policy model. Um in order to create a teacher that's smarter than this on-policy model, we need to create some kind of hint or have some kind of privileged information. And so the question is where does this hint come from?" (03:43-04:07)
- Offline hints: "deriving hints from some static or offline data. So this can be potentially known rubrics for a single task. It can be general priors about behavior that needs to get better, such as like a customer support agent that is too willing to give refunds." The defining property is independence: "it's independent of the online model's rollout." (04:07-04:42)
- Online hints: "hints that are dynamically constructed from the online rollout… the goal is to create a hint that is completely dependent on the online rollout and the online policy that's doing the work." Behavior priors can still be injected alongside. (04:42-05:05)
- The grid, with the caveat attached: "which leaves us with a very nice 2 by 2 grid… And again, these are all spectrums, right? So I've drawn lines and put things in boxes where boxes sometimes don't make sense as boxes, but more as spectrums." (05:11-05:33)
- Quadrant 1, offline hint on an offline production trace: "we take a trace from production and we have some prior about how generally production traces aren't good enough… and we construct a hint that we apply to all of these offline production traces." (05:33-06:05)
- Quadrant 2, offline hint on an online rollout: "we have a hint of a behavior we're trying to improve. We let an online agent do a rollout. We inject some hint that we're injecting into all of our rollouts. Try and update the policy model, and then continue serving production traffic." (06:05-06:28)
- Quadrant 3, the hybrid: "off policy traces with some on policy step and hints that are constructed against that one on policy step… the trace that led to the point where I inject a hint was fully off policy. It was some production trace that came from a few days ago. And we are using our on policy model to just roll out one step without actually having to interact with the environment. And we construct the hint based on what that on policy model did in that one step." (06:28-07:15)
- Quadrant 4: "we have an on policy model do a bunch of things in production. It finishes. We stop. We look at what the online model did. We then create a hint dynamically from the full rollout… and then we do some sort of distillation against this online production trace with an online hint." (07:15-07:44)
- The two corners they work in, and why. Quadrant 1: "we don't actually have to have replayability of a production environment… We can take a bunch of production traces, and we just look at what happened… And this allows us to really target specific behaviors." Quadrant 4: "our most scalable solution… this can be done with either a replayable environment or actually serving production traffic. And because we're constructing these hints online in a dynamic way, that we can actually cater to a bunch of different behaviors via distillation." (07:44-09:07)
- The commercial framing of the grid: "we can improve for free today, and we can raise all ceilings tomorrow. We can improve for free today by using offline production traces. Give us a dump of your production data. We'll find a way to make it valuable. And then as we deploy an online policy model… we can then raise the ceilings continuously by updating the model as it's serving production traffic." (09:20-09:49)
- The constraint, stated as a personal frustration with the field: "we want to do this without having access to some golden answer… a lot of distillation work is done assuming you have some kind of golden answer that you can distill into the model. And this is often not the case… we want to think about how we can do continual learning and distillation without having some beautifully golden rubric to accompany every task." He adds a structural prediction: "as the infra collapses between serving and training, we're automatically going to raise the ceiling continuously via online distillation." (09:49-10:36)
- SWE-bench setup: "take a Qwen 3.5 thinking model against SWE-bench and get it to submit its reasoning faster than it normally does. So on SWE-bench, we found that this model was essentially taking like up to 80 turns to submit its answer. What we wanted to do was encourage it to call a tool to submit its task before turn 40." The stated purpose is a proof to themselves that the behavior is installable "without letting it do its normal sort of full reasoning chain." (10:40-11:21)
- Three metrics, and why three. Task complete rate: "the percentage of the time that the agent calls this tool to finish a submission." Test pass rate: "how we measure the regression in performance on sort of the base task… the percentage of the time that the environment passes all tests accompanying the SWE-bench task irrespective of whether the agent… submitted the task via this tool call." SWE-bench pass rate: "the intersection of those two behaviors." The goal is stated as a joint condition — "raise the SWE-bench pass rate performance while not degrading the test pass rate." (11:20-12:04)
- The hint text itself: "you are near your 40-turn limit. There's only about three turns left… You often keep exploring and forget to wrap up investigating. So, finalize and verify your fix and then call this tool before you run out of time." (12:04-12:22)
- The result: "the task complete call rate increases dramatically from about 22% to 60%," while the test pass rate "is relatively constant. In fact, it goes up a little bit." (12:22-12:48)
- The mechanism, which he calls "the really interesting surprising result": this ran fully offline on "a trace that was created ahead of time that never basically never called this task complete tool. And we're nudging… the student model towards calling this task complete tool call without ever specifically changing the tokens for the tool call. Because again, the rollout is conditioned on the production trace… it never had the reasoning path to think to call the tool call. And so, the teacher doesn't force the tool call. It just starts to force the reasoning path towards the tool call without ever actually changing the tool call." (12:48-13:38)
- Quadrant 3 as an upgrade on the same task: "you can roll out just one step from the on policy model given an offline production trace. And when we do this, we obviously see that the student model sort of learns to wrap up its reasoning, and eventually the teacher starts encouraging it to actually call this tool token… by having something that's a little bit more on policy that we're able to increase sort of the SWE-bench pass rate more than in the fully offline world." No numbers are given for this comparison. (13:38-14:17)
- The production case: "we needed to teach a coding agent to use very specific formatting for hyperlinks due to a certain harness nuance of one of our customers. And obviously this coding agent needed to not regress on any of the base coding agent capabilities." The difficulty is stated as distributional: "these hyperlink formats were very very out of distribution for previously post trained models." (14:17-14:51)
- Two approaches that failed: "when we tried things like adding a reward for specific hyperlink formatting, or even doing SFT on traces where we knew the hyperlink was correctly formatted, we saw that there was this sort of degradation in overall coding agent performance." (14:51-15:09)
- What worked, as an online trace with an online hint: "we would do a rollout, then we would inject a hint specific to the rollout that occurred from the on policy model, and then say, 'In your prior rollout, you'd formatted hyperlinks like this. Next time, make sure to format hyperlinks in this way instead.'… the percentage of correct hyperlink formatting jumped drastically from about, I guess, 15% all the way up to around 80%." (15:09-15:36)
- The controlled comparison on the same task: offline hinting means "for every single rollout, apply the same hint, which says, 'Remember that when you do hyperlinks, you have to format it this way.'… we do climb the behavior a little bit, but far less than in this online hinting world." (15:36-15:56)
- Per-step hinting: "rather than injecting a hint to the beginning of a rollout, we use a judge to essentially decide where in the rollout we should be injecting hints. And then… it's best to just do distillation on that next step that occurs or maybe a few steps forward rather than the entire rollout. Because that's really the turn in the moment in time that you want to have the teacher teach something to the student." The supporting observation: "this KL learning signal basically goes down as you get further and further away from the hint, which makes sense." (16:13-16:57)
- Relevance-masked self-distillation: "we use an LLM judge to sample and choose which tokens we actually learn from from our teacher model. Um because often, we'll see that the teacher model has preferences of certain connector words that are not really relevant to actually what we're trying to teach the student." Reported effect: "we're able to increase our ability to learn a very, very out of distribution behavior, while also being better about avoiding catastrophic degradation." A blog post on their website is cited but not summarized. (16:57-17:36)
- The closing summary of the two-corner strategy: offline hinting with offline production traces "to provide value on day one to enterprise clients. Give us production traces and we can teach you a certain behavior," then online hinting with online traces for "this highest ceiling sort of continuous learning improvement across multiple improvement areas, because that judge is able to adapt to whatever the online model does in production." (17:40-18:26)

## Provenance and Caveats

- This is a vendor talk. Sam Denton leads platform research at Applied Compute, the results are the company's own, and the talk closes on hiring ("reach out to hiring@appliedcompute.com"). The grid is Applied Compute's taxonomy, not a field-standard one, and he says so by presenting the boxes as spectrums.
- **Every number is from unpublished internal runs.** No seeds, no variance, no sample counts, and no confidence intervals are reported for the 22%→60% or 15%→80% results. The SWE-bench task variant, the harness, and the customer are not identified beyond "a certain production use case."
- The quadrant-3 claim — one on-policy step raises the SWE-bench pass rate "more than in the fully offline world" — is stated with a chart reference and no numbers. It is the weakest-supported claim in the talk and the one most likely to be worth reproducing before relying on.
- The two failed approaches on the hyperlink task (format reward, SFT on correct traces) are reported as "degradation in overall coding agent performance" with no magnitude, so the comparison against the 15%→80% online-hint result is directional only.
- No cost accounting appears anywhere: the online-hint loop runs a judge per rollout and a teacher forward pass, and the talk never prices that against the offline path it says is cheaper to adopt.
- "Relevance masked self-distillation" is presented as a named in-house technique with a blog post cited but not walked through; the masking policy, the judge prompt, and the fraction of tokens masked are all unstated.
- The wiki already holds an earlier Applied Compute talk — [Efficient Reinforcement Learning](20251209_o15AaYl7Wu0.md) (2025-12-09), on pipeline RL and run-layout simulation. That talk's loop "grades final answers"; this one is organized around not having an answer to grade. The two are the same company's systems layer and method layer eight months apart, and reading them together is more informative than either alone.
