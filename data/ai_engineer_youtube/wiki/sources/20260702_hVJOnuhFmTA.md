# The Prompt Is Still a Punch Card

## Summary

Ted Johnson (co-founder of JoinIn.AI; 25 years building enterprise software, collaboration systems, and AI interfaces) reframes AI as an *interface* technology, not only an *intelligence* technology, and diagnoses why powerful models still "feel unnatural to use." He separates any human-machine interface into three parts: the **channel** (the physical transport that carries intent — keyboard, microphone, screen, prompt box; "bandwidth"), **expression** (the range and richness of meaning the channel is permitted to carry), and the **protocol** (the rules and shape of the exchange). The thesis: with LLMs the channel never changed (still typing into a box, still hitting submit) and expression *exploded* (from assembly opcodes → shell commands/flags → programming primitives → natural language, which "blew the menu open"), but the protocol stayed **batch** — inherited from the punch card, which inherited it from the weaving loom. Prompting *is* the batch protocol: assemble the whole request, submit, wait, read, find one thing wrong, resubmit. "Interactive sprinkles" (streaming updates, summaries) and faster wait times fooled us into thinking it became interactive; "shorter batch is still batch." Prompt engineering is just a flattering name for a set of rules for packaging a good batch deck ("think step by step," "act as an expert," paste more/less context, talk only in markdown) — the same mastery a punch-card operator had. The mismatch is that model capacity (reasoning, speech, vision, memory, planning) curves up while the protocol stays flat, so the *human* still carries every interface burden (deciding what context matters, remembering what to ask, choosing timing, noticing ambiguity, repairing output, engineering the prompt); when it feels like work and fails, people wrongly blame themselves ("I'm bad at AI"). The fix is to move timing and modality off the human and let the AI *participate* — using the affordances humans already use with each other (a question, a pause, a sketch, a checklist, a quiet aside, or saying nothing). Voice examples: a speech-to-speech model has "exactly one slot" and answers "Hey Ted, come on in" because it can't tell the words weren't for it; OpenAI's GPT real-time 2 (late May) adds backchannels ("mhm"); NVIDIA's research model handles interruption/turn-taking; and JoinIn's own approach adds a utility-driven, multi-party protocol that labels each statement (question/proposal/answer) and only takes the floor when no one else holds it. Cites Sherry Turkle (MIT): "conversation is the most human and humanizing thing we do."

## Notes

- Three-part frame: channel (physical transport / bandwidth), expression (range of meaning permitted through), protocol (rules/shape of the exchange). Channel is a keyboard, mic, screen, punch card, or prompt box. 01:38-04:00, 05:43-06:20
- Channels have barely changed (keyboards ~180 years, computer channels ~15 years); with AI "the channel never really changed — you're still typing into a box," but what you're *permitted to express* through it changed. 03:02-04:41, 06:00-06:14
- Expression ladder: assembly instruction set (dozens of opcodes) → shell commands/flags → composable programming primitives → natural language. Each earlier step is "a fixed vocabulary, requiring you to express your intent by choosing from a menu"; natural language "blew that menu open." 05:03-05:50
- Protocol is "the one that hasn't kept up." Punch-card batch = encode the whole request in advance away from the machine, submit the deck, wait (sometimes overnight), read the printout, fix one thing, resubmit; "the machine never engaged with you while you were thinking." Batch itself came from the weaving loom (set the pattern, then run the cloth). 06:14-07:09, 09:36-09:56
- Prompting maps 1:1 onto that loop; "interactive features" (updates, summaries) are "batch with interactive sprinkles"; shrinking the wait to seconds "fooled us into thinking it became interactive... it's still batch. You still package a complete turn before the machine is allowed to participate." 07:09-08:07
- "Prompt engineering" is a flattering label for rules to package a good batch deck (think step-by-step, give examples, act/don't act as an expert, paste more/less context, only talk in markdown — "we trade incantations"); the mastery is the punch-card operator's "knowing exactly how to assemble the deck so the job wouldn't fail." 08:13-08:52
- Prompts/command lines/punch cards were "brilliant solutions for constraints of their time"; the real question is whether batch is still the right protocol now that the machine "no longer needs us to" pre-package intent — it can ask a follow-up, clarify mid-thought, notice something missing. 08:52-09:36
- Capability-vs-interface mismatch: model capacity (reasoning, speech, vision, memory, planning) curves up while the protocol stays flat (box + submit button), so the human still decides context, remembers what to ask, chooses timing, notices ambiguity, repairs output, engineers the prompt — "the intelligence feels magical; it's the interface that still feels like work." When it feels like work and fails, users blame themselves; "the mismatch isn't the user, it's the interface." 10:10-11:06
- Single-slot failure: a frontier speech-to-speech ("voice mode") model answered "Hey Ted, come on in" as a turn because a prompt box "has no concept of who's speaking, whether the words were even meant for it." 11:03-12:07
- Field is converging on participation: OpenAI's GPT real-time 2 (released late May, tried in voice mode) backchannels ("mhm," "right") to show active listening; NVIDIA's research model ("Personal Plex") yields on interruption and picks the thread back up — "real turn taking, listening and speaking at once in real time." 12:29-13:44
- But "making listening noises is not really the same as knowing who's in the room"; those models "are not trained to tell that 'Hey Ted' wasn't meant for it." JoinIn's protocol adds understanding of human/group conversation. 13:44-14:10
- JoinIn's demo protocol: a utility-driven model creates goals to fulfill, labels each participant statement as a question/proposal/answer, and "only takes a turn when no one else is speaking or holding the floor"; tracking who a speaker is referring to is "critical." In a meeting it resolved a scope objective with "no one wrote the prompt, no one patched the turn and hit submit — the system was in the conversation, following it, understanding, and choosing its moment." 14:35-16:31
- Mindset shift: "AI is not just an intelligence technology; it's increasingly becoming an interface technology," so "book-smart models alone are not enough." Stop picturing AI as "a smarter machine hiding behind prompts, agents, loops"; see intelligence as something that can "remove interface constraints." 16:31-17:55
- Design question: "what burden are we still putting on humans only because the machine used to be too limited to carry that burden itself?" The right answer isn't always chat, voice, or "a wall of markdown" — it's the affordances humans already use (a question, a pause, a sketch, a checklist, a quiet aside, or saying nothing), with "timing and modality... not the human's job anymore." 17:55-19:07
- Computing has mostly improved "how humans encode their intent for machines," each step carrying the old constraint forward as "a translation tax, a precision tax, context tax, repair tax"; AI is the chance to put those down — not by making everything magical or voice, but "by making computers for once more fluent with us." Cites Sherry Turkle (MIT): "conversation is the most human and humanizing thing we do." 09:11-09:20, 19:07-20:09
