## Summary

A DeepMind research scientist's overview of text diffusion as an alternative to autoregressive (next-token) text generation. Like image/video diffusion, it corrupts clean token sequences during training and learns to denoise; at inference it initializes the whole output to noise and iteratively refines it over a few denoising passes, using bidirectional attention so every token can see the whole canvas. The talk grounds this in DeepMind's Gemini Diffusion research preview (released ~1 year earlier, ~100k users, branched from Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite, comparable quality at much better latency) and works through four advantages — low latency, bidirectional self-correction, adaptive/dynamic computation, and in-place editing — plus the one disadvantage that keeps it out of frontier production models today (low big-batch throughput / high cost to serve). The why-it's-fast section is a clean memory-bound-inference explanation; the self-correction section is a striking head-to-head where a small diffusion model beats much larger autoregressive models by revising an early wrong answer.

## Notes

- Paradigm: same principle as image/video diffusion — take a clean token sequence, gradually corrupt it (discrete: replace tokens with random vocabulary tokens at many noise levels), train the network to correct the mistakes; at inference initialize the sequence to pure random tokens and iteratively refine over a few denoising steps, filling in information in the order the network prefers. It does the entire block together over multiple passes, not one token at a time, and gets to attend to future tokens. (00:14-04:00)
- Gemini Diffusion: a Gemini variant doing text diffusion instead of autoregressive next-token generation, released ~1 year before the talk as a research preview to ~100k people; the baseline it branched from was Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite, with "very similar quality across the board" (slight code advantage, slight disadvantage elsewhere) "at much better latencies." ("Keep posted for new developments... upcoming soon.") (02:06-03:04)
- Four advantages: (1) lower latency / faster inference (the biggest, best-known one), (2) bidirectional attention → self-correcting generation, (3) dynamic/adaptive computation, (4) in-place editing. (04:00-04:46)
- One disadvantage: lower throughput for large batches → expensive to serve at scale, so "no one's landing text diffusion into any of these big models primarily because of that disadvantage." Autoregressive models are slow per user but you can batch many queries through the GPU to keep cost down; diffusion does multiple forward passes on the same data, hits a compute threshold earlier, and is lower throughput overall even though lower latency per user. (He notes Claude "have some throughput concerns" and "people really care about throughput right now.") (05:00-06:13)
- Why faster (memory-bound hardware): a GPU/TPU is a tensor core (lots of FLOPs) plus HBM holding weights/activations/KV cache, joined by a tight bandwidth channel; chips are FLOP-rich and bandwidth-poor (bandwidth is expensive to add, FLOPs are cheap), so the more FLOPs per streamed byte the better. Serving autoregressive models is memory-bound: each token streams the entire network + KV cache to produce one token. Diffusion generating ~256 tokens in ~24 passes does ~10x fewer memory transfers → up to ~10x faster if truly memory-bound. (06:13-08:00)
- ~2,000 tokens/second consistently for Gemini Diffusion (genuine raw tokens in the browser, including prefill); longer generations are less prefill-dominated, so you can lean into long sequences of very fast tokens; a one-token output is prefill-dominated. (08:00-08:45)
- Bidirectional self-correction demo (answer should be 39): after 1 forward pass "answer = 60" (wrong), then it reasons; after 2 passes 60→49; after 3 passes it finishes the reasoning (36+3=39) and goes back to fix the original answer to 39 — it made a mistake twice but corrected once reasoning completed. Two much larger autoregressive models failed the same prompt: GPT-4o first said 40, reasoned, then corrected ("I made a mistake, it's 39, not 40"); Gemini 2.5 Flash said 42 and stuck to its guns, even writing "36 + 3 is 42" — it incorporated the early error into later reasoning. Framed as a structural flaw of autoregressive (causal, past-only) models that diffusion (which attends to the future it will emit) does not have; reasoning/"thinking" models patch it but only by "punting the problem into something else." (08:47-11:55)
- Dynamic/adaptive computation: more forward passes (a bigger denoising budget) → roughly monotonic quality gains across all (six internal coding) evals, because even a nearly-clean solution gets re-examined and fixed. The model can be trained to decide itself when it's finished: "first 100 digits of pi" = 100 tokens in only 4 steps (memorized), FizzBuzz code = 18 passes, "explain quantum mechanics in a paragraph" = 31 steps; harder evals (GPQA Diamond) take longer, easier ones (MBPP basic Python) finish fast — entirely determined by the model. In the 4 steps diffusion used for 100 tokens, an autoregressive model would emit only 4 tokens. (12:00-14:26)
- In-place editing: like image inpainting (cut a region, the model fills it from surrounding context), diffusion edits text in place rather than regenerating token-by-token — "There is a bug in this code, can you fix it?" makes the fix in the correct spot, "add documentation" inserts it, "add a middle paragraph" fills a paragraph consistent with the surrounding ones. The reason: it's not autoregressive, so it sees the whole canvas/image and denoises it. (14:26-16:09)
- Low-latency unlocks new applications, "not just the same thing faster": a fake Wikipedia generated on the fly (even the HTML), a Reddit clone with AI-generated comments and images (text via the diffusion model, images via DeepMind's then-SOTA image generator "Juno," pre-Nano-Banana, arriving a minute later), an operating system where every click generates the next screen, and a to-do app built in ~15 seconds by voice ("create a to-do app, add 10 random to-dos, allow a completed state, mark four completed, sort by name and state, convert to dark mode"). (16:09-20:05)
- Q&A specifics: same training data as autoregressive models (algorithms change a bit); models can be distilled. Bigger models tend to need fewer denoising steps for the same output, so serving cost diminishes even for the biggest models. Variable length is handled by fixing a window length (e.g. 512 / 1000 / 32) and denoising within it, then autoregressing across windows for unlimited text (block-wise autoregression); a length-prediction head is optional; within a window they "set it in stone and continue" rather than revisiting prior windows. Prefill is the same as autoregressive. It's discrete diffusion — always tokens in, tokens out, a discrete corruption-then-fill process; latent-space variants exist but most literature is discrete diffusion today. Use-case split: low latency means worse throughput, so the trade-off favors on-device applications (phone, robotics in the Alphabet ecosystem) where you serve batch-of-1 and aren't batching thousands of queries — quality is the same as frontier models, so pick the lowest-latency one. Reasoning tasks with verifiable rewards can use RL (change the algorithm). Diffusion can be combined with autoregressive text models. All demos ran with a denoising-step limit but finished earlier, so latency is predictable ahead of time. (20:05-27:46)
