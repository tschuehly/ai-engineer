## Summary

A twenty-minute talk by the person responsible for Snowflake's internal AI tooling for its sales organization (00:21). The subject is one system with a long operating history: an internal go-to-market assistant launched in September of the previous year, which has answered more than a million questions — roughly 40,000 a week — for about 6,000 go-to-market users, built on Snowflake's own no-code agent platform as customer zero (00:25-00:43, 19:04-19:15). The talk is deliberately non-architectural — he says he was told to include code and does not have any (01:04-01:13) — and is organized as five lessons drawn from running the thing, not from building it.

**Why the surface exists.** Snowflake is close to 10,000 people and "almost half of our workforce is sales" (01:28-01:38). The problem he describes is not model capability but data topology: first-party and third-party data locked in SaaS tools, reps using "15 different tools, not because they love the UI of those tools, because every tool has a different data point, and then they end up stitching all of that together in spreadsheets" (01:46-02:07). The load is real — reps with 1,000 assigned accounts who must track news, consumption, support tickets, and earnings results, "30 times, 40 times a day," which "there's not a single human on this planet that can stay on top of" (02:08-02:29). The offered value chain runs data democratization ("no more like 1,000 dashboards… no more access to analysts") and tool consolidation to productivity to business results — book coverage, win rates, deal cycles, incremental revenue (02:33-03:09). None of the business results are measured anywhere in the talk.

**Trust and the first five questions.** The catch is non-determinism, and the failure principle is stated flatly: "user trust is earned extremely hard and is lost overnight" (03:16-03:36). The mechanism is specific to a free-form chat box: "people will come in and they will ask any question they can think of. If they like what they see in the first five questions, they come back. If they don't like what they see, it's 10 times more effort for you to win them back, if you can ever win them back" (03:38-03:57). The team's slogan is "quality is P minus one" (03:58-04:03), and the operating rule that follows is quality over coverage: "we don't want to try to answer 100 questions and get them 70% right. We want to answer 50 questions, but get them 95% right" (04:42-05:05). He is explicit that this is not a preference — "if you go for the coverage, you are going to shoot yourself in the foot" (16:43-16:47).

**The 150 questions.** The method that produced the rule is the most transferable artifact in the talk. Arriving on a team that already had dashboards connected, a knowledge assistant, and "three lines of agent instructions," he wrote the eval set before touching the product: "before I even tried the agent, I opened a spreadsheet, I took the sales process, I wrote down 150 questions." Engineering objected that the data was not connected; the answer was "it doesn't matter. These are the questions your sellers are going to ask" (04:08-04:36). The first run scored 50% accuracy (04:36-04:41). Sixty percent of the system's data was added after launch (05:06-05:11), and the current system spans "15 semantic views, 85 tables, 3,000 columns," five to six MCP connections and close to 20 skills (05:14-05:31).

**Phased launch with a different question per phase.** Every product ships in three stages. The pilot proves accuracy and quality using "your top AI-native folks in the organization who are eager to work with you, give you feedback" and smooths the rough edges (05:49-06:11). The 10% beta — 600 people — asks a different question: whether a minimum viable product exists. The signal is request concentration, "where are the actually the concentrations happening? Because that means that if you don't get those things in, you don't truly have an MVP" (06:12-06:41), and the exit bar is retention: "we exited for example that at like more than 70% retention rate that the weekly active users were coming back" (06:41-06:58). Then GA (06:59-07:12).

**Activation, not technology.** GA produces the next problem, and it is the one he most wants engineers to hear: "this is also where a lot of these products fail… how do you drive change management?" Two weeks post-launch, management is frustrated at low numbers, and the graph he shows them is trial: "only 20% of your organization actually tried the product. I cannot do anything. This is not the product's fault if people are not even taking 5 minutes to try the product. If they try it and if they don't come back, okay, that's my problem. But if they don't try it, then we have another problem" (07:13-07:53). The remedy is a couple of months of unglamorous work at 60-70% of his own time: "sales meetings, giving demos, building dashboards, which teams adopted, shaming the managers whose team is actually doing good, getting sponsorship from sales leaders" (07:53-08:26). His counterfactual: without it "we would probably be doing half of where we are today" (08:32-08:41).

**The collapsing wow factor.** Four to six months out, the same users who stopped him in the corridor return with frustrations and comparisons to other AI products (08:52-09:28). "This is what I call the collapsing of the wow factor… that becomes a habit. You basically change their habit and it becomes standard for them. Now, you need to raise the bar again" (09:26-09:38). The roadmap ladder he uses to keep raising it has four rungs: talk to your data, replacing dashboards and analyst queues (09:38-09:53); automate my workflows, once MCP connections exist — sellers monitor their inbox and Slack channels for customer questions, have the agent draft responses into Gmail for review, and automate outreach, so "I became an orchestrator" (09:54-10:26); team-built tooling, where go-to-market teams who were "always in the backlog of someone… or trying to get a SaaS budget" build their own skills, dashboards, applications, automations, and alerts (10:27-11:01); and hyper-personalization with living context of customers and contacts (11:02-11:14). The warning attached: "if you just do the first stage, and if you just wait there, you will get disrupted in a month or two… right now the switch is very easy. They're going to just switch overnight" (11:14-11:35), and "every time people are happy, you should be paranoid" (17:22-17:28).

**Stop shopping for the perfect architecture.** He describes enterprises that "are still trying to purchase that perfect architecture… testing different frameworks… but the thing that they don't do is they don't build, and then they don't launch, and they don't learn" (11:45-12:11). The counterexample is his own launch state: "a nine-page long agent instructions… a couple of Cortex Analyst tools, semantic views… a Cortex Search service for our unstructured data. And we were managing the agent instructions versions out of a Google Doc. That's how we launched it. To 6,000 people" (12:11-12:34). Everything after was added under pressure, in order: CI/CD; eval infrastructure with unit tests and routing tests; a skill library once business processes and workflows no longer fit in the agent instructions; orchestration instructions once MCPs arrived; progressive disclosure once the agent-instruction limits were hit; then user memory, task scheduling, and a Slack interface beyond the chat screen (12:35-13:16). The comparison to the original plan is the punchline — against the initial PRD and architecture diagram, 80% of the current architecture does not match (13:17-13:25) — and the budgeting consequence is a standing split: "maybe 60-70% of the work we are doing is adding new features, improving quality… But 30-40% of the work is that we are constantly re-architecting with the new technology" (13:26-13:40).

**Logs as the compounding loop.** The closing recommendation is to invest in logs, because "they create the feedback loop" (14:11-14:17). LLMs classify the log — 1.2 million questions, 40,000 a week — into a topic taxonomy with categories, subcategories, and example questions, and doing that at scale "without breaking the bank" is treated as the interesting engineering (14:17-15:00). Three uses follow. Feature gaps and quality problems become visible in real time, with two named frustration signals: users "swearing at the agent or repeating their question" (15:00-15:20). Sales enablement stops needing interviews — where the team "would need to interview maybe 100 sellers a week to understand… where there's gaps in terms of knowledge documents, battle cards," the same reading takes "a minute or two," after which connecting Confluence, Jira, and Slack and ingesting PRDs generates battle cards and enablement documents that are fed back into the agent (15:20-15:52). And overlapping teams targeting the same accounts can be detected and introduced: "they don't know about each other. We do. We are now able to ping them… do matchmaking" (15:58-16:11). His shape claim for the whole program: "the first features are difficult to get out. The next ones are easy. And then once you start tapping into your logs, this hockey stick exponential thing actually starts happening" (16:15-16:32).

**Q&A: one governed store underneath.** Asked what Snowflake Intelligence is relative to what he built, he says it was renamed Snowflake Co-work at the company's summit and is "our no-code agent platform… available for our business users," with Cortex Analyst and Cortex Search available out of the box (19:04-19:26). The strategic choice underneath is data consolidation: "it is important that we bring all our data together. And we do that in Snowflake. We bring all the first-party, the third-party data, all the Salesforce data, everything, the call transcripts… And then these agents then can basically inherit a lot of the role-based access controls. And I literally can deploy these agents without writing a single line of code" — with the chat UI out of the box, and platform-level curation and guardrails on which data sources agents may reach (19:26-20:19).

## Provenance and Limits

- This is a vendor describing its own product used on itself. The assistant is built on Snowflake Cortex/Co-work, the data lives in Snowflake, and the speaker names his team as "customer zero." Every architectural affordance he praises — inherited RBAC, no-code deployment, a chat UI out of the box — is a feature of the platform his employer sells.
- Usage is measured; value is not. More than a million questions, ~40,000 a week, ~6,000 users, and >70% weekly retention at beta exit are all reported. The business results the talk opens with — book coverage, win rates, deal cycle length, incremental revenue — carry no figures at all, and no before/after comparison is offered for any of them.
- The quality-over-coverage numbers are illustrative, not observed. "50 questions at 95%" versus "100 at 70%" is stated as a target shape; the only measured accuracy in the talk is the 50% first run against the 150-question set. No current accuracy figure is given, and the evaluation method behind "accuracy" is never defined beyond the spreadsheet.
- "First five questions" and "10 times more effort to win them back" are asserted from experience, not from a measured churn or reactivation study. The same applies to "we would probably be doing half of where we are today" without the change-management push, which is a counterfactual with no control.
- The 20% trial figure is a single point from two weeks after GA in one organization; no curve, cohort, or eventual trial ceiling is given.
- "80% of it doesn't match" the original PRD, and the 30-40% re-architecture share of sprint capacity, are recollections rather than a measured breakdown of committed work.
- The log-classification pipeline is described by output (categories, subcategories, example questions) and not by method: no model, prompt, sampling rate, label taxonomy, cost figure, or classification accuracy is given, despite "without breaking the bank" being named as the hard part.
- The organization is unusually favorable to the pattern: a data-platform company whose employees are technically fluent, whose data was already going to land in its own warehouse, and whose sales org can be reached through executive sponsorship. The change-management effort described is what it took *there*.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "we do a face launch" is a phased launch (05:46); "we do 10% better, right? With 600 people" is a 10% beta (06:14-06:17); "the collapsing law factor" is the wow factor (17:14); "rack-based like knowledge assistants" is RAG-based (18:21); "Cortex sense" (19:22) is uncertain and is not repeated elsewhere, so no product name is asserted for it. At 13:22-13:25 the captions read "80% of it It match" immediately after describing everything added post-launch and immediately before the re-architecture split; read in context this is "80% of it doesn't match," and it is recorded here as such. The product name heard as "Snowflake Co-work" is transcribed as spoken.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Choose Quality Over Coverage Because the First Five Answers Decide Adoption](../concepts/choose-quality-over-coverage-because-the-first-five-answers-decide-adoption.md) - the trust economics of a free-form chat box, and the answer-fewer-questions-better rule that follows from them.
- [Write the Question Set From the Business Process Before the Data Is Connected](../concepts/write-the-question-set-from-the-business-process-before-the-data-is-connected.md) - 150 questions taken from the sales process, deliberately including ones the system could not answer, scoring 50% on the first run.
- [Gate Each Rollout Phase on a Different Question](../concepts/gate-each-rollout-phase-on-a-different-question.md) - pilot proves accuracy, 10% beta proves the MVP through request concentration and a >70% retention bar, then GA.
- [Separate the Did-Not-Try Problem From the Did-Not-Return Problem](../concepts/separate-the-did-not-try-problem-from-the-did-not-return-problem.md) - the adoption funnel splits into trial and return, and only one of the two is a product defect.
- [The Wow Factor Collapses Into a Baseline Within Months](../concepts/the-wow-factor-collapses-into-a-baseline-within-months.md) - a delivered capability becomes a habit and then an expectation, so a static internal product is on a decay curve.
- [Stage the Internal Agent Roadmap From Answers to Automation to Team-Built Tooling](../concepts/stage-the-internal-agent-roadmap-from-answers-to-automation-to-team-built-tooling.md) - the four-rung ladder that keeps a data assistant ahead of its own baseline.
- [Budget a Third of Sprint Capacity for Re-Architecture](../concepts/budget-a-third-of-sprint-capacity-for-re-architecture.md) - launching on a Google Doc, the ordered chain of components added under pressure, and the standing 30-40% split.
- [Classify the Assistant Question Log to Find Feature and Content Gaps](../concepts/classify-the-assistant-question-log-to-find-feature-and-content-gaps.md) - LLM classification over 40,000 questions a week, with repeated questions and profanity as free quality labels.
- [Land the Data in One Governed Store So Agents Inherit Access Control](../concepts/land-the-data-in-one-governed-store-so-agents-inherit-access-control.md) - consolidation makes authorization a property agents inherit rather than something each agent implements.
