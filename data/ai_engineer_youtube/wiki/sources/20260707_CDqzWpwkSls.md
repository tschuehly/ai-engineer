## Summary

Angel Ortmann Lee (software engineer on security for the Duolingo English Test) argues that "human-in-the-loop" quietly assumes the human produces genuine discernment, when in practice throughput and trust pressures make the human a rubber stamp. She grounds this in two studies: a Wharton study on "cognitive surrender" (humans adopt AI output with minimal scrutiny — accuracy +25 pts when the AI was right, −15 when wrong, and 80% accepted AI answers even when wrong), and Duolingo's own "When Machines Mislead" case study, where experienced proctors shown *fabricated* copy-typing cheating alerts confirmed them ~50% of the time (a coin flip) despite scoring >90% on accuracy calibration. The model was fine (1% false-positive rate) and the people were skilled — the interface was the problem. A single proctoring-guideline copy change (the AI signal is only a preliminary alert; you are the final decision-maker; you must find independent video evidence before upholding a flag) lifted accurate rejections by 21%. The durable thesis: the human-AI interaction loop is a cyclical data flywheel (model → interaction → human behavior → data → evals → model), you can't change how a human behaves but you *can* engineer the interaction to elicit the reasoning you need, and every interaction is already a training label — so design for discernment, match friction to stakes, and capture the diff, not just yes/no.

## Notes

- Human-in-the-loop is a framework where a human participates in the operation, supervision, or decision-making of an automated system to ensure accuracy, safety, or ethics; naively modeled as linear (model output → human → decision). (00:15-00:52)
- Cognitive surrender (Wharton): as AI is integrated into atomic daily tasks (GPS routes, search-engine AI summaries over CDC/WHO), trust rises and caution drops. In a study giving humans AI resources on reasoning exams, performance rose 25 pts when the AI was right and fell 15 pts when wrong, and 80% of participants accepted AI answers even when wrong — AI can supplant a human's thinking without the human noticing. (01:19-03:31)
- The Duolingo English Test (DET) is a high-stakes, fully online, remotely proctored English-proficiency exam trusted by 6,000 programs; security combines identity verification, a lockdown environment, AI-assisted cheating detection, and a final round of human proctors reviewing video footage plus AI results. (03:31-04:35)
- "Copy typing" detection: a custom, highly conservative model flags anomalous keystroke patterns (transcribing vs composing differ); 1% false-positive rate, uncommon flag. (04:35-05:22)
- The "When Machines Mislead" experiment: inject a *fabricated* copy-typing AI signal into clean (no-cheating) historical sessions, presented to proctors as normal workflow (no test-taker impact). Result: proctors accepted 50% of the fake signals — falsely accusing test-takers half the time — despite >90% accuracy-calibration scores. A coin-flip rate is strong evidence of automation bias: deferring judgment to AI without corroborating evidence. (05:33-07:24)
- Diagnosis: not the model (1% FP, negatively-predicted sessions) and not the people (skilled, experienced) — so target the interface. (07:24-08:22)
- The fix was a proctoring-guideline copy change emphasizing two things: (1) the AI signal is a preliminary alert; the proctor is the final decision-maker; (2) they must find independent evidence in the video footage before upholding a flag. This alone gave a 21% increase in rejection rates (from 50% up to 71% correctly determined okay), closely matching production — no model or UI change. (08:22-08:51, engineer takeaway 08:51-09:11)
- The interaction loop is cyclical, not linear: model output → interaction → human behavior → data for evals → next model. You can't change how a human behaves unless you *are* that human, but you can tweak the interaction to elicit different behavior, and that data is golden. (09:11-10:00)
- Virtuous vs vicious cycle: a frictionless interface lets humans rubber-stamp confident model calls, which get logged as truth, making the model more confident and the human more deferent (AI ends up "in the driving seat"). An interface that forces independent judgment surfaces real disagreements → honest true/false labels → model improvements targeting exactly where the model is wrong. (10:00-11:56)
- Example — split conflated questions: a headphone-detection flag asked one yes/no ("flag it here?") that hid two questions: (1) did the model correctly detect headphones? (2) is this a violation? A hearing-aid wearer is a *true* detection but *not* a violation; answering "no" to avoid a false accusation feeds a bad signal to the model. Splitting into two questions yields more and better-quality data without harming the model. (11:56-13:23)
- Example — writing tutor: an LLM asked to give writing feedback dumped 400 lines (praise, then feedback, then an unrequested full rewrite) — overwhelming, hard to tie feedback to specific text, unnatural. A Duolingo-style markup UI (green good / yellow awkward / red mistake, hover for concise actionable feedback, accept suggestions inline, every note tied to a text span) mimics human peer feedback and enables incremental improvement — same AI output, different surfacing. (13:23-15:41)
- Example — coding agents: two common failure patterns both produce a rubber stamp — a giant multi-file diff you approve then review on GitHub, or a ping-per-file "yes, yes, yes" stream. Both collect only binary accept/reject signals (skewed to accept) on a code block. The better pattern is a coding agent that acts like a junior developer: plans, asks good questions, documents design decisions, gives a good spec, and breaks work into meaningful reviewable PRs — reviewable, steerable, and it captures rich structured data (bad assumptions, tradeoffs made, stylistic preferences) about parts of the development cycle. (15:41-18:50)
- Design principles: (1) Engineer the reasoning — decide what reasoning pattern you want from the human and make the interface challenge it; reframe the human as *investigator, not validator*; surface assumptions early for sign-off; present tradeoffs and options with reasoning so the user stays in control; build friction exactly where stakes are high. (18:50-20:18)
- (2) Match friction to stakes — high-stakes review (visas, admissions) wants deliberately slow reviewers, so add well-structured review gates/speed bumps; low-oversight delightful chat should be frictionless and seamless. (20:18-21:31)
- (3) Every interaction is already a label — approved/accepted = you matched intent; modified/overridden = something was wrong, but only if you *capture the diff*. A human clicking "yes" then silently editing or erasing the output logs a false positive that pollutes the dataset; measure the diff. Follow-up questions and explanations are also signals (low trust or a wrong AI). (21:31-22:58)
- (4) Stop asking how to evaluate the model *after* building — proactively define what success means, what concrete metrics measure it, and what data you need, and let those decisions shape the interaction so you have hard evidence for the next step. (22:58-23:40)
- (5) Engineer the interaction concretely: structured inputs/outputs (forms, tables, markup UIs) instead of vibes/walls of text; highlight assumptions and ask if legit (saves tokens later); build in friction and review gates for deliberate thought; collect explicit feedback at the correct touch points with nuance, not just thumbs up/down. (23:40-25:41)
- Closing: sometimes the fix is not a better model or more oversight — it's engineering the interaction itself; every AI system trains its users, the question is whether you do it deliberately. (25:41-25:51)
