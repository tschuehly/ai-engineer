## Summary

Ahnaf Prio is a senior engineering manager at Best Buy whose team is "working together right now to figure out what does agentic commerce mean and how can we meet our customers where they're at" (00:03-00:25). The talk is an orientation map rather than a case study: it names the five protocols that have accumulated around agent shopping, says what layer each one occupies, demonstrates a working loop against a toy merchant, and closes on evals. His framing number is that "about 45% of all agent sessions that happen within major providers like chatgpt.com and Google Gemini are related to shopping" (01:43-01:56), with agentic shopping "considered to be a $7 billion industry and might go up to $65 billion industry by 2030" (04:11-04:17). None of the three figures is sourced.

The first useful move is the postmortem on the previous generation. A year ago the pattern was "take screenshots, read the DOM, navigate to the merchant site, fill forms for me, do loyalty," and it "kind of just didn't work as expected. It was really clunky and slow and brittle" (03:08-03:30). The part usually left out of that story is the merchant side: "if you are a merchant who's trying to sell stuff, any engineering department of that merchant will tell you an AI impersonating your browser is just firing up all the alarm bells. So, a lot of times, you will probably be even stuck on the payment flow" (03:30-03:49). Browser-driving commerce agents did not merely perform badly — they were actively defended against by the counterparty, and they died at the step that mattered.

The reason protocols exist at all is stated in one line that is easy to skip: "for some of you who are shopping on the other side as the customer, there is not much of a difference between adding an item to cart, adding a second quantity. But to us merchants, that's a second line item, buddy. That's not the same scale. So, if we don't talk about the nuances and the primitives of commerce and standardize it, things will just not work and will remain to be clunky" (05:00-05:26). The buyer's interface collapses a distinction the seller's systems treat as structurally different, and an agent protocol has to carry the seller's version.

The acronym map is the talk's core. MCP is "still the model context protocol, the way that the AI agent identifies the tool" — how a merchant advertises what it can do: show products, show a specific product's details, show loyalty (06:18-07:11). A2A "is how agents talk to each other," both for domain-specialized agents inside one merchant (payments, loyalty) and for the cross-organization case where "your customer agent and your merchant agent need to talk to each other" (07:11-08:03). ACP (OpenAI) and UCP (Google) are "the primitives" — the commerce-specific vocabulary of product data and checkout state. AP2 is "the agentic payment protocol scoped payment mandate that Google's open specification came out" with, as an extension of UCP (06:43-06:56, 10:14-10:20).

Product data does not flow the way most engineers guess. "Both ACP and UCP right now, so Gemini and ChatGPT, does not support that search catalog call. They want you to send that feed to them" (08:35-08:57). Prio gives a commercial reason and a technical one: "sponsored products, retail media related things, ranking," and then "the most important technological challenge — if you have M number of merchants and N number of products, now it has to call that many. While if you send the product feed ahead of time, we can index that and be ready to offload when you ask for something" (08:57-09:19). The cost lands on the merchant, twice: every provider's feed schema differs — he shows ACP, UCP, and Meta's side by side, "similar, but still different. Everyone has an opinion. They think their opinion is the best one, and that's what they're rolling with" (09:19-09:30) — and the feed must be kept fresh, which in his demo means "every couple of seconds, we try to get the catalog in sync for what is in inventory, what's not" (15:00-15:13).

Payments are deliberately conservative today. "None of them are supporting that more autonomous form of, you know, x402 or some other kind of payments. We're just not there yet. We're just not confident yet. We want more human in the loop, a merchant to be talking to a payment processor who will take the responsibility, or in this case, liability, to actually initiate the payments" (09:36-10:03). Concretely: "in ChatGPT, payments only happen through a shared payment token right now, and Gemini UCP, the payments are only being accepted through Google Pay" (10:03-10:11). AP2 is the part he is excited about because it is the one designed for the autonomous case: "can you tell me who authorized the agent, what exactly can it buy, and what's the max amount that should be able to haggle with, maybe, and then the revocation URL, and the user consent proof?" (10:20-10:40), and in the demo the issued token shows a max amount, a currency, a revocation path, and single-use semantics (14:37-14:52).

The demo runs a made-up merchant — Ginny, his orange tabby, recast as a bakery agent — on a Cerebras-hosted model at "3,000 tokens per second" with an inspector "just like Chrome DevTools" showing every call (10:41-11:20). The flow is legible: an A2A message from the customer agent to the merchant agent; the merchant agent returning a completed task; an MCP `product_search` tool call underneath it, chosen because "when I give her the intent that I want to find products, you should call the MCP tool call product search" (11:20-12:20). Then add-to-cart and checkout, which is where the state machine appears: "the checkout APIs will have state, and the three different states are not ready for payment, ready for payment, and then completed" (13:04-13:26). Selecting a payment method is the transition — "it's added to cart, but it's not ready for payment. I have to pick what I want to pay with… and this is where I issue the AP2 token… and then it went from ready for payment to complete" (13:40-14:07). He then reruns the identical sequence under ACP: "you can see the same checkout calls, just different schemas are being utilized, and the order goes through" (14:07-14:22).

The last section is the one he says he would keep. "Working with AI and conversational experiences without evals is playing whack-a-mole" (16:14-16:30), and the illustration is a story he explicitly does not vouch for: "I love Chipotle. I don't know if it's true or not, but I found it really funny… when Chipotle rolled out their agent, people were using it to ask programming questions. If you don't tell your agent to not allow for those kind of things, people will use it. This is hands-down one of the most creative way to get free AI usage when you don't want to pay for that cloud subscription" (17:10-17:40). The merchant-specific leaks he names are commercial rather than generic: "the discount code will be told, even sometimes more sensitive things like who else is checking out this product" (17:44-17:55) — and his own demo shows him trying and failing to talk Ginny into a discount code (12:43-13:04). Four eval classes follow: behavior evals; protocol compliance, "because when we're selling it to Gemini or chat.openai.com, you want to make sure that the feeds are actually conforming, or else they will not support it" (17:55-18:12); latency benchmarks, because "every second in retail on the shopping journey where you're actually not selling, there are chances that the other website's going to be faster, and people are just going to move away" (18:12-18:29); and LLM-as-quality-judge, where "you don't have to use something fancy. Talk to your product friends and figure out what's the best way to do it" (18:29-18:44).

He closes with a maturity read — "MCP is widely adopted, A2A is used, UCP ACP is out there," while "what's still forming is AP2 and actual usage of it, ACP versus UCP convergence, do we always have to do two different specs, identity consent standards, and multi-agent checkout delegation" (18:44-19:08) — and with the advice that even a merchant building only its own on-site agent should reuse the standardized primitives, "because they've been standardized across merchants. So, they have been well thought out, and also you could probably reuse them to sell externally as well" (15:33-16:07). The giveaways are a three-service starter template (customer agent, merchant agent, catalog sync), eval templates, and agent skills for each piece (19:08-20:10).

## Provenance and Limits

- Nothing about Best Buy's own agentic-commerce implementation is described. The talk is Best Buy-credentialed but not Best Buy-evidenced: no traffic, conversion, integration cost, incident, or timeline figure from the employer appears anywhere.
- All three market figures are unattributed. "About 45% of all agent sessions… are related to shopping" (01:43-01:56) has no named study, provider, period, or definition of "related to"; the $7B-to-$65B-by-2030 range (04:11-04:17) has no source. Prio himself hedges the first one only for personal bias ("maybe it's a little biased that I don't use it as much"), not for provenance.
- The Chipotle anecdote is explicitly unverified by the speaker: "I don't know if it's true or not, but I found it really funny" (17:10-17:17). It is used to motivate scope evals, and it should be carried as an illustrative hypothetical, not as a reported incident.
- The demo is a single made-up merchant with a handful of baked goods, running locally against the speaker's own merchant agent. It demonstrates protocol *shape* — call sequence, state transitions, schema differences — and nothing about behavior at catalog scale, under concurrency, or against a real provider's conformance checks.
- Latency is recommended as an eval axis but never measured. The Cerebras "3,000 tokens per second" figure (11:09-11:14) is a demo configuration, not a result, and no end-to-end checkout latency is reported for any path.
- The eval section names four classes and no method. There is no dataset construction, no threshold, no pass criterion, no judge-calibration step, and no discussion of how a protocol-compliance eval is run against a spec that is still moving.
- Every protocol claim is a snapshot dated to the talk. "Both ACP and UCP right now… does not support that search catalog call" (08:35-08:57), "in ChatGPT, payments only happen through a shared payment token right now," and "Gemini UCP, the payments are only being accepted through Google Pay" (10:03-10:11) are all explicitly present-tense and should be re-checked before being relied on.
- The feed-versus-search argument is given from the aggregator's side only. The M×N scaling claim is asserted rather than worked through (it says nothing about caching, partial feeds, or hybrid designs), and the merchant-side costs the design creates — N different schemas to maintain, continuous sync, and freshness risk between syncs — are shown in the demo but never priced.
- Sponsored products, retail media, and ranking are named as reasons the aggregators prefer feeds (08:57-09:12), and then dropped. Who ranks, on what signal, and whether merchants can pay for position — the central commercial question of the design — is not addressed.
- Liability is mentioned once, as the reason a payment processor sits in the loop (09:58-10:03). What happens when the agent buys the wrong item, the wrong quantity, or at a stale price is not discussed, despite the second-line-item example that opens the talk being exactly such a case.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "Anup Priyo" and "Priu" → Ahnaf Prio (00:03, 20:16, per the video title and description); "Genie" and "Jenny"/"jenny.com" → Ginny, the speaker's cat and demo merchant agent (per the description); "e-vows," "e-vow," and "emails" → evals (17:39, 17:44, 19:36); "chat.gbt.com" → chatgpt.com (01:44); "Cloud Chrome extension" → Claude Chrome extension (02:47); "Agent tech shopping" → agentic shopping (03:57); "user concept proof" → user consent proof (10:39); "scope demands" → scoped mandates (10:11); "as by" → at Best Buy (16:24); "X402" → x402 (09:44). Left unresolved: the fragment at 07:26-07:36 where the speaker loses his place listing domain agents, and "identity concept standards" at 19:04, which is either "identity consent standards" or "identity and consent standards" and is recorded here as the former.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Map the Agentic Commerce Protocol Stack by Layer](../concepts/map-the-agentic-commerce-protocol-stack-by-layer.md) - MCP, A2A, ACP, UCP, and AP2 occupy five different layers and are not competing choices, except for ACP and UCP which are.
- [Agent Protocols Must Encode the Distinctions the User Interface Collapses](../concepts/agent-protocols-must-encode-the-distinctions-the-ui-collapses.md) - a second unit is one gesture to the buyer and a second line item to the merchant, and the protocol has to carry the seller's version.
- [Push a Product Feed, Because Per-Merchant Catalog Search Does Not Scale](../concepts/push-a-product-feed-because-catalog-search-does-not-scale.md) - both ACP and UCP require merchants to send a feed rather than answer search calls, for an M×N reason and a retail-media reason.
- [Model Agentic Checkout as an Explicit Session State Machine](../concepts/model-agentic-checkout-as-an-explicit-session-state-machine.md) - not-ready-for-payment, ready-for-payment, completed, with payment-method selection as the transition, and the same sequence under both competing specs.
- [Eval an Agent Surface for Protocol Compliance, Not Just Behavior](../concepts/eval-agent-surfaces-for-protocol-compliance-not-just-behavior.md) - when a platform is your distribution channel, schema conformance is a continuous test rather than a one-time integration.
- [Public Agent Surfaces Get Repurposed as Free General-Purpose Compute](../concepts/public-agent-surfaces-get-repurposed-as-free-general-compute.md) - an unscoped merchant agent answers programming questions, hands out discount codes, and leaks other customers' activity.
