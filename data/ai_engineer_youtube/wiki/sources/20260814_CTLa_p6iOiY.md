## Summary

D'Oro presents work done at Meta Superintelligence Labs (paper: arXiv 2605.08261) attacking computer-use agent evaluation from two sides. The attack: a **replay agent** — record one successful trajectory per task from a frontier model, compile the action sequences into a script under a megabyte, and replay them blindly without ever looking at the screen — matches or beats the model it was copied from on standard deterministic benchmarks, and the paper proves that `pass@k` on a deterministic environment *is* the success rate of that replay agent, so a widely used metric is a formal measure of the exploit. The repair has two halves. Environments get the **PRISM** principles (privileged verification, realism, integrity-checked configurations, sandboxed execution, multifactorial variation), instantiated in **DIGIWORLD**: 15 sandboxed Android apps, 387 verified scenarios, 3.2 million verified configurations produced by a compiler that enumerates combinations of task template, verifier, mock data, base data case, and base UI state and rejects the broken ones — because "a lot of software is not the same as an effective environment." Metrics get honest uncertainty: naive rollouts on a single base case produce nominal 95% confidence intervals whose real coverage is ~17-20%, and the priced consequence of that overconfidence is a wrong deploy decision between two models 4% apart costing hundreds of thousands of dollars a month at a million tasks.

## Notes

### The replay agent (00:36-02:05)

- Construction: run a frontier model on a benchmark you like; for every task collect one successful trace ("a recorded tape") of actions — tapping, typing, scrolling; do this for all tasks; "compile this into a replay agent that just, when the task arrives, replays that sequence of actions blindly."
- Size: "if you do this for common benchmarks with hundreds of tasks, this is going to be a script that is less than a megabyte."
- Legitimacy is the point of the argument: "this is a completely valid type of agent that you can evaluate on the benchmark" — nothing in the benchmark's rules excludes it.
- Result: evaluated on standard benchmarks (OSWorld, and a mobile benchmark rendered in the captions as "mobile word"), "the success rate of this agent compared to the frontier model from which the agent was extracted is actually the same or even better." (01:34-01:50)
- His own framing of the anomaly: "this is a kind of weird but maybe trivial phenomenon, but I would argue that we shouldn't accept this kind of blind scripts beating the frontier models." (01:52-02:02)
- Cause: "the determinism of most existing benchmarks… if the benchmark is static, is deterministic, then it is somehow gameable by this sort of strategy." (02:05-02:19)

### pass@k as the metrification of the exploit (02:22-03:12)

- Definition restated: pass@k is "the probability of at least one of k attempts succeeding."
- The claim: "if you look into the details of how this metric works on a deterministic environment, you will see that it is literally — and we prove it formally in the paper — evaluating the success rate of the replay agent that I've shown to you."
- The consequence he draws: "if that replay agent felt weird to you, also pass@k on computer-use tasks should somehow feel weird to you… pass@k is sort of a metrification of that exploit of the replay agent."
- Generalization: these are two specific problems pointing at two general classes — environments that "don't have exploitable structure" and metrics that "are not based on fragile statistics." (03:12-03:43)

### PRISM principles for environment design (03:47-05:31)

- Derived by asking what would stop a replay agent. First answer: make the benchmark **multifactorial** — generate variation, i.e. stochasticity, by varying "data or appearance or simply the initial state."
- Second: because variation can break things, the environment needs "a system for checking and verifying that everything is working as intended for *every* combination."
- Third and fourth, "the usual things": sandboxed execution, and support for verifiers — "so privileged information."
- Fifth: realism — "if it's a reproduction of a real system, you want that reproduction to be faithful so that the score that you get out is a good one."
- Named: "you can remember these sort of principles as the PRISM principles for environment design." The video description expands the acronym as privileged verification, realism, integrity-checked configurations, sandboxed execution, and multifactorial variation across data, theme, and starting screen.
- Coverage claim about the field: "if you look at existing benchmarks, some of them do some things in a good way, some others do other things in a good way, but there is no unified benchmark that sort of matches all of these boxes." (05:38-05:53)

### DIGIWORLD (05:31-07:25)

- Built as a set of mobile apps for Android devices: 15 apps spanning different domains, 387 verified scenarios, 3.2 million configurations — "but the important thing is that they are verified."
- Variation axes, per task: the *instance* ("what is the exact amount of money that you're sending"), the *data profile* ("which kind of contacts or emails you have in the data"), the *theme*, and the *starting screen* ("do you start from the login page or do you start from another valid page?").
- Combinatorics: "even if you start from a relatively low number of base cases for each one of these variables, you end up having many many combinations… you can get to millions, and if you scale this up, you can get to easily billions."
- Why coding agents are relevant: the axes "can be manipulated by coding agents because in the end they are like forms of software" — a coding agent can generate different instances, themes, and such.

### The compiler, and why generation is not the hard part (07:25-09:21)

- The tempting shortcut, named and rejected: "you just generate as much software as you can with a coding agent and then you have a diverse environment. But it's a little bit trickier than that… coding agents can generate a lot of software, but a lot of software is not the same as an effective, cool environment."
- The strategy: "generate many configs, all the combinations of the different factors… and then have a system that rejects the broken ones, the ones that are not valid, and just keeps the valid configs."
- Implementation "looks a little bit like a compiler": inputs are a **parameterized task template** ("send a certain amount to a certain recipient"), a **verifier** corresponding to that template, and **mock data** the task needs; the compiler takes those plus a **base case of data** and a **base case of UI state** and emits a valid configuration.
- Where the difficulty actually sits: "you can build systems like this in which the main craft is good software engineering, to make sure that actually the combinations that you have are both diverse and valid." (09:09-09:20)

### Replay stops working; frontier models look fragile (09:21-10:56)

- Re-running the same attack on the PRISM-compliant benchmark: "the replay agent doesn't get a lot of performance. It gets a little bit of performance that is probably what you want — sometimes some tasks maybe are repeatable by nature, but on average you shouldn't expect a replay agent to have good performance." Note that the target is a low but non-zero replay score, not zero.
- The second payoff of multifactorial configs is measurement: "you can also do other things like measuring the robustness of frontier models over different axes of variation."
- The finding: "in the worst case, frontier models are pretty bad actually at being robust to these variations." The expectation being violated is stated explicitly — "if you have a model that seems to be good at a given task, you would expect that if you just vary which screen the task is starting from, or what is the theme of the app, the model should pretty much have the same performance. But this is actually not the case for most frontier models."
- Use: "if you have infrastructure like this, you can actually measure that and tailor your expectation about this kind of robustness."

### Two sources of variance (11:05-12:31)

- Framed as equally important and not the same: variance from **actions** ("you run your model multiple times; in many cases you can have even quite different trajectories out of it because the action at each step would be different") and variance from **the environment**, which only exists once the benchmark has multiple configurations.
- Why the second matters: "that is what we are going to find in the real world," so "we need a methodology that captures both of these types of variation."
- The paper's contribution is a methodology that "can accurately capture these two types of variation taking into account the *structure* of the benchmark."

### Coverage, and the price of an overconfident interval (12:26-14:56)

- Coverage defined operationally: "you would expect that a 95% confidence interval would say that 95% of the time the performance of the model is in that range."
- The measured failure: "if you only use rollouts, so you only use the base case — and what people would use normally, actually, in realistic cases — you have something like 17% or 20% coverage… only 20% of the time you guess the right performance of the agent." The video description states the same figure as "around 20% rather than 95%."
- The fix: "if you take into account the hierarchy and you use the proper way of computing confidence intervals, you can get to the full confidence interval and be 90-95% accurate."
- The decision it corrupts: choosing between model A and model B for deployment, where "the confidence intervals seem really really small, and so you make a decision based on those small confidence intervals, but they are actually overconfident" — the true performances (the orange bars on his slide) fall outside.
- Priced: one million tasks, "a 4% mismatch in performance for real in the models," and each mistake costing "$20 — uh, like $12 on average" (he corrects himself downward mid-sentence) "can cost you like hundreds of thousands of dollars in a single month… just for a confidence interval being overconfident."
- The useful output of an honest interval is a refusal, not a number: "the method would tell you 'I'm not confident enough to make an informed decision,' and so you can choose to spend more money, to spend more time on evaluating models, and avoid the costly mistake." (14:24-14:47)

### Closing checklist and the disposition argument (14:50-17:10)

- Recap split into environment properties (the PRISM principles) and metric properties (avoid replayability; accurate confidence intervals that "respect the benchmark structure" and avoid "underestimating the uncertainty overall").
- Which principle is actually missing in practice: "some of these things are rather common, but some things like *varying initial state across runs* — they are pretty rare across existing benchmarks, but they are very important, and so I would suggest you try to incorporate these into your evals." (15:16-15:30)
- The two excuses he has heard, quoted verbatim: "this benchmark can be gamed but everybody's still using it," and "there is no error bar but I don't see people using them." (15:57-16:06)
- The answer: "a non-rigorous benchmark is misleading" — for the field, because "everybody could be maximizing a score on a benchmark that maybe is not capturing what we care about," but "especially it can be misleading for your own decisions… if you are deluding yourself on thinking that a score is confidently telling that your model is good, actually you are going to pay for those mistakes." (16:08-16:46)
- Positioning: he has started Programma Labs, "building the best infrastructure for [computer-use agent] verification," and is hiring. (16:51-17:06)
