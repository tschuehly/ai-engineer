## Summary

Shlok Khemani spent a year reverse-engineering how consumer AI products implement memory — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Poke — and has since worked with several teams on their own memory designs. He scopes "memory" narrowly to personalization for consumer applications, then walks the three-year evolution: ChatGPT's February 2024 v1 was a user-managed list of extracted facts, visible and deletable in settings, which put the burden of memory management on the user and went stale the moment a fact stopped being true. April 2025's v2 replaced it with a **running profile** — every few days a background pass reads new conversations, extracts what it thinks matters, and rewrites a profile that is then prepended to every new conversation. His own profile is ~4,000 tokens across 16 sections, written as dense keyword clues rather than prose, on the bet that frontier models infer a lot from a little. Claude launched the opposite architecture in August 2025: no profile at all, just two tools for searching past conversations by keyword/topic and by time period, with every conversation starting cold and retrieval happening on demand. On the day his "Claude's memory architecture is the opposite of ChatGPT's" post hit the Hacker News front page, Claude shipped v2 and added a running profile — visible in settings, ~1,000 tokens, complete sentences, refreshed every 24 hours, user-editable with each edit triggering a resynthesis. ChatGPT then added a conversation-search tool and, in June, partial profile visibility. After three years of independent evolution the two converged on the same shape: a running profile plus tools over past conversations.

The talk's three lessons are architectural rather than product-historical. First, **there is no single way to do memory** — the industry-default RAG recipe (chunk, embed, vector store, semantic search) is not what either flagship does; Gemini attaches creation and update timestamps to each memory; Claude Code, OpenClaw, and Hermes use Markdown files, heartbeats, knowledge bases, and skills. The implication he draws is commercial: memory cannot be outsourced, it evolves alongside the product, and every top consumer product he surveyed builds it in-house. Second, **memory is a function of compute**: a profile costs to maintain (update frequency × compute per update) and costs again on every conversation it enters (serving), and the two flagship designs sit at opposite corners of that one budget — ChatGPT pays more to serve 4,000 tokens but updates only every few days, Claude serves 1,000 tokens but updates daily. Absent GPU constraints you would update after every conversation and store 400,000 tokens; the constraint is what makes the design a choice. Third, **continual learning is already here**, running outside the weights: the profile shapes conversations, conversations feed the next synthesis, and the loop repeats. Whether it moves into per-user weights is open, and his framing of the blocker is economic — enterprise training amortizes across employees and customers, individual personalization does not.

He closes on what he calls a rant: the best memory architecture in the world is still capped by how much context it can gather about you. His profile says he travelled to Turkey in 2025; he went to Thailand, and the overlapping dates came from conversations where he was deciding between the two. The decision itself was made in person with his partner, which ChatGPT could not hear — understandable. What bothers him is that ChatGPT does not notice the contradiction and is not curious about filling gaps, even though the evidence that settles it (flight and hotel bookings) sits in an inbox the product is already connected to but does not reason over or synthesize into the profile. "It's not a technology problem. It's a product problem." The second half of the same complaint is fragmentation: chatbots, assistants, vertical applications, agents, and hardware devices each build a private memory of him, share nothing, and must each be updated by hand when his life changes, while email, calendar, and photos go unread. "None of this feels like 2026… when will personal AI feel like personal AI?" His disposition is still optimistic: memory for AI is a three-year-old field and there is much left to build.

## Notes

- Scope statement: "memory is a very overloaded term now… when I talk about memory today, it is going to be in the context of personalization, especially for consumer AI applications" (00:28-00:43). Credentials: a year reverse-engineering how ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Poke implement memory, plus work with multiple teams on their memory designs (00:45-01:05). All of it is outside-in reading, not documented vendor internals.
- 2023 baseline: context held within a thread, nothing carried across threads; early adopters simply re-pasted context by hand, and the gap only became a problem as non-technical users arrived for learning, cooking, and companionship (01:28-02:20).
- ChatGPT memory v1, February 2024: say "remember that I'm vegetarian," the model extracts a fact, stores it in a list, and the list is added to the context window of every conversation; visible and deletable in settings (02:20-03:00).
- Two v1 flaws, stated as fundamental: seeing each memory get created made the user feel responsible for curating memory while trying to have a conversation — "the burden of memory management fell to the user" — and facts true at write time do not stay true, illustrated by a memory that he is going to Bengaluru still entering his context window long after (03:00-03:53).
- ChatGPT memory v2, April 2025: "user knowledge memories," which he calls a running profile. Every few days the system reads new conversations, extracts what it thinks is important, and updates a maintained profile that is then prepended to every new conversation (03:56-04:58). He notes others call this update pass "dreaming" and points to Lance Martin's talk at the same event (04:30-04:47).
- Profile shape, from his own: extremely dense entries, "keywords almost like clues," which works "because LLMs, especially the frontier models today, are so good at inferring context from limited information"; 16 sections including personal life and things he is working on; almost 4,000 tokens total; updates happen asynchronously in the background (04:58-05:47).
- Staleness survives v2: a memory about places he travelled in 2025 lists Thailand and Turkey with overlapping dates, because its source was conversations deciding between the two. He went to Thailand and has never been to Turkey (05:56-06:29).
- Visibility regression in v2: settings would show v1 memories but not the raw profile. He read his own by jailbreaking — "you might have to attempt a few times, try different thinking modes, but prod enough and you shall receive" (06:29-06:56).
- Claude memory v1, August 2025: no user profile and no fact list. The model gets two tools — search past conversations by keyword or topic, and search by time period ("what did we discuss last week," "at the start of November of 2025"). Every conversation starts fresh with no user context and the model retrieves on demand when it decides it needs to (06:59-08:03). He had expected two such similar chat products to converge on similar memory designs; they had not.
- His September 11 (2025) post arguing Claude's memory architecture is the opposite of ChatGPT's hit the Hacker News front page, and Claude shipped v2 the same day (08:03-08:20).
- Claude v2 profile, contrasted point by point with ChatGPT's: raw profile visible in settings; ~1,000 tokens against 4,000; complete sentences rather than dense keywords; updates every 24 hours rather than every few days; the user can request an explicit edit, which triggers a resynthesis of the profile, with an interface for managing prior edits and deleting what no longer holds. Unchanged since September 2025 (08:20-09:16).
- Two ChatGPT changes during 2026: a tool to search past conversations, so the model can retrieve summarized context from its own queries; and, at the start of June, partial profile visibility — what you see is "an LLM generated summary of your profile, which is weird because your profile is already an LLM generated summary of your conversations." Explicit edit requests were added and the v1 fact list was deprecated (09:16-10:09).
- Convergence: after three years of independent evolution both products have a running profile that is visible and editable (with caveats), plus tools to look over past conversations (10:09-10:26).
- Lesson one, no single way to do memory: the assumption "including me" was that RAG — chunk conversations, embed, vector store, semantic search on the query — was the answer, and "neither ChatGPT nor Claude really do this." Gemini has a running profile where each memory carries detailed timing logs (created, last updated). Agents such as Claude Code, OpenClaw, and Hermes have completely different systems again: Markdown files, heartbeat, knowledge bases, skills (10:32-11:31).
- The commercial implication he draws: "memory cannot be outsourced. If you're a serious team, you do not outsource memory. It is something that you build alongside your product. Your memory system evolves with your product and it cannot be thought of as an afterthought." Evidence offered is a survey slide of top consumer products across categories: each has some form of memory, none outsource it, all build in-house (11:31-12:08).
- Lesson two, memory is a function of compute. Two cost types: maintenance, which "depends on how frequently you update it and how much compute you apply to each update"; and serving, because the profile enters the context window of every conversation, so "the longer the profile the more it costs to serve" (12:08-12:37).
- Unconstrained thought experiment: update every hour or after every conversation, spend a large model with subagents on each update, and store 400,000 tokens instead of 4,000. "Unfortunately we live in a GPU constrained world and trade-offs have to be made" (12:37-13:14). The two flagships take opposite corners: ChatGPT 4,000 tokens updated every few days — higher serving cost, lower update cost; Claude 1,000 tokens updated every 24 hours — the exact opposite trade (13:14-13:37).
- Lesson three, continual learning is already here, prefaced with "I'm not an expert here." The loop: the profile starts as what the model knows about you, is applied to every conversation, each conversation brings new information, the update pass synthesizes it back into the profile, and the profile then shapes the next conversations — "and what you have is a continual learning process," happening outside the weights (13:37-14:24).
- Why it may not move into weights soon: training is expensive, and "continuous learning does make sense at an enterprise level because the costs of these models are amortized across different employees, different customers, but that's not the case at an individual level" (14:32-14:54). Open questions he names: will each of us get our own self-learning model; what data kicks the process off and how is it generated; who pays for it (14:54-15:12). He recommends an essay titled "Guardian Angels" for the one-model-per-person future (15:12-15:32).
- The rant, stated as a cap: "You could have the best memory architecture in the world. You could pour infinite amounts of compute into it. You could have continuous learning working at an individual level… yet your memory system is capped by how much context it can gather about you" (15:32-16:03).
- The worked example of that cap: the decision to go to Thailand was made in an in-person conversation with his partner, which ChatGPT could not hear — "I think that's okay, it's understandable." But traces existed in his email as flight and hotel bookings, and "even if ChatGPT is connected to my email, it doesn't reason over my email and it doesn't update my profile over my email," so the conflict stood (16:03-16:50).
- The incuriosity complaint, which he calls the part that actually bothers him: "ChatGPT today doesn't realize that there is a conflict. It's not curious about trying to fill in gaps in the information it knows about me… it's not a technology problem. It's a product problem. There is no fundamental reason from an LLM level that these things can't be solved" (16:50-17:34).
- Fragmentation, the second half of the context problem: his personal stack is chatbots, assistants, vertical-specific applications, agents, and hardware devices; "each of these products is trying to build its own memory of me. None of these memories are shared with each other. So I have to rebuild context within every single product from scratch. Every time something in my life changes, I have to individually update all of them." Rich existing sources — email, calendar, photos — go unreasoned-over by all of them. "None of this feels like 2026… when will personal AI feel like personal AI?" (17:34-18:33).
- Closing disposition: memory for AI is "just a three-year-old field," foundational to how humans interact with AI, "and there's so much left to build" (18:33-19:05).
