## Summary

A twenty-six-minute fireside interview with Mike Krieger — Instagram co-founder, Anthropic's chief product officer for two years, and now an individual contributor in Anthropic Labs. The role change is the frame for everything else: he moved because "I kept seeing people build with the models and the FOMO just kept increasing," and he notes the pattern generalizing — "several people that were CTOs at other places are like now joining as ICs at Anthropic and other places" (01:02-01:41). The interview covers five separable areas: how large an ask should be, how a large port actually got done, how review works when nobody reads every line, how Labs is organized, and where startups still win against the labs. It closes on burnout, which technical keynotes usually skip.

**Ask size.** The interviewer relays a previous day's talk — "he said, be unreasonable" (02:54) — and Krieger supplies the missing half: *why people still ask small*. His diagnosis is that the constraint is gone but the habit is not. "The first generation of AI products, we put them too much in a box and constrain their access to tools or their degrees of freedom, which means it was much harder to be unreasonable... when you say, do this thing for me, and then it would be like, well, I can't. I can barely — I can write code, but I can't really run it, or I can kind of introspect my environment, but not really" (03:31-03:53). The design corollary is that granting apparently excessive capability is what makes large asks possible: "does every single knowledge worker need a virtual machine that can write bash? On the face of it, no" — until the built-in PDF parser fails and the model writes a script instead (03:55-04:16). And teaching the ask is a product-team job, prompted by a non-technical colleague asking *him* for a change: "I'm just going to go ask Claude to do this. Like, why don't you ask Claude?... as an industry or even as a product team, we have to teach people to be more unreasonable in their usage" (03:04-03:27).

**The port.** His own most unreasonable ask was a Labs project: "I need to port this whole thing from Python to TypeScript... if I put on my 2010s engineering hat or even my early 2020s, that's a dumb idea. Who would ever port... a couple hundred thousands of lines of code. But I was like, I think this is doable now and I basically created this dynamic workflow setup and over the weekend had it port the whole thing, verify it, double-check it, then read both code... basically churn and churn and churn and then came back Monday to a completed workflow that was a ported version of that thing" (04:31-05:14). The motive was not code quality but deployment: "Claude Code had figured out a better deployment story with Bun." Pressed on whether a *product* could be ported the same way, he answers with Instagram's Python 3 migration and MonkeyType — "we captured runtime type... the types that were actually getting used in production and then map those back to the types in the code base" — and generalizes it: "if you're doing conversion or cross compiling using LLMs, you can also lean on production data a lot more or run sort of like segmented tests" (05:33-06:19). The residual difficulty is scoping, not translation: "the hardest part is always finding the boundary around where you can start doing it incrementally without trying to boil the whole ocean and swap it overnight" (06:20-06:28).

**Two 2010 scaling lessons, restated for AI.** Instagram melted in its launch week; an investor's unrelated infrastructure lunch became an impromptu clinic. What he kept: "pre-measure everything that you think you might even remotely need because the worst thing is an outage where you're like, well, is this number normal or is it high? And, oh, I don't know because I don't have data until I just added this metric," and "being really thoughtful about knobs and feature flags... dynamic config too where a lot of our runtime configurations had to be changed in a matter of seconds so that we could handle load" (07:08-07:44). He applies both directly: "I'm seeing that definitely in AI as well where we're making all sorts of different trade-offs and having that kind of runtime configuration is super key" (07:46-07:52).

**Delegation and review.** Most internal Claude usage is not the CLI. Claude Code is for "things that are more interactive or if you're iterating on a particular specific thing where you want a high bandwidth back and forth, but most usage is actually much more delegating via tagging" (08:44-09:02). The reason he emphasizes is social: "the reason it's really interesting is how multiplayer it is. And it reminds me of like Midjourney, like the fact that everyone was on Discord seeing how other people were using it" (09:02-09:11) — which is the transmission mechanism for ambition. Watching one colleague hand an agent standing ownership ("don't just fix this bug, but now you are responsible for this part of the code base and I want you to monitor this feedback channel and proactively take on tasks") made him realize "I've totally underutilized this thing. I've just been using it as a glorified Claude Code in Slack" (09:13-09:39). On review, he makes a distinction most discussions of the review bottleneck skip: "it's actually more subtle than just being bottlenecked on review, cuz that's — okay, we can carve out time differently. It's like bottlenecked on human ability to even fully conceptualize what we're doing" (10:19-10:30). The response is an artifact that travels with the change: "you would send somebody a PR, and then they'd be like, I don't know, man. This is like 2,000 lines of code. It looks like code to me. And what we started doing instead is sharing much more like, here's a Claude Code artifact. Here's the explanation. Here's the intention of the change. Here's the trade-offs that were made" (10:36-10:52), with the division of labour stated as "the code is ultimately verifiable using some things, but actually discussing intent and trade-offs, and then measuring in production" (10:52-11:08). He does not read every line: "I actually talk to Claude about the code and say, these are the questions that I would have. Can you go investigate it? So it is kind of Claude-powered code review, but still human-driven" (11:08-11:20), and cosmetic visual changes get fix-forward instead (11:21-11:29).

**How Labs is organized.** The cadence is "two-week reviews where every project goes up for we call it persevere or pivot," and "we've shut down projects basically every single one of those cycles" — treated as the design: "the more you do it, the less it's just like, oh no, my project is shut down. I failed. It's like, no, that is definitely the intention of the labs team" (12:12-12:44). The structural consequence is deliberate: "if you align the org chart too much to the individual projects, you're going to end up re-orging every two weeks, which would be a total nightmare," so teams assemble per *bet* from product and engineering, and "there is the concept of a bet lead or a directly responsible individual. But the interesting thing is that they don't manage usually any of the other people" (12:44-13:23). Engineering management survives, redefined: "the death of the engineering manager discipline has been greatly exaggerated" (11:56-12:05), and the EM's job becomes placement and coaching — "make sure every individual is assigned to the thing that they're most excited about" (13:29-13:38). Structure solidifies only after traction, with Claude Design as the worked example: ad hoc, then shipped, then a second release, then a hired dedicated team — "loose until it gets solidified down the line" (13:38-13:58).

**What he would delete.** Anthropic runs a Slack channel called "project unship" (15:41-15:48). Two deletion arguments appear. One is generational: styles were unshipped because they were "very prescriptive in the way that it worked and skills [are a] very much better application... you have to be willing to take the primitives of one generation of AI and unship them or at least supplement them or supplant them with the next one" (16:11-16:32). The other is the surface split: "we're asking people to make code versus co-work versus chat distinctions and one, they don't interoperate well and they can't delegate to each other, and two, I think the average person off the street could not explain to you why those are all different" (16:33-16:52). The symptom he names is the user acting as the integration layer: "there's nothing more frustrating than having a co-work session where you're like, great, I've mapped out exactly what I want you to build and then be like, can you please create a paragraph that I can paste into Claude Code? That is some 2020 kind of workflow that really shouldn't exist anymore" (17:02-17:16). The same complaint applies to Claude Design's future — "the things that are holding back Claude Design from being even better is better interaction with our other surfaces" (14:10-14:20).

**Why start a company.** Asked why anyone should build when "tomorrow's Anthropic could wake up and publish some markdown files that destroy my industry" (17:30-17:37), his answer separates what models cheapen from what they do not: joining Anthropic was partly a bet on "how much it would unlock a whole next generation of startups. Not because it was going to solve their ideation or their taste, but because it would make experimentation way simpler" (17:44-18:03). The labs are constrained by being platforms — a Google photos product "is going to have to be bound by the integrations that they already have and it's going to play to their strengths" (18:10-18:24) — leaving room "to be laser obsessed with your particular vertical... in a way that none of the labs are ever going to get to that level of understanding" (18:28-18:46). The honest counterweight: "it's definitely harder in the age where the models can just do a lot and so some of these things can be skillified and maybe don't need their own dedicated product" (18:47-18:57). What remains hard is unchanged — "understanding the needs of people, figuring out how you're going to reach them, listening to them and iterating on them really quickly" (18:57-19:05) — because "writing code was never the limiting part" (19:29-19:34).

**Vertical AI and finance.** Vertical startups' own evals function as an external barometer of model progress: "there's some good vertical specific finance startups that have done their own evals, which has been interesting to track. And it's not like we're playing to the eval, but it is a useful barometer around is this actually getting better at these finance use cases" (20:28-20:42). The architecture question he names for the domain is a boundary, not a feature: the model should "dive in and create just-in-time analyses or dashboards or workflows" over "the not immutable, but at least verified set of data," because "having all of that be totally free form is a recipe for confusion and is not what most companies in the financial services space want." The design work is "finding that right cut line where you have verifiability and audit logging and data provenance here, but not in a way that constrains the kinds of applications that you can build on top" (20:42-21:24). And the incumbent systems are on the wrong side of it: "a lot of the systems that were built to do the verifiability, auditability are almost by design not super flexible in terms of agentic workloads on top. So there's opportunity at both sides of the stack" (21:28-21:41).

**Burnout.** The intensity comparison is concrete: at Instagram the calendar risks were WWDC once a year and "maybe a competitor launches every three or four months"; Anthropic's Wednesday all-hands carries a "week in AI" slide that is already full mid-week (22:11-22:49). Three things he offers. A hard permission — "there's no job that is so important that you can't be offline for a couple of days" — with the reason that recovery is slow: "I've seen it happen unfortunately to people I'm really close to and then it takes a long time to recover" (22:51-23:13), plus the diagnostic that if you *can't* be offline "you're probably doing something wrong" (23:19-23:25). A framing borrowed from sports against the "it's so over / we're so back" cycle: "you're never as good as your best game and you're never as bad as your worst game," and "it's never just about today's model launch" (23:26-24:42). And a leadership practice from his coach: verbalize the emotion first, because "if you're feeling something it's really often the case that other people on the team are feeling it too" — he opened a meeting with "I'm really sad, I'm frustrated, I wish this thing had worked out," which "holds the space for other people" and only then moves to "great, what are we going to do about it" (24:50-25:36).

## Provenance and Limits

- This is an unprepared interview, not a talk with slides or data. Nothing in it is measured. There are no adoption rates, no review-time figures, no shutdown counts, no cost or latency numbers, and no before/after comparison anywhere.
- The port is the biggest claim and the least verified: "a couple hundred thousands of lines" is his own estimate, the "dynamic workflow setup" is never described beyond port/verify/double-check/churn, and no correctness result, test outcome, or deployment outcome is reported beyond "a completed workflow." Whether the ported service was actually deployed and behaved identically is not stated.
- "60 something percent of your code is written today" is the interviewer's figure about AI-written code, not Krieger's, and no source, denominator, or measurement method is given. It is recorded here as asked, not as an Anthropic statistic.
- The organizational claims are self-reported by one participant. "We've shut down projects basically every single one of those cycles" carries no count, and no failed-bet example is named. The claim that normalizing shutdowns removes the stigma is asserted from his own vantage point, not from anyone whose project was shut down.
- The startup-versus-labs argument is delivered by a lab employee to a startup audience and is explicitly hedged as such ("I'm not like giving advice on how to compete with Anthropic, I guess"). The strongest counterpoint in it is his own — "some of these things can be skillified and maybe don't need their own dedicated product" — and it is left unquantified.
- The finance material is directional. No vertical finance startup, eval, or customer is named, and "not playing to the eval" is a stated intention with no mechanism behind it.
- Product references are present-tense and undated except where he dates them: Claude Code artifacts "shipped a couple weeks ago," a Claude Design "big second release in June," styles unshipped "recently."
- Caption artifacts resolved: "Cloud Code" → Claude Code and "Cloud Design" → Claude Design throughout; "the topic co-founders" → the Anthropic co-founders (22:55); "an Entropic" → Anthropic (24:09); "the week in AI at Pinterest" → the week in AI at Anthropic (22:35); "Bourbon" → Burbn, Instagram's predecessor app (15:26); "most people often their own CLIs" → most people off in their own CLIs (09:59); "Monkey Type" is the real Instagram tool MonkeyType (05:56). People's definitely way way smarter than me," which in context is the model name Fable and is quoted here accordingly.
- Left as heard: "internal snapshots of what became Mythos and Fable" (01:47); the async delegation surface is captioned only as "tag"/"tags" (08:03-09:02) and no fuller product name is audible; "was not really AGI filled" (16:17) is most plausibly "AGI-pilled" but is not resolvable from the audio; "the Bun Zig to Rust version" (05:17) is the interviewer's reference and is recorded as spoken; Chris Lovejoy's prior company is captioned "interior" (19:53), most plausibly Anterior, and is not asserted here.
