## Summary

Peter Gostev uses BullshitBench and Arena vote data to show that rising benchmark scores can hide stubborn model failures: many models still answer nonsensical premises instead of pushing back, extra reasoning can worsen that behavior, and user dissatisfaction remains visible even among top models on real expert prompts.

## Notes

- BullshitBench asks models nonsensical questions and grades whether they clearly push back, partially accommodate, or accept the premise; the speaker reports about 155 questions and uses LLM-as-judge grading checked against manual reading. (02:19-04:33)
- A good response should reject or reframe an invalid question, while a bad response may acknowledge doubt briefly and then invent proxy explanations or proceed with analysis anyway. (03:27-04:27)
- In the BullshitBench results, some current top models pushed back well, but GPT and Gemini families were described as roughly 50/50 on whether they accepted the nonsense; even green responses sometimes tried to accommodate the bad premise. (04:33-05:29)
- Increasing reasoning effort did not reliably help on nonsense-premise tasks; the speaker says it often made results worse, with some traces questioning the premise once and then spending many paragraphs trying to solve it. (06:36-08:23)
- Arena's anonymous battle mode includes a "both bad" option; the speaker treats that as a dissatisfaction rate and uses it to inspect failure rates over time for top-model battles. (09:31-12:09)
- The top-25 model dissatisfaction rate reportedly improved from roughly 17-20% before reasoning models to about 9%, but the speaker argues that 9% remains high because users are still rejecting both responses from strong models. (12:09-12:55)
- Expert-prompt analysis showed uneven improvement: quantitative tasks improved substantially, while creative writing, finance, law, gaming, and some software subcategories improved less or reflected shifting user expectations. (13:12-18:43)
- The closing argument is that narrow, well-specified benchmarks can be true while still missing fuzzy human judgment and the bottom of the real-work distribution. (18:49-19:36)
