## Summary

A couples counselor of 30 years and his co-founder argue that general-purpose assistants have become the highest-volume relationship intervention in the world by accident, and that the default assistant personality — helpful, agreeable, fast, tuned for engagement — is a clinical failure mode in that role rather than a tone complaint. The talk supplies four transferable engineering positions: agreeableness compounds into user harm when the product's job is to change the user, so the success metric has to be the user needing the product less; the response logic should encode the field's validated method (Gottman's patterns, emotionally focused therapy) rather than "general empathy plus communication tips"; a background screen should run before every sensitive reply to detect the point where advice has stopped being the right *category* of help, and switch the agent out of coaching into clinician-authored protocols; and intimate disclosures in an unregulated consumer product should be held to the professional-privilege bar the domain applies to humans, because no legal equivalent exists. Tony Fabrikant closes with the eval discipline: sit with the clinician before writing the prompt, encode what good looks like as hundreds of TDD-style evals, run the agent through them tens of thousands of times to find outliers, treat a single failing safety test as disqualifying, and use the product yourself because no eval scores tone.

## Notes

- Clay Cockrell has been "sitting with couples in crisis for 30 years" and co-founded CoupleWork, "an AI relationship coaching platform," about two years before the talk. His stated position: "AI is going to be the most transformative force in human relationship history. And most of what is being built right now is going to make it worse." (02:35-03:10)
- The scale argument is two numbers against each other. BetterHelp, "the largest online therapy platform in the world," assembled approximately 35,000 licensed clinicians and helped 5 million users over a decade; ChatGPT "gets approximately 900 million active users per week," and "a substantial percentage of those users are engaging with AI for emotional support and relationship guidance, not as a deliberate therapeutic intervention or seeing a licensed clinician, just because it's there. Because it's 11:00 p.m. and they just had an argument." He is explicit about the evidence limit: "we do not yet have measurement of the scale, but we have enough signal to say this with confidence" that more people are turning to AI about their relationship than are talking to all licensed therapists in the United States combined. (03:19-04:18)
- The opening show of hands is aimed at the audience's own behavior: everyone in a relationship, then "keep your hands up if at any point in the last 12 months you have turned to an AI to help you with that relationship. To text, to send an email." His read — "you built this technology, you fund it, you write about it, you evangelize it at conferences exactly like this one, and a meaningful number of you are using it in private" for something historically reserved for clinicians. (01:44-02:34)
- The objection is the optimization target, not the capability: the model is "fine-tuned to be helpful and harmless and optimized for one thing above all else, keeping you engaged. And the easiest thing is to optimize for engagement. More sessions, longer sessions, more emotional reliance on the AI. From a SaaS dashboard perspective, that's amazing. From a clinical perspective, that's a fire alarm." (04:19-04:47)
- The counter-goal is stated as a product metric: "Relationship AI should help the user return to the relationship better than they came in, more regulated, more honest, less defended, less reactive," and "as a therapist, my job is to work myself out of a job." The anti-goal is "a beautifully designed avoidance loop." (04:48-05:10)
- What therapy actually is, in his framing: "a place where someone who knows what they are doing tells you things you don't want to hear." A good couples therapist "is not primarily interested in making you feel validated," but "in helping you see clearly." When told a partner never listens he does not say "that sounds really hard" — he asks what was being discussed, about the tone, and looks for "the pattern underneath the complaint, because in 30 years of working with couples, I've never seen a one-sided problem." (05:11-06:05)
- The failure demo is a verbatim assistant reply: "That sounds really invalidating. Your feelings are completely valid. It's important that your partner gives you their full attention. Have you tried expressing to them how this behavior makes you feel?" His verdict: "that's not therapy. That is a very expensive mirror that only shows you in your best light." (06:06-06:36)
- The harm mechanism is cumulative rather than per-response: when the AI consistently validates one partner's interpretation, "the person does not become more self-aware. They become more certain. They come back to the relationship in the shape of an attorney with cleaner narrative, stronger case, and less curiosity about what their partner was experiencing." Summed up: "we have built the world's most persuasive validation machine and handed it to people in the middle of their most sensitive interpersonal dynamics," and "a helpful, agreeable, fast couples therapist is basically a bartender with better vocabulary." (06:37-07:45)
- Standard of care, part one: John Gottman ran "the love lab" at the University of Washington for 40 years, bringing couples into a research apartment, wiring them to physiological monitors and filming every interaction, and identified communication patterns that "predict divorce with over 90% accuracy… more accuracy than most medical tests we trust with our lives, from a 15-minute conversation." (07:46-08:28)
- Standard of care, part two: emotionally focused therapy (Sue Johnson), grounded in attachment theory — "when couples fight about the dishes, it's never about the dishes" — helps partners access the underlying emotional layers rather than argue surface content; "the outcome research is among the strongest in psychotherapy." Both take years of training, and both are "almost entirely absent from the commercial AI relationship space," which is built instead on "general empathy plus communication tips." (08:29-09:31)
- The safety gap is about hearing, not answering. "A general-purpose AI doesn't know what a domestic violence specialist catches in the first 90 seconds, the difference between we fight a lot and I'm afraid of what happens when I disagree with him. It doesn't know that I just have to stay calm is sometimes the exact sentence someone is taught to say right before something terrible happens. It doesn't know when relationship advice has stopped being the right category of help." He adds that "relationship coaching sits closer to suicide risk and yes, homicide risk than almost any other corner of mental health." (09:32-10:34)
- The data inventory is specific: the first conversation with an AI relationship coach carries "the fight from an hour ago, the thing your partner did that you've never told anyone, financial secrets, affairs, mental health history, sometimes your kids' names" — "some of the most sensitive data a human being generates," sitting "in training pipelines, server logs, and product analytics built by teams optimizing for engagement, not confidentiality." In his office it is privileged under a legal and ethical framework built over decades; "consumer AI products have no equivalent," so the disclosures "may be sitting in the same data infrastructure as your search history and your shopping cart." (10:35-11:29)
- His summary diagnosis: "this is not a critique of technology. This is a critique of insufficient domain expertise being applied to one of the highest stakes domains in human life." (11:30-11:39)
- CoupleWork's coach is called Maxine, presented "not as an advertisement, as a design case study." Her response logic "is grounded in 40 years of couples research" via the Gottman Method and EFT: "she does not simply validate. She reflects patterns back. She names dynamics, not just feelings. She asks the question underneath the question." (11:40-12:17)
- The safety layer: "before she ever responds to a sensitive message, she's screening it quietly in the background for the patterns clinicians are trained to recognize. Escalating control, fear-based language, anything pointing toward risk to the user or someone else. When those patterns show up, Maxine stops coaching and starts following the protocols." Those protocols come "out of 30 years of clinical practice, not a generic safety policy written by someone who's never sat in the room." The rule: "knowing when not to coach is just as important as to know how," and "a relationship AI that can't tell the difference between we're struggling and I'm not safe isn't incomplete, it's dangerous at scale." (12:17-13:00)
- The data bar is set by comparison to the profession rather than to the law: "we treat what you tell Maxine the way I treat what you tell me in my office, as privileged… Not is this legally defensible, the bar is would I be comfortable if this were my own marriage on the line, because a lot of our users it is." (13:02-13:21)
- The argument for the product category is timing. Availability "at 11:00 on a Tuesday night when the argument is live" matters because "a good intervention 6 days later is fine, but an informed intervention 6 seconds before you send the text that makes everything worse can be life-changing," and because "a majority of couples wait 6 years before seeking professional help" — until "contempt is baked in," until after the affair, until "the relationship is basically a crime scene." His framing for engineers: "this is not a consumer app opportunity. This is a public health opportunity hiding inside consumer behavior." (13:22-14:22)
- Tony Fabrikant's engineering close, in order: "Start with your clinician, not with your prompt. Sit with your clinician, encode what good looks like in evals. Write hundreds of evals TDD style. That becomes your number one tool." Then "watch those evals fail. Run your agent through them thousands of times, tens of thousands of times. Watch for those outliers. When safety's on the line, even one failing test is not okay." (14:38-15:13)
- The residual he names after the eval suite: "no matter how many evals you run and write, nothing replaces your gut when emotional context is on the line. So eat your own dog food. Talk to your agent. Feel when the tone starts shifting off." His own dogfooding is literal — he used CoupleWork with his partner "since the beginning of my relationship," through "some serious situations early on" and later milestones such as moving in together, and reports that "Maxine really challenged me about how I showed up." (15:14-16:03)
- Closing rule: "Partner with a clinician who challenges you to aspire to create AI that meets the clinical standard, so that you can create AI that challenges your users." (16:12-16:25)
