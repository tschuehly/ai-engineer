## Summary

A nineteen-minute lightning tour of go-to-market engineering from someone at a vendor that sells the infrastructure, structured as four problems in dependency order: data, orchestration, agents, execution. It is the wiki's fifth go-to-market source and the first that treats the world model as something you have to *buy*, and therefore the first that costs it. The data section supplies the missing mechanics under "keep a live model of your market": no single vendor covers a field, so you waterfall providers until it is filled and run evals to decide which to trust; refreshing everything is unaffordable when the data is purchased, so field refresh becomes a per-field decision priced by volatility; and because each vendor represents the same company differently, entity resolution is a precondition rather than a cleanup step. The orchestration section reclassifies tool sprawl as data engineering — heterogeneous refresh needs across 10-30 tools, fan-out to many destinations, constant partial failure, and a specific race condition where the tools sync to each other behind your orchestrator, so a contact created in the CRM is not yet actionable in the sequencer. Clay's answer is a graph of five general-purpose node types: agents, tool calls, conditionals, code, and a map-reduce fan-out. The agent section proposes one long-lived agent per account, dormant most of the time, woken by triggers or a heartbeat, rehydrating from the data and orchestration layers, with a learning phase the speaker says is explicitly unsolved — and one concrete operational rule: agents get their own CRM fields, separate from those written by deterministic systems or people. The execution section is where the constraints turn human: reply rates of half a percent to one percent make agent mistakes expensive at the margin, sending as the rep puts company domain reputation at stake, so you split domains and then have to route replies home, and a booked call has to suppress the email sequence and the lifecycle campaign. Asked afterward for the hardest problem, he does not name any of the four: it is the interface between the human and the agent, because a rep may disagree with what the agent decided or not know it happened.

## Notes

- The framing claim for the role: GTM teams "realized that it is now possible to ship as fast as a product and engineering team," and the best ones are "pushing changes to their GTM structure almost at the same cadence that an engineering team might be doing releases" — at Clay, "every 2 weeks we're pushing new data to our teams, we're pushing new automations, we're of course running new campaigns." (00:32-01:15)
- The definition offered is negative rather than functional: "GTM engineering at its heart is really about removing the constraints that have historically stopped GTM teams from shipping at speed using technology." He adds a claim about the role itself — "it's one of the first roles that actually is an index on the advances that we're making in AI. And so, as models have become more powerful, GTM engineers have gained more leverage within their organization and become more valuable." (01:15-01:58)
- The talk is explicitly four problems in dependency order — data, orchestration, agents, execution — and he flags the vendor position up front: "If you're not familiar with Clay, we are obviously building infrastructure that kind of attacks these areas. But this is not meant to be a sales pitch." (01:58-02:19)
- The data goal is stated as a copy, not a summary: "the core goal that I think we're trying to accomplish with our data is to create a perfect virtual copy of the market, the ideal customers, the accounts and contacts that you're going after." The base model is two entities: accounts (companies) and contacts (people). (02:19-03:00)
- Why the copy will not hold still — the state changes come from three directions at once. The account changes ("a company gets acquired, opens offices, ships products"), you change it ("you're marketing at them, you're selling towards them, you're trying to book meetings. That's changing the state of the account"), and the company generates signals on its own ("hiring people and firing people and doing things that provide signals for you"). "As GTM engineers, we need to manage all of this state within our accounts." (03:00-03:42)
- What the state actually looks like in the CRM: "a ton of fields that are simply telling me what state this account is in, whether they're a customer or not, whether they're expanding or churning, how big they are, how well they're scoring." The build order in a new CRM is contacts first, then third-party layering: "the account hierarchy, the firmographics that describe how big the company is and so forth, the technographics that describe what technology they're using, and then I'm trying to create signals on top of those accounts." (03:42-04:42)
- Waterfalling, with the one number in the section: "within GTM, there is literally hundreds of vendors that you can turn to… but none of those vendors is going to have a complete picture of all the information you desire. And so, the key technique here is called waterfalling. This is where I'm going to actually go and look into multiple providers to try to fill in all the information that I need. If you see here, if I was just using Forager to get phone numbers for this set of countries, I'd only get halfway there." It generalizes: "that's not only true for phone numbers, but for most the other data points that we care about within GTM." (04:42-05:18)
- The selection mechanism is an eval, and it can be delegated: "either you or the vendor that you're using needs to run evals against these data providers in order to obtain the most accurate information." (05:18-05:28)
- Refresh is a budget, not a schedule: "It's also incredibly expensive to update data all the time, especially if you're purchasing it. So, I can't just update all the fields. I need to kind of selectively choose which fields to update." Pull is not the only direction — "if I'm running a signals program, information that I need is getting pushed to me all the time as well." (05:38-05:57)
- Entity resolution is named as a distinct requirement of the multi-vendor design: "because I'm using multiple third-party sources, the actual representation of a single account in those different sources is going to be different. So, I need to resolve the entities in between them." The section closes with the stakes: "a great data layer will tackle all of these things and allow me to move on to the more interesting work, but without this, it's really, really hard to execute automated GTM plays." (05:57-06:19)
- Orchestration is defined as a maintenance job over disagreeing systems: "in most cases, the view of the world that those tools have is different depending on where you look. And so, orchestration, in my view, is really the act of keeping all of that up to date." The named base stack is "CRM, data warehouse, sequencer, a dialer, a note taker for call recording, and some chat interface like Slack," but "I usually see like 10 or 20 or 30 tools that actually these teams are interfacing with." (06:19-07:19)
- The four properties that make it data engineering rather than integration: heterogeneous cadence ("some systems need kind of like real-time updates one record at a time. Other systems are going to need hundreds of thousands of records, maybe updated once a day… monthly or weekly"); heterogeneous field volatility ("some data points like employee count change all the time. Other data points like headquarters location change very rarely"); fan-out ("take a single system that I'm working with and actually fan its information out to multiple different systems"); and constant partial failure ("because I end up with this kind of distributed setup, I also have failures that are happening all the time"). (07:19-08:18)
- The race condition, stated concretely: "usually they are not fully orchestrated, which means that one system is talking to each other while I'm trying to talk to both of those systems at the same time. So for example, if you have a Salesforce connected to Outreach or a sequencer, usually that CRM [and] sequencer are syncing independently of your orchestration system. And so if you create contacts in your CRM, you actually need to wait for that contact to sync to the sequencer before you can then take action on it. This creates some difficult problems where you actually need to introduce things like [waits] and loops to check if information is ready." (08:18-08:55)
- Clay's orchestration model, after iteration: "we are basically taking a graph-based view of the orchestration problem with a series of general purpose nodes that are executing various things. And so we have nodes that run agents, nodes that make tool calls, nodes that handle our conditional logic, nodes that run code, and then nodes that run effectively this map reduce system to fan out the information and bring it back." The recommendation is deliberately vendor-neutral: "whether you buy it or build it, I think this is like fundamentally the kind of modern way to set this up." A run is "some sort of event that kicks off my orchestration, some trigger or some schedule," then talking to several systems, combining, and pushing out "to different interfaces that my reps are using." (08:55-09:53)
- The account picture that motivates agents: "multiple signals that are occurring, data that needs to be updated, actions that my agents or reps are taking, and then meetings that are happening and feedback that's occurring" — described as "one of the simplest views of what is happening with an account." Today "we are relying on sales reps in a lot of cases to manually sort through this." (09:59-10:38)
- Three properties that make GTM agents hard, given as a set: duration ("agents that run over a course of weeks or months that keep track of the state of an account throughout a deal cycle"), consequence ("a high bar for error because a lot of the results of our GTM work is customer communication and getting that wrong can have disastrous consequences"), and impedance mismatch ("the agents are often doing unstructured work and pushing that into systems that are highly structured like a CRM. And so the mapping of what the agent is producing is super important"). (10:38-11:21)
- The proposed architecture: "an agent that exists for each account and maintains a persistent state of that account. It's always going to execute, and because it's executing over [a course] of weeks or months, it's often going to be dormant for most of the time that it's available. So, we need to use smart triggers or a heartbeat or something to wake it up. And then when it wakes up, it needs to ingest the current context of the account from our data layer and our orchestration layer. And because the agent is making decisions for us in many cases automatically, we need to allow for feedback on the agent as well." (11:21-11:47)
- The learning phase is named and disclaimed in the same breath: "the cutting edge of doing this is the learning phase where as the agent works on an account or a series of accounts, it updates its own view of what's working. Today in GTM, this is not fully solved yet. And in fact, the continual learning effort and the next best action suggestions are kind of one of the cutting edge problems that we're working on." (11:47-12:10)
- The one prescriptive rule in the agent section: the demo agent pulls from several sources, uses reasoning steps, and updates "different values in my CRM that are just for the agents. So, I always recommend separating the fields that agents are updating from the fields that deterministic systems are updating or that people are updating." (12:10-12:43)
- The fuller example is a closed-lost re-awaken agent talking to "Gong, email, CRM, and my data warehouse," triggered on time rather than on an event: "if we lose an account, we're not going to immediately go after that account again. We're going to wait for a little bit of time in order to attack it again. So, there's a number of timing and context issues that we have to address when we're building agents for GTM." (12:43-13:11)
- Execution is placed last and called hardest: "unfortunately, messaging and execution is one of the hardest problems in GTM engineering." (13:11-13:42)
- The channel numbers, with his own caveat attached: "generally, what we observe is that cold email works less and less well as the years go on. This is a trend that's been true forever… if you look at the far left here, I actually think these are pretty elevated rates for some of these, but the relative differences between these channels is correct. So, LinkedIn can be three to four times more effective than cold email. Cold calling and cold email are roughly the same. And then on the right here, this is a series of statistics from [Smartlead]. This is across, I think, 20 million emails. And so, you can see we've got somewhere between a half a percent and 1% reply rates." (13:42-14:19)
- Why that sets the bar for agents: "if we've got 100 contacts that we're sequencing, maybe one of them will reply. And so, that really raises the stakes for agentic execution within GTM because if your agents are doing the wrong things, then you're missing out on the margin, which is where most GTM teams are having success." (14:19-14:39)
- The identity decision and its blast radius: "do you email on behalf of the rep, or do you let the agent do the emailing? If you email on behalf of the rep, what that means is like everett@clay.com is actually reaching out directly to customers. But if I do that in the wrong way, or I don't get the replies that I need, that then means that my overall domain reputation is going to suffer for my company." (14:39-15:07)
- The workaround creates its own problem: "a common technique then is to use multiple domains to get in touch with customers. But then if I do that, I actually need to find a way to route responses on that domain back to my main domain so my reps can process it." (15:07-15:26)
- Cross-channel suppression as the second execution problem: "if you get a call connection and a meeting booked on your call sequence, you then need to suppress your email sequence and maybe [unenroll] someone from a life cycle marketing campaign. So, the coordination of the execution of all of this is also a hard problem and also something that we can use agents to help resolve." (15:26-15:48)
- Both modes run at once in Clay's own setup: "a kind of rep proxied view where we have a bunch of rep inboxes that are connected to our sequencer and we're actually sending that on behalf of reps. But like I said, we do this for kind of a portion of our accounts. For many of our accounts, we actually use multiple domains to go after them and then we have to tackle the routing problem." (15:48-16:09)
- The closing claim is about neglect rather than difficulty: "a lot of teams actually overlook some of the harder pieces here and don't necessarily design around some of the constraints that they have to deal with." (16:09-16:35)
- Q&A — the hardest problem is not on the four-part list: "I think one of the harder problems is probably the interface between the human and the agent… I think the most powerful use of agents within GTM is to act as the reasoning and decision layer for a lot of tasks that a sales rep was previously doing. And so you run into a lot of instances where the rep might think that they should do something different or the rep might not know that the agent did something. So, because ultimately a human still needs to get on a call with a prospect, coordinating the connection between what the automated systems are doing and what the human sales rep is doing and making that work well, I think is probably one of the hardest problems." (16:51-17:47)
- Q&A — scope covers inbound, not just outbound: "the orchestration problem is pretty acute in inbound. You have to get the routing right. You have to qualify the account properly. There's usually historical context on inbound that comes in that needs to be understood. So, I think GTM engineering covers all of GTM." (17:47-18:33)

## Provenance and Limits

- This is a vendor talk. Every screenshot is Clay's product, the waterfall example is Clay's own coverage chart, and the "hundreds of vendors" problem is the one Clay exists to intermediate. The speaker flags this himself ("this is not meant to be a sales pitch") and frames the recommendations as buy-or-build-neutral, but no independent implementation is described.
- Almost nothing is measured. The four-part architecture, the graph node vocabulary, the per-account agent, and the agent-field separation rule all arrive as recommendations with no accuracy, latency, throughput, cost, or adoption figure attached, and no comparison against a team that did it differently.
- The only quantitative claims in the talk are about data coverage and channel performance, and both are second-hand. The Forager phone-number example is "only get halfway there" for one field across one unspecified country set, shown on a Clay chart. The reply-rate figures are attributed to Smartlead across "I think, 20 million emails," and the speaker explicitly discounts the absolute values on his own slide ("I actually think these are pretty elevated rates") while defending only the relative ordering — so "LinkedIn three to four times cold email" should be read as a ranking claim, not a rate.
- The learning half of the per-account agent is disclaimed rather than demonstrated: continual learning and next-best-action are called cutting-edge and "not fully solved yet," so the loop that would make the architecture self-improving is aspiration in this source.
- The eval recommendation has no method. "Run evals against these data providers" is stated without a ground-truth source, sample design, per-field metric, or refresh policy for the eval itself — which matters because the ground truth for a phone number is the same market the vendors are selling.
- The hardest-problem answer in Q&A is an opinion, not a finding: no mechanism, interface design, or measurement is offered for the human-agent coordination gap the speaker names.
- Caption artifacts: "at Clari" at 00:55 is Clay; "weights and loops" at 08:50 is waits and loops; "a closed loss free awaken agent" at 12:43 is a closed-lost re-awaken agent; "Smart Lead" is Smartlead; "unenrolled someone" at 15:36 is unenroll. Left as heard: "Forager" as the named phone-number vendor, and "consta[nt] flux" truncated at 03:00.
