## Summary

A nineteen-minute talk from a principal sales operations and strategy manager in Cloudflare's revenue operations organization, six months into the role after seven years on the machine learning side. It is the wiki's first go-to-market source told from the back office rather than from the sales floor or the architecture diagram: the unit of work is an operations team that either rebuilds the same analysis in spreadsheets every week or ships dashboards that "meet the needs of most teams, but not all of them." Joyce's answer is a three-pillar framework — scale analysis, scale insight, scale self-service — implemented as role-specific skill files carrying business semantics plus the questions the business actually asks, a three-agent drafter/reviewer/tone pipeline that writes the weekly go-to-market summary over pre-shaped data, and Cloudflare OS, an internal agentic workspace on Workers and Durable Objects where reps pull forecast briefs, QBR decks, account plans, and renewal prep against a centrally reviewed skill repository. The findings he closes on are curation, the feedback loop, and layering; the number he closes on is "2x our efficiency," and it is unsupported by any method.

- [Push the Narrative Because Dashboard Adoption Is Always Uneven](../concepts/push-the-narrative-because-dashboard-adoption-is-always-uneven.md) - some people never open a dashboard, so the story has to be delivered and the dashboard demoted to drill-down.
- [Layer Ask, Push, and Self-Serve Because Teams Interface Differently](../concepts/layer-ask-push-and-self-serve-because-teams-interface-differently.md) - three delivery modes for the same substrate, none of which replaces the others.
- [Run a Submission-and-Review Alias for Shared Skills](../concepts/run-a-submission-and-review-alias-for-shared-skills.md) - a central intake with named curators as the control on skill proliferation during a capability explosion.
- [Separate the Context Gap From the Expert Gap](../concepts/separate-the-context-gap-from-the-expert-gap.md) - two distinct deficits in frontline work, with different fixes and different measures.
- [Read-Side Agents Scale First Because the Write Side Needs Approvals](../concepts/read-side-agents-scale-first-because-the-write-side-needs-approvals.md) - the honest sequencing behind an internal rollout, and the reason quoting and CRM writes are still ahead.

## Notes

- The diagnosis is about the operations function, not the model: "usually teams on the back office side... they're either doing work in Excel or sheets at worst, um building analysis each week, multiple hours a week, and as they take on multiple projects, it gets exponentially long with how many of those analysis that they're doing. Uh at best, they are producing dashboards... which, you know, meets the needs of most teams, um but not all of them." (01:57-02:46)
- Two named gaps on the go-to-market side. The context gap is the switching cost: a rep goes from a prospect call to a current-customer call to an adoption conversation and "they have to gather information for those specific calls, which is good... but they have to do all that work in between." The expert gap is between "how your expert salesperson... how he would handle a customer satisfaction issue" and "a new salesperson or someone that's just ramping up," with the target stated as "everyone working at the same operational level, so you have consistency in execution, consistency in messaging." (02:55-04:35)
- Pillar one is stated as a time conversion — "how can they take things that would take 2 hours to do down to 5 minutes?" — and as a role change for the operations team: with analysis scaled, "we can actually focus on the second part of my job, which is strategy." He dates his own first skill files to January. (04:56-05:49)
- Skill files are role-specific and cover both audiences: technical users "who are building SQL and being able to data engineer a lot of solutions" and non-technical users "who are closer to the business with the sales people who may not know how to write SQL." (06:47-07:18)
- The distinguishing content of the skill file is the question set, arrived at empirically: "through testing, we've included the types of questions that the business would ask of the data. In this case, looking at closed date changes and opportunities, as well as uh changes in the amount of the opportunities, so that we can answer essentially 80% or more of the questions, where the other 20% might be more uh complex strategic questions." (07:27-07:53)
- The bottleneck that disappears is a person, not a query: "I've seen users who do not know any SQL, and essentially their request in the past we bottleneck to someone who knows data and can write SQL for complex queries, be able to just ask questions of the data and get answers." (08:02-08:15)
- The same files have a second consumer — application code. "Usually, you know, that is done in IT and bottlenecked in those areas, we're able to use the semantic information about the business knowledge, as well as the columns table to build these applications rather quickly." (08:29-08:50)
- Pillar two's thesis is one sentence: "there's a story in the data and they really shouldn't have to search for it." The artifact is a weekly summary "which highlights how the business is doing, how they're pacing to their goals, and then highlighting trends, standouts, as well as watches," with the phone-assistant morning briefing as the explicit analogy. The screen shown is synthetic data. (08:57-09:42)
- The justification for pushing is a distribution fact rather than a preference: "there's different level of adoption of the KPI metrics at any given company. You're going to have people who love dashboards, people are never going to look at them. So I think you really need to have a way to scaffold that across the business." (09:42-10:04)
- The data preparation is where the determinism comes from: "a big part of this is simplifying the data so that the AI agents can actually analyze the data in a very consistent and clean way. Here what we do is we transform the data by the dimension of time, also slice of the logical part of the business, which is manager, theater, and finally the metric." The demo table is wide, the trend feed is long, and pre-processing highlights trends. "The embedding of the logic of how you would filter this data to even analyze it, as well as the logical aggregations the business want to see is all engineered up front. This... handles 80 or plus percent of the requests." (10:04-11:03)
- The writing pipeline has three agents and one of them is not about correctness: "we first get the data, and then we do a first pass draft on the data calling our MCPs, and then this we have a second reviewer agent who checks the veracity of the data, and then we have a third agent, which is a tone agent, who using a multi-shot prompt is able to just craft the message and highlight the risks and opportunities equally. And with every run, we have observability into each of the LLM calls, so we can see what is passed and what is the response." (11:03-11:41)
- Acceptance was manual and long: "this architecture we tested for about 2-3 months and, you know, looking every single run to see what is going wrong." The stated ambition is to extend the same pipeline "beyond just what I've shown you for multiple teams, but also down to the customer level." (11:41-12:03)
- Cloudflare OS is described as "an agentic workspace that is running on Cloudflare where the go-to-market team can come in here. It spins up their own compute and their own persistent environment using Cloudflare workers as well as durable objects, which is basically a storage sort of like S3." (12:03-12:29)
- The use-case list is artifact-shaped rather than chat-shaped: "doing a forecast brief, building QBR decks, building a purchase deck on what the customer that they're onboarding has purchased, doing account planning, general queries of the data, as well as renewal preparation." Screenshots show a prescriptive daily plan being assembled from MCP calls and a generated custom slide deck for a customer call. (12:36-14:28)
- The workspace is three parts plus a gateway: "through the three-part piece of the skills in the lower left, which is our expert level information, as well as the MCP connection and the AI gateway, they're able to have conversations in this agentic workspace to pull data they need and using expert-level skills, which is curated." (13:01-13:35)
- Curation has a concrete mechanism: "we have a central alias where skills are presented to the central team, curated by the go-to-market team, as well as by operations team, and they're reviewed, so we can make sure that we're not having a proliferation of skills, and we have an expert-level knowledge skill at every level." (13:36-14:02)
- Closing findings, in his order: "skill curation is the basis for all of this agentic workforce... you're really able to give them the ability to use the agentic systems in a more predictable and deterministic way so that they can execute evenly across the board"; the feedback loop, argued by analogy to selling externally — "with these internal teams, the feedback loop is very important to be able to see is what you're building is it actually useful?"; and layering, because "some of the go-to-market team, that's how they like to interface with the operations team is to be able to ask questions," then push, then self-service. (15:34-16:55)
- The outcome claim is a single unqualified figure: "we've really been able to 2x our efficiency and be able to serve the teams." No baseline, denominator, or method is given. (16:56-17:08)
- The forward list separates two kinds of hard. Integration is a plumbing-and-security problem — "how can we set up meetings for the go-to-market team and embed those artifacts in those meetings so they don't have to actually pull them... but that requires some information or some security setup" — while "quoting and approvals and updating the CRM itself" is the harder class, currently mid-build against Salesforce and planned to reuse the multi-agent shape: "I see that being set up in a way that I set up with that automated analysis where you have workflows to just make sure that everything is getting done right." (17:08-18:11)
- The governance concern is framed as a phase rather than a policy: "we're sort of reached the Cambrian stage of using agentic systems, which means there's an explosion of excitement and skills and finding out ways to solve anything with AI. But I see as we get to this fuller integration and standardization, we're going to want to come back and not really limit, but just figure out a really strategic approach for allowing each team to use the agentic system so that the source of truth in all the systems are aligning." (18:19-18:49)

## Provenance and Limits

- Nothing in the talk is measured with a stated method. "2x our efficiency," "80% or more of the questions," "80 or plus percent of the requests," and "2 hours to 5 minutes" are all recollections without a baseline, sample, or definition; the 80% figures apply to two different things (question coverage in a skill file, request coverage by the pre-aggregated tables) and are asserted separately.
- The reviewer agent's accuracy is not reported. The evidence for the pipeline working is the two-to-three-month manual review of every run, with no described pass criterion, defect count, error taxonomy, or held-out eval set — and no statement of what happens now that the reading stopped.
- The weekly summary shown on screen is explicitly synthetic data, so no real output quality can be judged from the demo.
- Adoption is not reported. The talk gives no user count, usage rate, or retention figure for Cloudflare OS or the weekly summary, which is the axis a comparable internal-assistant source ([Izmit](20260826_DrTdD-ttjCY.md)) identifies as the binding constraint.
- The speaker is six months into the role and describes the framework as one "I'm proposing... as we flesh this out in the future," so parts of it are intent rather than operating history.
- Vendor dogfooding: the internal workspace runs on the speaker's own employer's platform (Workers, Durable Objects, AI Gateway), and no alternative was evaluated.
- One technical characterization is loose: Durable Objects are described as "basically a storage sort of like S3," which understates that they are stateful single-instance compute with attached storage. Recorded as heard.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "Agentyc"/"Agenty" is agentic, "Cloudflare S setup" is Cloudflare OS, and "the uh burning gun or the smoking gun" is the speaker's own self-correction. Left as heard: the host's name ("Moda"), the previous employer ("Grainger"), and "theater" as the organizational slice.
