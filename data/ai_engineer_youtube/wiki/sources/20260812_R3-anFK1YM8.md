## Summary

Stefania Druga, a research scientist at Sakana AI in Tokyo, reports a single-author ablation study of *memory harnesses* run entirely on local models on her own machine. The framing she asks the audience to keep is that memory is a **write–manage–read control loop wrapped around the model**, not a database you attach to it: "it's not just a database store. It's actually this control loop around the model" (03:36-03:55). Her harness has three blocks — a **core** always shown to the agent, a **recall** block whose policy is the thing under test, and an **archival** block that carries information across sessions. She starts from research agents specifically because "they have zero durable memory, and I wanted all the memory to come from the harness" (03:57-04:28). The model is held fixed across every task; the only thing that varies is the recall policy, which climbs a ladder: no recall at all, vector RAG by similarity, a **decisions ledger** that records what was decided on every turn and then ranks those decisions, and an **oracle** that is handed the correct memory for every loop as ground truth (04:28-05:27).

The two tasks bracket the boundary the study is really about. Task one is a literature review whose corpus contains a Nature paper claiming the discovery of 742,000 promising materials — a claim later retracted, where the retraction is "a much smaller haystack needle in that corpus than the headlines and the citations" (05:29-06:05). Every paper fit inside the context window, and the harness changed nothing: "it was the same performance with memory and without memory, and it only added more cost… when your task fits in context, the harness doesn't add much" (06:14-06:39). Task two comes from xbench, an established long-horizon memory benchmark, and inverts that: the answer sits at step 124 while the question is asked at step 500, "completely outside of the context window," so the model has to go through the harness to get it (06:56-07:33). Across 68 questions with multiple trials and seeds, the **rank-only ledger performed best** — better than vector similarity, and better than gating the harness by asking the model "do you need to use memory or do you not need to use memory" (07:34-08:32). Ablations that fed arbitrary examples, the wrong step, and the most recent step left the ranked policy on top, and the result held on a second model (DeepSeek V4 Flash as well as the model captioned "Qwen 27B") and on a second benchmark (Spider V2) (08:59-09:36).

Two findings do most of the transferable work. The **oracle does not reach the ceiling**: it "provides the right information, the right memory to the model, but it doesn't force it to use it," so the model "can get the right memory but still retrieve the wrong information or choose to ignore it or be confused" (08:35-08:59). And ranked recall was not merely more accurate but *cheaper*: "it's not just that it gives you better recall, it actually costs less," which she compresses into "bad memory is expensive because it spends more tokens and it can send the agent the wrong way," while "a good structural policy for recall can save you a lot of tokens and budget" (09:36-10:02). Her ask is to treat **recall policy as a first-class metric** and to answer three design questions explicitly: what types of memory do you store, how do you rank them, and how do you design the recall function — plus a fourth about what survives across repeated runs and sessions (10:02-10:35). The motivation is timing: she cites METR's projections that task horizons keep lengthening while model releases get rarer, converging later in the year on "many more long-term horizon tasks and fewer model releases," which makes context rot a priority rather than a curiosity (01:07-01:36). Running local also bought her control of "the data I was using, the entire traces of compute and evaluations," which she calls an example of sovereignty — at the cost of serial execution, since the local models do not support batch querying, which is why the evals were still running in Tokyo and on the flight over (11:30-12:20).

## Extracted Concepts

- [Treat Memory as a Write–Manage–Read Control Loop, Not a Store](../concepts/treat-memory-as-a-write-manage-read-control-loop.md) - the mental model the talk asks for, decomposed into core, recall, and archival blocks.
- [Ablate the Recall Policy With a Ladder and an Oracle](../concepts/ablate-the-recall-policy-with-a-ladder-and-an-oracle.md) - hold the model fixed, vary only recall across no-recall → vector RAG → ranked ledger → oracle.
- [A Memory Harness Adds Only Cost When the Task Fits in Context](../concepts/a-memory-harness-adds-only-cost-when-the-task-fits-in-context.md) - the measured null result on the literature-review task, and the boundary it marks.
- [An Oracle Ceiling Separates Retrieval Failure From Use Failure](../concepts/an-oracle-ceiling-separates-retrieval-failure-from-use-failure.md) - the oracle misses the max because the right memory can still be ignored, misread, or confusing.
- [Rank a Decisions Ledger Instead of Retrieving Memories by Similarity](../concepts/rank-a-decisions-ledger-instead-of-retrieving-memories-by-similarity.md) - the winning rung: record per-turn decisions, then prioritize them, beating vector recall.
- [Do Not Gate Memory Use on the Agent's Own Judgment](../concepts/do-not-gate-memory-use-on-the-agents-own-judgment.md) - always-on ranked recall beat asking the model whether it needed memory.
- [Bad Recall Costs More Than No Recall](../concepts/bad-recall-costs-more-than-no-recall.md) - recall quality and token spend move together, not against each other.
- [Run Harness Ablations on Local Models to Own Every Step](../concepts/run-harness-ablations-on-local-models-to-own-every-step.md) - sovereignty over data, compute traces, and evals, priced against serial-only execution.

## Notes

- The problem statement is a symptom list for long-horizon degradation, not an abstraction: "when the model starts contradicting itself, or it has to redo the work because it forgot it did that task in the first place, or it starts to drift from your questions because it forgot them" (00:46-01:04). She names this context rot (01:32-01:36).
- Why now: METR's projections show "the trend is to solve longer and longer horizon tasks, and also that we're getting fewer and fewer model releases," converging "at some point later this year" on many more long-horizon tasks and fewer releases, which is what makes context rot "a priority" (01:07-01:36).
- Second-hand cost datapoint, cited from a two-day-old tweet by the CEO of Coinbase: the company "managed to reduce their AI spend while actually increasing the AI usage" by transitioning to many more local models plus better practices — "better routing, better caching, keeping the context clean," and better visibility into who is using AI for what kind of task (01:44-02:17). ## Provenance and Caveats

- The speaker is a research scientist at Sakana AI, and the talk closes on Sakana's sovereign-AI positioning plus a recruiting pitch. The sovereignty framing in the final two minutes is therefore aligned with her employer's direction; the ablation results are separable from it.
- This is single-author, unpublished work run on one machine, described in a 13-minute talk. No repository, paper, or results table is cited for the experiment itself.
- **No effect sizes are reported.** Every result in the talk is ordinal ("performed the best," "more frequently than without," "costs less"). The trust that survives is the *ordering* of conditions, not any magnitude, and there is no stated variance or significance test despite "multiple cells and lots of different seeds."
- The results were obtained with local, quantized models in the tens-of-billions-of-parameters class. Whether a ranked decisions ledger still beats vector recall when the base model is a frontier hosted model is untested here.
- The generalization claims (a second model, a second benchmark) are asserted without accompanying numbers or a per-benchmark breakdown.
- Both xbench and Spider V2 are named as existing benchmarks; neither the task subset nor the scoring method is described beyond "68 questions."
