## Summary

Aditya Khandelwal opens on the gap the wiki's coding-agent material has mostly worked around: "there's so much content about… how do you set up your own code base to like work well with agents… But it all seems to break the moment you like actually try to use it with your team in your actual production setup." His answer, from leading a team of about ten for a few months, is that agent adoption is a leadership problem rather than an IC one, and the argument is structural rather than motivational — the two changes that actually move the needle, restructuring a codebase for progressive disclosure and converging everyone onto a shared setup, are not changes one engineer can make: "if you want to change the way your code base is organized, you can't do that as an IC." The cost of leaving it to individuals is a specific trap he names: some engineers reach ten PRs a day and "look like… gods" next to the ones shipping one or two, and the one-to-two group inherits the review burden, so "not only can they not ship, they're going to actually see bad code and then curse the agents and hence not be able to get on to the let's ship 10 PRs." Uneven adoption is self-reinforcing, and it degrades the belief of exactly the people who have not adopted yet.

The diagnostic layer is the most immediately usable part. Adoption state is plotted on two axes — fear ("is it coming for my job?" versus "a really handy tool") and how much a person actually uses agents — and the trajectory he describes is not monotonic: fear started high with utilization low, early adopters pulled utilization up, mandates and "token maxing" raised usage without raising confidence, and then shipped slop *lowered* fear for the wrong reason ("I'm not really that scared cuz… it just ships slop. Like I'm going to still be needed"). Separately, five symptoms say a setup is broken regardless of what people report: engineers babysitting runs, sessions that need constant intervention, a constant slop factory, "insert whatever latest model there is being really dumb today" — "the model didn't change… the harness may have changed underneath" — and silent context burn where a not-especially-complicated task blows through 500k and hits auto-compaction.

The playbook is three moves plus a maintenance discipline. Do the basics right, which means finding the best ICs, extracting how they make the codebase work for them, and shipping those practices as a shared setup — the hard part being that this is "basically accepting that my setup is imperfect." Invest in *one* high-value skill; theirs, `ship it`, carries a change from code-done to PR-ready (open the PR, write the description, handle review comments and merge comments, fix CI failures) and "often… was running for over an hour," which scared people until they saw what it bought: "I don't need to constantly baby sit it. I can trust it." Then close the loop with issues and boards wired into the repo, CI/CD, agentic reviews, and a nightly code gardener that checks whether the code is "not organized correctly," where correct organization is codebase-specific. The maintenance discipline is that none of this converges: things change underneath, so a standing fraction of IC time goes to iterating on the shared setup, work that "is not going to lead to like meaningful PRs like up front, but it's useful and it's worth it."

The failure list is the talk's most honest section. Wiring agents into an issue tracker without wiring *which* agents may file produced 400–500 issues in a couple of weeks (the video description says roughly 4,500) — "so many like different agents all trying to create issues cuz they've not been wired correctly." Skeptics who saw the shared setup misbehave once were one step from reverting to babysitting, so complaints had to be routed back into the skill rather than answered. Long agent runtimes are an expectation problem, not a performance problem: "it's good if agents take too long. That means you can actually go off and do other things." Merge hell is accepted rather than solved. And prototype code is explicitly allowed to opt out of the codebase's rigorous standards, on the grounds that it will never ship. The Q&A supplies the two most checkable artifacts in the talk: a hard limit of about 100 lines in a `SKILL.md` ("cuz your skill is really a folder"), an `AGENTS.md`/`CLAUDE.md` that is "a thin index that can point through the right files," and first-prompt context burn as the test of whether progressive disclosure is real — 20–25K tokens get consumed anyway, and "if you're coming to like 40K, 50K, like something's wrong."

## Provenance and Caveats

- **One team, one report, no measurements.** Every claim comes from a single engineering leader describing a team of about ten over "the last few months." There is no before/after number anywhere in the talk: not PR throughput, not review latency, not incident count, not cost. The "10 PRs a day" versus "one to two" contrast is illustrative of a pattern, not a reported measurement of his team, and he says so about the playbook generally — "I'm not going to over emphasize on this is the exact way to do it, but this is like roughly how we did it and you can take from it what you choose."
- **The three numeric artifacts are stated as team conventions, not as tuned or validated values.** The ~100-line `SKILL.md` cap ("we've kind of set a hard limit"), the 20–25K first-prompt baseline ("I think like"), and the 40–50K failure threshold carry no derivation, no model, no harness, and no repo size. They are usable as starting points precisely because they are cheap to re-derive locally; they should not be treated as measured optima.
- **The context-burn symptom numbers need an interpretation to make sense.** "500k context… 750k [or a] million and hit auto compact" exceeds the window of the coding harnesses in common use at the time, so the figures read as cumulative tokens consumed across a session rather than window occupancy — auto-compaction implies a window smaller than the total. The talk does not name the harness, the model, or the task, so the threshold is directional only.
- **The issue-explosion count is ambiguous in the captions and inconsistent with the video description.** The transcript renders "we blew up to like 4 or 500 issues" (400–500); the channel-authored description says "roughly 4,500 open issues in a couple of weeks." No claim on any concept page depends on which is right, and both are recorded where the number is used.
- **The most attractive claim in the talk is also the least supported.** "It's good if agents take too long… the longer the agent like thought, the better its output" is offered as an expectation-setting move with no measurement behind it, and the reasoning-paradigm generalization ("a similar mindset for your entire code base and for your skills") is an analogy, not a result. It is worth holding against the wiki's own evidence that longer runs are not uniformly better.
- **The `ship it` skill is described by what it does, never by how it is built.** No prompt, no file layout, no failure rate, no cost per run, and no measure of how often its PRs needed human rework. The hour-long runtime is reported as a fact about the skill and as the thing that convinced people; nothing establishes that the output quality justified it.
- **Speaker affiliation is stated but the work is not attributed to a product.** Khandelwal is introduced as Amazon AGI Lab; the talk names no Amazon tooling, no vendor, and no product, and the codebase in question is never described. That removes the usual vendor-pitch bias and also removes any way to check the setting.
- **Half the material is organizational advice, which does not carry the same evidentiary weight as a technical result.** Psychological framing ("fear is real"), the skiing analogy, and "go full send" are heuristics from one manager's experience; the wiki records them as reported practice, not as validated method.
- **Merge conflicts are named and explicitly left unsolved** ("we just have to deal with it"), so nothing here should be read as a complete account of running many agents against one repo.
