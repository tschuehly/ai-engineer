## Summary

Simran Arora is a principal scientist at Together AI who leads the frontier performance research team, did her PhD in the Hazy Research lab with Chris Ré at Stanford, and is an incoming professor at Caltech (00:18-00:39). The talk has an unusual two-part structure: the first two thirds is a systems tutorial on why multi-GPU kernels are hard and what the small set of decisions inside one actually is, and the last third uses that hand-derived decision set as a grading rubric for frontier models. The order is deliberate and stated as a methodological position — "we think it's important to build our own fundamental understanding and to manually do the work to understand it rather than just throwing say an LLM at the problem" (14:38-14:50).

The framing claim is that the bottleneck has moved. "A few years ago, GPU utilization used to be limited by poor intra-GPU memory access and single GPU kernels. But with significant investment in better kernels like flash attention, memory efficient architectures like from DeepSeek, sparse attentions, mambas and so on, and better DSLs, we've sort of shifted the bottleneck to multi-GPU communication" (00:51-01:17). The supporting number is a hardware-generation comparison: from NVIDIA's A100 in 2020 to the B200 in 2024, "BF16 tensor core speeds improved by 7.2x, while intra node communication by just 3x and inter node communication by just 2x" (10:30-10:46). Compute outran the links by a factor of roughly two-and-a-half within a scale-up domain and roughly three-and-a-half across nodes, and the effect at the top is that "on many production distributed training and inference workloads, communication is increasingly consuming the majority of the runtime and yields low model FLOP utilization at scale" (06:45-07:04).

The claim is given a measurable form. Across the 87 problems in the group's benchmark, a naive baseline "representative of very popular libraries in machine learning stacking PyTorch with NCCL" falls "below 50% of their communication aware roofline bound" on the majority of problems (12:03-12:32). That is the number that makes the rest of the talk load-bearing: the headroom is not a few percent of a well-tuned system, it is more than half of an analytically computable bound on the default stack most teams run.

Arora surveys three existing routes and finds each limited in a different way. Off-the-shelf collective libraries — NCCL, and RCCL on AMD — receive heavy vendor investment but "they're not very flexible. So they're tuned for bulk transfers for large contiguous chunks of data transfers. And the design really breaks down when you care about peak performance, fine grain communication, and sort of non-trivial collectives that you want to fuse together" (11:33-11:53); frameworks layered on top (Megatron-LM, FlexFlow, NanoFlow) are "primarily orchestrating bulk collectives via NCCL and require synchronization before and after data transfers" (12:43-12:52). Compilers and DSLs such as Triton-distributed and TileLink face a portability problem: "it's very difficult to support the rapid pace of networking improvements within these frameworks," and their published result is that Triton-distributed, "originally tuned around 8 H800 GPUs, fails to adapt efficiently to other architectures like H100s" (13:07-13:27). Hand-tuned per-operator kernels reach peak performance but do not scale as an engineering practice: "some of these methods have been designed in one precision and it takes five or six months to scale it to another precision" (13:50-14:07).

The group's answer is ParallelKittens, "a small set of minimal primitives and patterns for multi-GPU kernels" that "usually add roughly a dozen lines of code over a single GPU kernel to insert these multi-GPU primitives," in production at Together AI and at Cursor (14:50-15:12, 20:41-21:13). Its design rests on two decisions the talk teaches explicitly. The first is the transfer mechanism, of which there are three with different resource costs: the per-GPU copy engine is host-initiated, good for large messages, and spends neither registers nor SMs; TMA (tensor memory accelerator) is device-initiated, "can saturate our NVLink bandwidth using relatively small message sizes," consumes very few registers and few processors, but "can't effectively take advantage of these in-network computations" the NVSwitch offers; register-level PTX instructions (`ld`/`st`/`red` with `multimem`) are the ones that can reach the in-network reductions (15:56-18:08). The second is the overlap schedule: intra-SM warp specialization, where warps inside one processor split between compute and communication, works when "the communication and computation pattern really need to like align and jive with one another. They need to use the same data as inputs"; inter-SM specialization, where whole processors are dedicated to compute, communication, or memory, is for when they do not align, when the kernel "would otherwise need to split across resources like the register file or shared memory in misaligned ways," or when "it's hard to maximize NVLink traversal with intra-SM overlapping" (18:18-19:47). The worked pair: GEMM + reduce-scatter favours intra-SM, GEMM + all-reduce favours inter-SM because it leverages the NVSwitch in-network reductions (19:50-20:22).

ParallelKernelBench is the second artifact. Each task hands the model "an unoptimized reference implementation written in PyTorch with torch.distributed NCCL operations and then a system topology that specifies the number of ranks and intra-node hardware configuration," and asks for "a performant CUDA kernel that uses unified virtual addressing" (22:07-22:33). Coverage is deliberate rather than opportunistic: because "a standard transformer layer can be parallelized across data, sequence, tensor, context, layer, pipeline and expert dimensions, and each composition induces a different communication pattern," the group built a taxonomy first and picked representative problems for each cell (22:37-23:12). The 87 problems are drawn from GitHub repositories, optimized library implementations, and existing multi-GPU DSL kernels, chosen so that "solving PKB would lead to net new useful production kernels rather than artificial or useless kernels" (23:23-23:49). Two metrics: pass@k, correctness after k attempts, and fast_1@k, solutions that are both correct and faster than the PyTorch+NCCL baseline (24:00-24:30).

The results are the point of the talk and they are negative in a specific way. Zero-shot, the best frontier model tried solves 28 of 87 problems, 22 of them faster than baseline (24:32-24:44). Drawing more samples raises correctness to 36 but "the fast one performance still plateaus out at roughly 31%. So we don't see much room from continuing to scale there as we increase the number of parallel generations" (24:45-25:11). GPT 5.5, the best model benchmarked, "drops off very quickly as the speed up threshold increases," with DeepSeek V4 Pro below it (25:50-26:24). The diagnosis is that the failures are not surface-level: "we found that there's deeper issues than CUDA syntax… if you do multiple sampling or have the model kind of look at its errors and correct them, it can often compile the kernels. But the models really struggle to reason through the tradeoffs that we talked about in the prior section — collective ordering, data partitioning, thinking about intra versus inter-SM scheduling, or deciding between the different transfer mechanisms," and "they often do not use things like the register transfer instructions or tensor memory acceleration when writing the kernels" (26:28-27:06). Where they do win, the wins are distributional: successes concentrate in "collective primitives, tensor parallel GEMMs and Ulysses style context parallelism… patterns that we see heavily represented on the internet rather than necessarily patterns that the model has used its reasoning abilities to think through" (25:24-25:47), and the speedups themselves come from one move — "eliminating NCCL staging overhead in favor of direct NVLink loads and stores" (25:12-25:22).

An agent harness helps and then stops helping. Taking "the mini-SWE-agent multi-turn harness and one of the best performing models, Gemini 3 Pro," with access to a local bash environment "to sort of mimic the standard Claude Code setup," lifted that model from 24 to 35 of 87 problems solved, with 26 over 1x speedup — "but we found that as we scaled the amount of time the performance plateaued, and we find that additional techniques would be required to continue seeing the scaling there" (27:07-27:56). The benchmark has nonetheless already produced kernels nobody had written: a NeMo vocab-parallel filtering kernel, a Hyena-architecture context-parallelism kernel, and an IoU-suppression kernel for the SAM 3 video segmentation model (28:23-28:51). The closing lesson is stated as a pair: "there aren't that many patterns that are involved in writing [multi-]GPU effective kernels… but unfortunately models do not currently understand how to reason through these trade-offs even when we provide them in context" (28:58-29:17).

## Provenance and Limits

- No absolute performance numbers are given for ParallelKittens. The kernels are said to achieve "state-of-the-art results compared to strong reference baselines" across data, sequence, and expert parallelism (21:13-21:26), with the chart shown too briefly to read and the reader referred to the paper and repo. The "roughly a dozen lines of code" figure is the only quantified claim about the abstraction itself.
- The "below 50% of the communication-aware roofline" result is measured against the group's own benchmark by the group that built both the benchmark and the alternative. The roofline model is not defined in the talk, and how the bound is computed per problem — which is what determines whether 50% is damning or ordinary — is deferred to the paper.
- The model evaluation is a snapshot with an unnamed winner in one place and named models in another. "The best of the frontier models we tried" scores 28 zero-shot (24:35) and GPT 5.5 is later identified as "the best available model that we benchmarked" (25:50-25:56); DeepSeek V4 Pro is the only other model shown on the speedup-threshold chart, and Gemini 3 Pro is called "one of the best performing models" in the agent experiment. The full model list, sample counts, and the k in pass@k are not stated.
- The agent-harness comparison changes two things at once. Gemini 3 Pro goes from 24 problems solved to 35, but the 28-problem zero-shot figure belongs to a different (best) model, so the harness gain is measured within Gemini 3 Pro and cannot be read as an improvement over the best zero-shot result. The talk does not say how much wall-clock or token budget the harness consumed relative to the parallel-sampling condition.
- "Additional techniques would be required to continue seeing the scaling" (27:50-27:56) is the talk's only statement about what to do next for the agent condition. No hypothesis about which technique is offered.
- The three named net-new kernels (28:23-28:51) are described as "signs of life" and are not benchmarked in the talk. Whether they beat their PyTorch+NCCL references, and by how much, is not reported.
- Vendor and topology scope: everything concrete is NVIDIA. AMD xGMI and the TPU 3D torus with optical wraparound links appear once, as evidence that networking stacks are diverse (07:22-07:45), and the group's own claim that networking is "really different across vendors still" (11:04-11:07) implies the ParallelKittens abstraction's cross-vendor portability is an open question rather than a demonstrated property, notwithstanding the related HipKittens and ThunderMittens ports for AMD and Apple silicon (06:24-06:32).
- Forward-looking hardware claims are dated and unattributed: 72-GPU scale-up domains "in the coming chips" and "Nvidia planning on a single system in 2027 with 576 GPUs" (09:02-09:15).
- The NVLink figure "up to 900 gigabytes of unidirectional bandwidth" (08:03-08:10) is stated without a time unit and without a generation; read it as 900 GB/s on a specific NVIDIA generation, which is how the surrounding sentence qualifies it.
- Caption artifacts resolved: "Siman" → Simran (00:18); "Chris Ray" → Chris Ré (00:23); "nickel"/"Rickle" → NCCL/RCCL throughout; "pietorch" → PyTorch; "cutless" → CUTLASS (13:47); "tileang" → TileLang (06:10); "thunder kittens"/"thunder mittens"/"hipkittens" → ThunderKittens/ThunderMittens/HipKittens (06:13-06:32); "tile link" → TileLink (13:04); "3D Taurus" → 3D torus (07:39); "XGMI" → xGMI (07:26); "LDST red multime" → PTX `ld`/`st`/`red` with `multimem` (16:35-16:39); "Ulyses" → Ulysses (25:33); "mini sui agent" → mini-SWE-agent (27:17); "clawed code" → Claude Code (27:10); "the colonel" → the kernel (19:26); "gem"/"map moles" → GEMM/matmuls; "interm"/"intram"/"intraSM" → inter-SM/intra-SM. Note that the speaker repeatedly says "intra-GPU" where "inter-GPU" is meant — at 05:04 ("in the intra-GPU regime we'll have NVLink"), 15:59 ("three main ways to do intra-GPU data transfers"), and 29:03 ("writing intra-GPU effective kernels") — all three describe transfers *between* GPUs; they are read as inter-GPU here.
- Unresolved: the hand-tuned-operator list at 13:38-13:45 is transcribed as "DPP, comet, ring attention, flux, flashdoe," which most plausibly reads as DeepEP, COMET, Ring Attention, FLUX, and FlashDMoE, but only Ring Attention is unambiguous from the audio and the list is recorded here with that uncertainty flagged.
