# The Rise of CaaS: Context-as-a-Service for Agentic AI — Omer Primor, Bright Data

Uploaded: 2026-08-14

## Summary

Primor names an emerging vendor category — context as a service (CaaS): companies that crawl, index, entity-resolve, and enrich a slice of the web into a knowledge graph an agent queries over MCP, CLI, or API, behaving "like vertical search engines… very, very, very good search engine for something very specific," and reachable in e-commerce, travel, finance, market research, HR, real estate, and GTM. He positions them between AI search (Perplexity, You.com, Tavily, and an "Exa"-or-"xAI" reference the captions garble) and the older data-as-a-service incumbents (ZoomInfo, which launched `gtm.ai` as an agent-facing second brand), and argues CaaS is an evolution of DaaS aimed at a different consumer: "when agents need them, it's different than people." The load-bearing premise underneath is decay — his team's chart puts social content stale "far less than a day" and news, finance, and retail mostly irrelevant 30 days out — so web context "is not a snapshot. It's not a one-time effort. It's not even a monthly effort."

The second half is a self-described test, "not a benchmark": enrich one company across 25 fields, run it 100 times against the event's sponsors, with a simple loop-in-a-loop agent and budget guardrails, comparing AI search products, CaaS vendors, plain Google via a SERP API, and the model's native search. Coverage converged — everything "did fairly well" — with one counterintuitive result he leads with: two CaaS vendors placed *lowest*, because "they know what they have about an entity. If I ask you the question that is beyond that, they will never have that data," where a search engine can keep exploring. He immediately qualifies his own metric: those vendors had many fields he did not ask for, so "how do we measure coverage when it relates to the specific job that we need to do rather than in general" is unresolved. Cost converged too, but from different components: the CaaS bill was the vendor invoice alone, while every search-based path also burned tokens to structure results into something retrievable — "it's the same output." The model's native search was "obscenely expensive," and a cheap low-coverage CaaS is read as a niche player already "carving that niche of the long tail" for small shops that do not need as much data.

The argument then turns from volume to frequency. A million is not a million companies in one context window; it is the same entity re-asked as time passes ("Was there any new news about them? Did somebody join? Did somebody leave?"), and "every repeated query costs the same as the first. Even if it brought back the exact same answers. Nothing changed? Pay up." What he observes teams doing about that is quietly cutting scope — check weekly instead of daily, take 10 results instead of all, skip the question — so "we already have the setup… but we're not extracting all of the value because we're starting to be conscious about cost." That is the frame: renting context rather than owning it. The build-it-yourself alternative skips general search entirely on the premise that "you use search when you don't know the source" — for company enrichment everyone knows the sources (LinkedIn companies, LinkedIn jobs, Crunchbase), the CaaS and ZoomInfo pull from them too, so "why are we doing that middleman thing?" Two scrapers built with an AI scraper builder plus merge heuristics produced a 100-company dataset with "zero AI cost involved. There's no tokens," at coverage that was "fairly well. Not amazing." Pricing the week of setup at $5,000, the crossover landed "just over 15,000 entities or queries," with the range left open ("maybe it's 30, maybe it's 100,000, maybe it's 10,000. Really depends on the use case") and the closing claim that the tipping point "is much lower than you think" because owned context compounds while rented decays.

## Notes

### Speaker, company, and scale framing (00:22-01:01)

- Omer Primor leads the product marketing team at Bright Data, joined about three years ago. (00:22-01:07)
- Company scale as stated: more than 20,000 teams, "including more than 70% of the world's biggest AI labs," extracting "well over 50 billion pages HTMLs every day, more than 20 petabytes of video, audio, and other media data." (00:38-00:57)
- The framing shift the talk is built on: "the web is no longer just a source of data. We can actually start looking at it as a source of context" — data is "only a step in the process for something bigger, for the actions I need to take, for the conclusions I need to draw." (02:06-02:30)

### Data decay makes context a process, not a snapshot (02:41-03:28)

- "The web is messy, it's unstructured, and most importantly, it changes all the time." (02:41-02:47)
- A decay chart produced by his own team, measuring "how long after a new page, a new piece of content goes live, it is no longer relevant": social media "far less than a day"; "also news, finance, retail, 30 days later, data that was collected mostly no longer relevant." No methodology for the chart is given in the talk. (02:47-03:09)
- The consequence he wants carried through the rest of the talk: "extracting context from the web or relying on the web is not a snapshot. It's not a one-time effort. It's not even a monthly effort… an ongoing process." (03:09-03:28)

### Search fragmented, then specialized (03:30-05:35)

- Three years ago "everything was Google… complete and total dominance up until 3 years ago for the past 20 or so years. Purely for humans." (03:30-03:56)
- Then search moved inside the chatbots ~1.5-2 years ago, "which already started blurring the line between humans and agents because now the same bots also have the same web search available… the same LLMs have the same access through API for the bots." (03:58-04:20)
- The AI-search cohort as he names it: Perplexity, You.com, Tavily, "and a bunch of others" — "purely built in indexing the web especially for agents, not even looking at the humans involved anymore." (04:28-04:48)
- Two hyperscaler entries as evidence the space is crowding: Amazon "developed their own index and have started allowing the ability to retrieve data from the web, to retrieve context for agents on AgentCore" (announced "just last week" relative to the talk), and Microsoft two weeks before that "repackaged it and launched it again as part of… their suite for agentic development and orchestration." (04:58-05:35)

### What search cannot answer (05:42-06:32)

- The limit is change over time, not coverage: "I can search for… the cost of a certain pair of sneakers this morning… I cannot really search for how has that price changed over the last 6 months, what discounts it had." (05:48-06:05)
- Same shape for hiring: "I can search for what open job positions we have at Bright Data… But I can't see how that was a chart and how that change over time and how the head count of the company change over time." (06:05-06:17)
- The kicker is that this is not a data-availability problem: "all of that information existed on the web simply back then." The gap is that nobody retained it, so "there's much more context in the web than what web search allows us to extract." (06:17-06:31)

### The CaaS category (06:32-09:05)

- Naming: "We like to call them internally CaaS, context as a service, because that's what they do. They allow agents to tap into them, MCP, CLI, just pure or good old API, and actually start extracting data… to retrieve data so they can reason over for whatever knowledge work they are responsible for." (06:32-06:53)
- Verticals named: e-commerce, travel, finance, market research, HR, real estate; later also retail, GTM, and sales intelligence. (06:53-07:04, 07:37-07:45)
- What separates them from a crawler: "they don't only just discover the web… crawling… searching… accessing, extracting the data, and indexing it, they take it a step further. They actually develop knowledge graphs to start structuring all of the entities and to dedupe them. And they start enriching them with a lot of different sources, so they actually start merging all of that data." (07:06-07:27)
- The shape: "they kind of behave like vertical search engines… very, very, very good search engine for something very specific." (07:27-07:37)
- Disclosure worth keeping attached to the vertical list: "what all of these companies, by the way, have in common, they're all part of Bright Data's startup program," followed by a credits pitch he closes with "that's enough self-promotion." (07:45-08:06)
- Incumbents are moving too: "good old data as a service, you see ZoomInfo," which "launched that thing at the top, it's called gtm.ai… as a secondary brand for ZoomInfo, that is catering specifically for the need of agents… Straight from Claude Code, straight from Codex, or any other agent. They understand the gap." (08:18-08:55)
- The lineage claim, with its qualification: "it's fine to think of CaaS as an evolution of DaaS, and it is in a way, but it's catering for a very specific need, as much as the AI search engines are different than Google. When agents need them, it's different than people." (08:55-09:07)

### Web context engineering as a routing problem (09:10-09:52)

- Having two paths to complete knowledge work "as an agent" is what makes this a design decision: "we can start thinking about this in terms of web context engineering… how do I optimize for the specific task, and… how do I optimize this for breeds of task?" (09:10-09:34)
- Framed for the audience's job: "If I'm an AI engineer, I need to serve different teams. They may have different needs. It's very tempting to throw AI search at all of them, but maybe that's not optimal. Maybe I need a combination of both. Maybe I can start seeing all sorts of cost efficiencies emerge from that." (09:37-09:52)

### The test design, and its stated limits (09:53-11:00)

- "This is not a benchmark. You won't see any something concrete that I can say with great confidence other than the actual research that we did." (09:56-10:04)
- Task: "take a company, an entity, and try to enrich it across 25 different fields. Some of them are very easy… the company domain, the name, the headquarters, but some are more challenging… things about hiring and people." (10:10-10:27)
- Harness: "a simple agent, a loop in a loop that… uses 4.0 as the harness and it starts to go over field by field, go out, search for it, or retrieve it from the CaaS. Do it again and again and again until it completes," with "some guardrails, you know, like budget and stuff just to keep it fair." (10:27-10:47)
- Sample: "we ran it 100 times on all of the sponsors of today's event." (10:52-11:00)

### Coverage: convergence, and one counterintuitive result (11:00-12:25)

- Headline: "there's pretty good convergence. They all did fairly well." Search was "consistent performance," one of the major CaaS providers "also very well." (11:00-11:15)
- The comparison set includes an Unlocker + SERP path — "SERP is good old Google. We basically did the same thing just with Google and it performed pretty well in extracting that information" — and "Native," the model's own built-in search. (11:15-11:32)
- The surprise: "I was originally surprised about the two CaaS solutions at the bottom. It was counterintuitive. I expected CaaS to dominate this thing… you had one job, right? To map all these companies." (11:32-11:43)
- The mechanism, stated plainly: "they are limited in the sense that they know what they have about an entity. If I ask you the question that is beyond that, they will never have that data. Not like a search engine can go out and continue searching and exploring it. If they didn't collect data about the recent job hiring, it will never be there." (11:49-12:06)
- He then discounts his own metric: "I'm sure at the same time that they have a lot of other advantages that we simply didn't ask for. A lot of other fields that they didn't have that aren't represented. So… it creates some complexities when… how do we measure coverage when it relates to the specific job that we need to do rather than in general." (12:06-12:25)

### Cost: same total, different composition (12:25-13:38)

- "Most of the search and the CaaS and even using Google… converged to pretty much the same cost only different." (12:32-12:42)
- The composition difference is the point: "The CaaS was just about the service itself, what you paid the vendor. All of the other search solutions, you also needed a lot of token burn… to actually structure that data so you can actually act on it and use it as something retrievable. So it's the same output." (12:42-12:57)
- Two outliers: "Native, obscenely expensive," and one unnamed CaaS as "the bar farther[st] most expensive in the industry. I will not name and shame them." (12:57-13:07)
- The cheap CaaS is read as market structure, not failure: the cheapest vendor is also the lowest-coverage one, and "we're seeing even within this industry niche players that have… lower quality data but much cheaper. They're already carving that niche of the long tail… of small shops or small usage that don't want to pay as much and don't need as much data." (13:07-13:38)
- Note on evidence: the per-record dollar figures live on slides and are not spoken, so the only numbers this source supports are the $5,000 setup price and the >15,000-entity crossover below. (13:55-14:02)

### Frequency as the cost multiplier (13:55-15:14)

- The unit is clarified before the multiplier: "This is the cost, not for the whole hundred. This is the cost per one. For one record." (13:55-14:02)
- "What happens with a million?" — and immediately, the reinterpretation: "a million records will not fit in a context… You can think about a million in terms of the frequency." (14:05-14:16)
- The worked case is private-equity due diligence: "I revisit these companies all the time. I ask more questions about them as the time goes by. Was there any new news about them? Was there anything that changed? Did somebody join? Did somebody leave? Do they have new hires? I keep on asking the same thing… It's not just about the number of companies. It's the frequency in which I'm asking it." (14:16-14:38)
- "Frequency is the cost killer when we talk about these." (14:38-14:41)
- The mechanism: "Every repeated query costs the same as the first. Even if it brought back the exact same answers. Nothing changed? Pay up." Noise and false positives are paid for too, and token cost "doesn't shrink well over time. There's always some volume element in terms of the cost, but it's not the same as flatlining." (14:48-15:14)

### Cutting corners (15:15-15:48)

- What teams do under that pressure: "I won't research this company every day. I'll look at it once a week or once a month. I won't ask that question now. I don't want all the results. I'll only take 10 results, 20 results." (15:19-15:31)
- Why it matters: "we already have the setup. We have what we need to do the knowledge work, but at the same time, we're not extracting all of the value because we're starting to be conscious about cost." (15:31-15:40)
- The name for it: "Basically, we're renting context. We're not owning the context that we use. That is a very important distinction." (15:40-15:48)

### Building it: skip the middleman when the source is known (15:52-17:33)

- The naive version he opens with is the one he does *not* do: "What if we take all of that web data ourselves and stick it in some vector database and try and see what comes out of it. So, I asked my AI engineer to do exactly that." Caveat repeated immediately: "this is a test. This is not a benchmark or a full-blown operation. This is a day's work at best." (15:55-16:23)
- The routing rule: "You use search when you don't know the source, but when we're talking about company enrichment, we all know these sources. We all know where that data comes from. The CaaS also bring it from them, ZoomInfo bring it from that. It's the same thing over and over again. Why not just go straight to the source? Why are we doing that middleman thing?" (16:37-16:53)
- The sources named: "LinkedIn companies, LinkedIn jobs, Crunchbase. Right there, we have scrapers for those, you just tap in and… pay as you go." (16:53-17:00)
- Build tooling: "We built two dedicated scrapers. We have a new AI tool called Scraper Studio. It's basically lets you build a scraper for any website in less than 5 minutes, all powered by AI, and then it also have a self-healing function… if the website changes, it fixes itself and keeps on going." (17:00-17:16)
- Assembly: "Merge it all into one entities. Basic heuristics of those conflict, choose that over that. And eventually, we have a data set of these 100 companies." (17:16-17:28)
- The claim that makes the cost chart move: "Zero AI cost involved. There's no tokens." (17:28-17:33)

### The built pipeline's results and caveats (17:34-18:25)

- Coverage: "Fairly well. Not amazing. Not the best that we saw here, but stacking up pretty well. And again, this is just a day's experiment. Probably not even that much." (17:34-17:45)
- Explicit scope limit: "very specific task, very limited context, very limited situation. You know, tread lightly and… proceed with caution when it comes to conclusions." (17:45-17:58)
- A qualification of the knowledge-graph step other vendors perform: "We think about knowledge graphs, we think about entities, but if you think about, for example, LinkedIn, the data is already structured in form of entities. There's an entity for a company, there's an entity for a job, and they're connected between them. Sometimes the ontology is already there." He immediately grants "this is not the most complicated of scenarios." (18:08-18:29)

### The tipping point (18:32-19:22)

- The fairness disclaimer comes first: "yes, it took time to set up. So, it's not really fair to compare apples to apples when it comes to the cost because these are out of the box. You can just tap into the API. That one required some setup. Let's say it's a week. Let's price it at $5,000 just to give us a perspective." (18:32-18:48)
- The result: "everything to the left of that dot… in this case, it was just over 15,000 entities or queries… So, it made sense to do it at this point." (19:02-19:10)
- And its range: "Maybe it's not 15, maybe it's 30, maybe it's 100,000, maybe it's 10,000. Really depends on the use case. But there is a typical point in which it actually makes sense to do it yourself." (19:10-19:22)

### What ownership buys beyond the invoice (19:23-20:47)

- The routing condition: rented services "are very good in the sense that you can just plug and play. But if your knowledge work needs are persistent and consistent and to a certain degree may even continue escalating and growing, then this is perhaps a direction to start considering." (19:25-19:46)
- The shape of the cost curve: everything before retrieval "is upfront investment, and the most important thing that whatever retrieval happens later on from the agents… is free. Not really free, but you get what I mean… There's no added cost." (19:46-20:05)
- The behavioral payoff, which he rates highest: "I can just ask that question over and over again. I did not like the first answer, I'll ask it again. I'll ask it a hundred times until I get what I need. I have no more fear, no more cutting corners, which is maybe the most important thing." (20:05-20:16)
- Two advantages he flags but does not develop: "this is also custom business logic. I can connect it with my own data. There's all sorts of other advantages of owning it… we'll keep it to the imagination." (20:16-20:26)
- Scale back to the opening: "Remember, we asked about a million, not about 15,000. This compounds. This compounds greatly… Remember, the web keeps changing. We saw the staleness of the data and how the data decays." (20:28-20:47)

### Close (20:50-21:57)

- Rented context is not dismissed: "AI search, CaaS, they can get you very far when what you need is ad hoc, when what you need is always changing, when sometimes you look at different things, even the mix and match of them. For certain task use this, for certain task use that." (20:50-21:05)
- He grants his own test is unoptimized: "There's a lot of ways to optimize it, just like any other context engineering… use lighter models and use other stuff. There's a lot of great stuff to be done." (21:05-21:14)
- "But eventually, the frequency will come and bite you in the ass when it comes to cost… And there's a fair chance that that tipping point is much lower than you think." (21:14-21:25)
- Final line: "owned context compounds while rented decays… if it's a one-time question, use AI search, it will be amazing. When you need to do it over and over again… you're missing out on potential compounding effect, and you are losing out." (21:32-21:57)

### Provenance caveat

- The speaker leads product marketing at Bright Data, and the "build it yourself" path in the test is assembled from his employer's paid products (Scraper Studio, per-site scrapers, Web Unlocker, SERP API). The conclusion that owning beats renting past a crossover therefore favors his company's pricing model, and the competing CaaS vendors are anonymized while his own tooling is named. What survives that bias is the structural argument — decay makes context recurring, repeated queries do not amortize, and a crossover exists — plus his own hedges: "this is not a benchmark," "a day's work at best," "tread lightly," "not really fair to compare apples to apples," and the admission that his coverage metric was shaped by which 25 fields he chose to ask for.
