# Always-on agents run production without the on-call tax — Justin Smith, Resolve AI

Source: [Always-on agents run production without the on-call tax — Justin Smith, Resolve AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vSx5IULvBns)
Uploaded: 2026-08-09
Transcript: `raw/20260809_vSx5IULvBns/vSx5IULvBns.en-orig.vtt`

## Summary

Justin Smith (founding product engineer at Resolve AI; ~15 years in monitoring and observability, an architect on Splunk's observability suite, ten years at VMware before that) argues that the bottleneck AI moved was never the one people optimized. His premise is a survey figure — "70% of the time from an engineer is actually not focused just on writing code. It's actually spent on actually running the code that is actually shipped into production" — followed by the list that fills it: maintaining platforms, scaling infrastructure, debugging incidents, being on call, shipping hot fixes, dealing with alerts, updating runbooks and operating procedures, restoring services, handling escalations, and answering other teams' questions. "Really coding was never the the big bottleneck." Coding agents made that half worse by raising the volume of change flowing into production — "AI is creating a lot more issues in production… it's not clear we have the right sort of structures in place to deal with the amount of kind of changes that are coming through" — which is the case for using AI *inside* production rather than only in the editor. The talk's sharpest distinction is between the two halves of a task: "a task is just execution and the context to understand how to actually execute the task," and the second half is the scarce one, because "it's one thing to go check a dashboard. It's another thing to say that metric smells off. And the execution is can load the dashboard. It's the production context that's going to say, this feels wrong. And I don't know if I can even explain why it feels wrong." What the context buys is a prioritization decision, not a retrieval: "you really need that production context that tells you is this important or not important." Beneath both sits the piece he names as the real work and describes least — a learning system, because models are "incredibly capable" but "truly understanding your environment and the way that your services interact and where the hotspots are" is what has to "grow as your system evolves… your system is evolving faster and faster." Resolve's product spans an on-call agent (triage and full root-cause investigation per alert), incident agents (a single page escalating into a cross-team incident, keeping everyone in sync), and the **background agents** the talk focuses on: the long tail of work "when there's not a fire brewing." That long tail is defined by the absence of a ceremony — "on-call you've got a page that goes off. You know somebody's going to receive that. Incidents you create a bridge, you invite people in. That's great. Um, but there's just a long tail of other things that we are accountable for that doesn't have sort of a thing that's going to show up in your sort of job description" — and its examples are concrete: watching deploys go out healthy, a morning report or incident digest so "we're all on the same page," "hey that P99 drift kind of came back. Is somebody looking at that or not?" (explicitly not a page, "because we're not going to alert on everything"), a capacity report against a quarterly company goal, and a recurring health check so you are "not waiting for a customer to come complain first." Background agents are defined by three questions. **When they run**: a schedule (a weekly on-call handover every Thursday, with the agent preparing "what are the kind of interesting trends from the last week that the next on-caller needs to sort of understand"), an event stream (CI/CD deployment events, Slack messages), or a plain message. **How they run**: "Always runs. It's in the cloud. So if you close your laptop, it's okay. Runs inside of a sandbox, so it has kind of a file system underneath it," which "allows it to sort of self-organize a lot of its work," with a shared knowledge and memory system so it can "reflect on that task" and apply what it learned from one task to a different one. **How they know what to do**: a task system, skills pulled in from systems you already have ("you can connect those"), and integrations. Four workloads are offered as proven primitives. *Deployment monitoring* is the flagship, and it is argued from two named gaps in CI/CD rather than against it: the standard checks "are good. They're good baselines, but it's not exhaustive based on the type of changes that are going in," and "often times you have change systems that you're not piping through a CICD system like a feature flag or maybe some infra changes that might happen which maybe don't get any monitoring at all. And you're sort of just trusting that an alert might fire and an on-caller will wake up and say, 'Who changed what?'" The posture is explicit: "our goal is not to sit here and say, 'We're going to replace an entire CI/CD pipeline. You've spent time organizing that.' But this can sort of patch a lot of parts of your system that may not be as robust as they should be." In the demo, a GitHub release tag posted in Slack is classified by the agent itself ("That's a release. Is that a release? Yes, that is a release. Let me go watch that"), which then reads the changes, works out "what telemetry might help us evaluate whether those changes are… putting the system in an abnormal state," and builds "a sort of customized plan that it's going to check just for this specific release" — here, checkout replacing the currency service, so checkout latency and error rates, then the Kafka pipeline "cuz that's sort of involved. This is the sort of causal chain I want to make sure is healthy." Timing is derived, not configured: "none of this is hard-coded in. It's not like, oh, let's just wait for 15 minutes and then try this again," because the agent "could decide, I want to wait for another hour cuz this type of issue might only hit every so often," or "come back in 3 days and say, is this deploy still kind of healthy? Are we seeing the change in the effect that I expected to see out of this?" — and the same shape covers "feature flags, infra changes, sort of any sort of eventing system." *Scheduled health and anomaly checks* include a time-boxed variant that retires itself: worried about a third-party service, "set an agent to kind of watch that maybe for the next week just to make sure everything is kind of stable and then that agent can sort of stop [its] job." *Operational reports and handoffs* cover the ceremonial summarization, and the demo shows the standing job being edited conversationally — he replies in the report's own Slack thread, "this is too verbose. Verbose. Make it shorter," and "the agent is able to update its task underneath… so that the next time it fires, it's not going to be as verbose." Creating a task works the same way: "Hey, I want to do a new recurring health summary for my team" leads the agent to explore the environment and "come back and ask me a couple questions about what I want to see, what kind of reports do I want, how verbose do I want it," then stand up an initial version to test before sharing it with the team. The fourth, and the one he plainly enjoys most, is *first responder to engineering questions*: an agent passively watching critical Slack channels with no at-mention required — "I'm not having to know that resolve exists" — instructed "if you see something that you think you have an answer for that somebody's digging into, go ahead and respond. Otherwise don't," which "determine[s] whether it has enough sort of confidence to answer the question or not" and is shown declining to answer one message. Because the agents also have Slack DM access, an emergent behavior appeared: the agent DMs him, "I think I know the answer to this, but I'm not sure. Can you confirm this for me before I respond back?" The cost being removed is interruption rather than difficulty — "it's not hard for me to go answer questions, but it's disrupting me and if I don't go answer it, they won't get an answer for a while." On surfaces, the product has a UI where you "inspect all the different tasks… view their reports, view previous runs" and "see all the work that the agent has done underneath," but the stated design principle is that the primary surface is Slack or MS Teams, "the surface area being where you live," with "ideally a lot of your interaction… in the places that you're already kind of doing work." On composition, "if you have an agent harness, if you're building your own, everything that I showed is accessible through kind of MCP servers," so Resolve can be grafted into an existing harness as a production-context source, and "bring your own skills along for the ride. Um, don't go duplicate a bunch of stuff." The closing takeaway restates the thesis as a cost claim: "cost of operational work… it's not just in the task execution, it's in the environment complexity. That's where the biggest issue is going to happen."

## Extracted Concepts

- [Separate Execution From the Production Context That Judges It](../concepts/separate-execution-from-the-production-context-that-judges-it.md) - loading the dashboard and deciding the metric smells off are different capabilities, and only the second decides whether anything is important.
- [Derive the Post-Deploy Check Plan From What Actually Changed](../concepts/derive-the-post-deploy-check-plan-from-what-changed.md) - read the diff, pick the telemetry that would expose trouble for *that* change, follow the causal chain, and let the agent choose when to look again.
- [Watch the Change Paths That Bypass Your Deployment Pipeline](../concepts/watch-the-change-paths-that-bypass-your-deployment-pipeline.md) - feature flags and infra changes ship with no verification at all, and the current detector is an alert waking someone who asks "who changed what?"
- [Give Unowned Operational Work a Trigger](../concepts/give-unowned-operational-work-a-trigger.md) - the long tail is work you are accountable for that nothing fires for, so a schedule, an event stream, or a message has to stand in for the missing ceremony.
- [Answer Unaddressed Questions Behind a Confidence Gate](../concepts/answer-unaddressed-questions-behind-a-confidence-gate.md) - an agent that was not at-mentioned decides both whether it can help and whether to say so, defaulting to silence and escalating privately.
- [Amend a Recurring Agent Task by Replying to Its Output](../concepts/amend-a-recurring-agent-task-by-replying-to-its-output.md) - "this is too verbose. Make it shorter" in the report's own thread edits the standing job, not just this run.

### Provenance and Limits

- A vendor talk by a founding product engineer at Resolve AI, in a ~25-minute slot ending at 24:38 with no Q&A. The expo booth is promoted three times.
- **Nothing in the talk is measured.** There is no count of deploys watched, no catch rate or false-positive rate for the per-release check plans, no baseline comparison against the CI/CD checks the agent is said to complement, no cost figure for an agent that may keep re-checking for three days, and no adoption or retention data from the customers referenced. The strongest evidence offered is "these are things we've seen be very successful inside of Resolve, but also with a number of our customers."
- **The demo is explicitly synthetic** ("sort of fake environment"), and two of its three segments did not complete on stage: the conversational amendment ("no time to watch this actually go, but this works") and a final section skipped entirely for time. What the audience saw was the posted instruction and the prior output, not the round trip.
- **The 70% premise is uncited.** "This was a survey study done" is the entire attribution — no publisher, sample size, population, or year — and it is the number the whole argument rests on.
- **The learning system is named as the hard part and never described.** Knowledge, memory, reflection, cross-task transfer, and keeping up with an environment that "is evolving faster and faster" are all asserted; no storage model, retrieval mechanism, staleness or eviction policy, conflict handling, or evaluation of the learned knowledge appears anywhere.
- **The confidence gate has no threshold, calibration, or error rate.** "Determine whether it has enough sort of confidence to answer the question or not" is the mechanism as described. One declined message is shown; no false-answer or false-silence rate is given, and there is no discussion of what happens when a confidently wrong answer lands in a channel where colleagues will act on it.
- **No security or data-boundary discussion at all**, despite agents that passively read critical Slack channels, hold Slack DM access, and take instructions from arbitrary messages and GitHub tags posted by anyone.
