## Summary

Angie Jones recounts leading the transformation of Block's 3,500-person engineering org into an agentic/autonomous one. The core diagnosis is that adoption is not impact: within months ~90% of engineers were using Goose (Block's internal coding agent, the MCP client reference implementation) and Claude Code, yet features weren't reaching customers any faster, because usage was stuck at IDE-level autocomplete and chat. Her two durable organizational tools are (1) an agentic-engineering **maturity model** (Stage 0–5) that ranks each engineer by their *delegation/orchestration relationship* with agents rather than by usage, and (2) a leverage strategy grounded in the 1-9-90 rule: rather than upskill all 3,500 engineers, hand-pick a ~1% **AI champions** cohort from the most critical repos and have them make the shared repos **AI-ready** (context/rules files, slash commands and skills, an AI code reviewer, PR attribution) so agent delegation becomes native for everyone. She then walks the org up the ladder — hands-off delegation from where requirements already live (Jira/Linear/GitHub/Slack), multi-agent parallelism (a Codex auto-fix review loop, isolated cloud workspaces), the Builder Bot orchestrator, and a machine-readable **company world model** over 25,000 repos — reaching Stage 5 where anyone could @Build-A-Bot in Slack to ship a fix. The talk closes on a caveat rather than a victory lap: the autonomy push was followed by layoffs, and she leaves the ethical/strategic question of where this is heading deliberately open.

## Notes

- Provenance: Block built Goose "even before the LLM supported tool calling," worked with Anthropic as design partners on the initial MCP release, and Goose became the MCP-client reference implementation; ~90% of engineers used Goose/Claude Code within a couple of months. (00:18-00:52)
- Adoption ≠ impact: the CEO believed engineering wasn't using AI because they weren't shipping faster; the metrics and token bills proved usage, but "features certainly weren't making it to our customers any faster." Three enablement phases: experimentation, adoption, impact — 90% usage cleared experimentation but stayed IDE-bound (questions, boilerplate). (00:57-01:51)
- She led AI enablement for all 12,000 Block employees (every function) in H1 2025; the CTO then tasked her with building an agentic engineering org, with "no playbook" — other companies' blogs said they were "making it up as [they] went along." (01:51-02:28)
- Definition of the target: engineers "leverage AI agents as their primary means of producing engineering outcomes" — treating agents as core workflow members: decomposing problems, delegating work, reviewing/verifying — as their default way of operating. (02:28-03:03)
- Maturity model measures how engineers "think and delegate and orchestrate"; she had a form of it in Q3, and Steve Yegge's "Gas Town" article helped her reorganize it. Stage 0 no AI; Stage 1 autocomplete, never agent mode; Stage 2 chatting with agents but no PRs; Stage 3 delegate tasks + review output; Stage 4 multiple agents in parallel; Stage 5 ("final boss") delegate complete tasks with shippable results and no human hand-holding. Bulk of engineers sat at Stage 1–2; goal was Stage 5. (03:03-04:18)
- Rollout headwinds: highly experimental / no playbook; this-week's best practice outdated by next week's model (AI fatigue); people turned off by top-down "AI or die" pressure. (04:18-04:52)
- 1-9-90 rule ("1990 rule"): in digital communities ~1% create, 9% interact, 90% consume; maps to AI adoption. "If my AI strategy depends on every individual leveling themselves up, I'm never going to see that broad impact" — so form the 1% power users from the most critical teams. (04:52-05:40)
- AI champions program: hand-picked ~50 engineers (not volunteers, "strategic about the selection") who could dedicate ≥30% of their time and wouldn't give up on non-deterministic AI when it failed out of the box; drawn from every corner of Block (Square, Cash App, Afterpay, Tidal; front-end, back-end, mobile, data, infra) and repos of all shapes (nasty legacy monorepos, small services, mobile apps) to pressure-test patterns across engineering realities and see what actually scales. (05:40-06:50, 07:50-08:20)
- Around June 2025 models could write a feature but the code often didn't conform to team conventions, so engineers didn't trust agents enough to delegate. The leverage point: make repos AI-ready — embed AI directly into the repos so agents perform better *and* the whole team benefits, not just the 1%, because repos are the central reference for every contributor. (07:20-07:50)
- Standard AI-ready components (customized per team): context files (`AGENTS.md` / `CLAUDE.md`) for repo guidance; rules files as guardrails; repeatable workflows (slash commands, later agent skills); an enabled AI code reviewer with instructions on what matters; AI attribution on PRs. Monorepos used inheritance — shared contexts/rules at the root, more specific ones layered at the service level; web ≠ mobile (Android and iOS needed different approaches). (08:20-09:39)
- Bottom-up beats mandate: each champion figured out what worked for their repo, teams with similar shapes/sizes converged naturally on the same tools/patterns, and engineers "loved this… instead of pushing a top-down mandate." (08:44-09:20)
- Hands-off delegation from where requirements already live — issue trackers (Jira/Linear), GitHub issues, and informally in Slack; champions implemented all three so delegation "felt native to how people already work" and required no new skill. Slack demo: `@goose` diagnosed a bug, pulled the files, proposed three fix options, implemented the chosen one, and returned a PR link — full discussion→diagnosis→issue→alignment→fix cycle in ~5 minutes; assigning Linear/Jira/GitHub tickets to an agent made agents "part of the sprint" (one team ran out of tickets twice). (10:00-12:20)
- Results ~3 months into the champions program: AI-authored code up 69%, reported time savings up 37%, automated PRs up 21×. (12:39-12:52)
- Stage 4 multi-agent parallelism surfaced two bottlenecks. PR review: engineers 3–4× their PR output and PRs stalled waiting on review (already a pre-AI problem, "not a totally solved problem"). Fix: enable Codex AI review on all repos + an **auto-fix loop** — when Codex flags issues, another agent auto-fixes and commits to the PR so humans review PRs already in good shape. (13:00-14:20)
- Second parallelism bottleneck: agents "bumping into each other" and laptops running out of memory / CPU choking. Fix: dedicated cloud workspaces, each agent in its own isolated environment, runnable in parallel from anywhere. (14:20-14:50)
- Builder Bot: a small group (many champions) built an in-house orchestrator to coordinate the 4–5 agents each engineer was now running, needed to reach an autonomous org. (14:50-15:10)
- Company world model: a machine-readable view of every service and how they connect, built over the entirety of Block's ~25,000-repo codebase, so orchestrators and delegated agents pull context as needed, explore parts of the system in parallel, each build their own understanding, then reconvene for a plan spanning multiple codebases — useful for offerings spanning multiple products. (15:10-16:00)
- Reached Stage 5: agents produce shippable results without hand-holding, and not just for engineers — anyone at the company could @Build-A-Bot in Slack to fix a bug or implement a feature without even needing GitHub. (16:00-16:40)
- Caveat / open ending: "This felt like a dream. Until it became a nightmare." The autonomy push was followed by layoffs, prompting her open questions — was enabling employees to do "the most incredible work of their careers" what led to their dismissal, and "are we sure that [this] is where we want to end up?" (16:40-17:36)
