## Summary

Dan Feng describes how Maven Clinic — a digital health platform for maternity, fertility, parenting, and menopause — moved from a traditional technology company to an AI-native one over about two years, and what broke along the way. His organizing claim is that the cost structure of software inverted: implementation used to be the expensive step that justified weeks of upfront requirements, but building now takes minutes and *arguing* is what is expensive. Planning changed to match — a one-year view kept only as direction (assume the models will do what you need by then), real commitment at two to four weeks, short argue-with-me documents instead of long PRDs, and the three-to-six-month plan treated as close to unplannable because nobody knows what models will do by then. The rest of the talk is the operational fallout. Code review had to be redesigned rather than scaled when engineers went from hundreds to thousands of lines a day: engineers self-certify which PRs need a second reader while staying accountable, PRs are capped near 500 lines, big features are stacked into several, and the rubber stamp is named as the worst outcome because it buys false confidence rather than none. On reliability he refuses a single bar and sorts failures into tolerable and not — a scheduling action that fails one in a thousand is survivable because the user clicks again, while a reimbursement claim has zero tolerance in either direction, so several models read the same receipt and it proceeds only if they agree. Release confidence comes from running each integration test many times against a high pass rate rather than once, and post-launch from rubric-based automated evaluation plus a dedicated human group that spot-checks conversations and raises coverage to about 20% for new features.

## Extracted Concepts

- [Commit at Sprint Scale When Implementation Is Cheap](../concepts/commit-at-sprint-scale-when-implementation-is-cheap.md) - the cost moved from building to arguing, so the one-year plan is direction, the two-to-four-week plan is commitment, and the mid-term plan is the casualty.
- [Sort Failures by Whether the User Can Retry](../concepts/sort-failures-by-whether-the-user-can-retry.md) - scheduling failures are absorbed by a second click; reimbursement amounts are not, so those get cross-model agreement and a human off-ramp.
- [Earn Release Confidence From Repeated Runs and Post-Launch Sampling](../concepts/earn-release-confidence-from-repeated-runs-and-post-launch-sampling.md) - passing a nondeterministic system once proves very little, so tests run many times at a pass-rate bar and rubric scoring plus human spot-checks continue after launch.
- [Route Each Change to the Proof It Needs](../concepts/route-each-change-to-the-proof-it-needs.md) - Maven's implementation of per-change routing puts the call in the author's hands: self-certify whether a PR needs a reviewer, stay accountable either way.
- [AI Output Speed Can Overwhelm Review Capacity](../concepts/ai-output-speed-can-overwhelm-review-capacity.md) - a first-hand account of the 10x volume shift and the specific policies (500-line cap, stacked PRs, anti-rubber-stamp norm) a team used to absorb it.

## Notes

- Framing exercise opening the talk: hands up if you are already an AI-native company (a few), hands up if you have thought about it but not started (a few), "that means most of us is in between," 00:28-00:44.
- Company context: Maven Clinic is described as the largest digital health platform, focused on women and their families — maternity, fertility, parenting, menopause. The AI journey started two years back and produced **Maven Intelligence**, "an orchestration layer across all our product to enable AI for everybody in this company and for our clients," 00:47-01:15.
- The adoption argument is competitive rather than technical: "adopting it is not an optional. Even you choose not to, your competitors will do." The quote he repeats: "tractors aren't to replace farmers, but the farmers who can operate the tractor will replace the ones who cannot," 01:18-01:43.
- No playbook: "I don't think there's a one single definition what it means by AI native. And more importantly, there's no predefined playbook you can just follow and bingo, you become AI native," 01:46-01:59.
- Their three parts of AI-native. (1) *Internal use wherever possible* — daily summaries, meeting management, creating a Jira task; the test is "anything you need to do today manually, you should think about to say can use AI to do it," and specifically "whenever you want to ask other people to do something for you, you should be saying with yourself I can use AI to do it." Leaders including sales now solve those tasks themselves instead of delegating. (2) *AI in the product*, for two goals: improve user experience and reduce operational cost — an AI chatbot is 24/7, instant, "way better and cheaper compared to human agents." (3) *Culture, process, and the way we work*, which he calls the more important one, 01:59-03:20.
- Adoption segmentation into three groups. Early adopters need almost nothing — "enable the tools for them and encourage them to share what they learn with the company." The majority is where the effort goes: shared AI infrastructure, easy-to-use tools, seamless adoption, and consistent feedback intake. Reluctant adopters get their concerns understood, "but more important, we should be crystal clear with them where the company is heading to," 03:22-04:35.
- Meet engineers on whichever tool they moved to: "last year, most of folks and Maven they are using Cursor. This year, a lot of them switch to Claude Code. For us, we need to support the both. We need to meet where they are," 04:04-04:16.
- Why senior engineers stopped delegating implementation: the old loop was a senior engineer sensing the problem, designing a solution, and handing implementation to others for parallelism. Now "they already figure out how to solve the problem. They just use AI to solve it instantly. Delegating to other people means more overheads and less efficient." The consequence for new hires is that they must be able to solve problems independently — "we cannot afford other people delegate implementation task for them," 04:43-05:41.
- What they now hire for: genuine interest in AI and continuous learning because "the domain is moving so fast"; product understanding, because "the boundaries between PM and engineers is getting blur" and an engineer who understands the product contributes "way more than a traditional engineer who only focus on software side"; and deep system understanding plus the ability to handle complicated, ambiguous problems — "this is where AI [falls] off," 05:41-06:35.
- Incentives follow: performance reviews now ask "what do you have done for AI side?" and explicitly reward people who "leverage AI to multiple their impact," 06:35-06:55.
- The cost inversion, stated directly: the old process spent weeks or months fleshing out business requirements and finalizing design before implementation "because implementation can be really expensive. If we didn't get the other part right in the beginning, it can be very costly to change it later." His counter is that "we never get the sense and right in the beginning anyway for any of big projects," and now "with AI, building is super fast. It's probably couple minutes you can get it done. Argument is really expensive one," 06:58-07:47.
- The three planning horizons. One year: "you can assume AI models can do anything you want in one year… really dream big," but "it should only serve as inspiration, inspiring, and directional." Two to four weeks: "what we really need to focus on"; the ask to PMs and designers is "tell me what I need to deliver in this sprint," the engineer releases by sprint end or sooner, and the PM uses that time to flesh out the next batch. Reversal is cheap and expected: "if at the end of this sprint, they say, 'No, what we decided two weeks ago is wrong.' It's totally okay. We can switch the gear, get it fixed quickly," 07:47-08:45.
- Document length follows: "we prefer people not write pages or pages of PRD or TDD anymore. We prefer them to write just a short one or two pages. That one is really serve as communication, so we can iterate on it," 08:45-09:01.
- The named casualty: "the really awkward part is mid-term goals. Those like a three months, six months. It's very hard to plan these days. The reason is I don't know what AI models will be capable in three months. There may be multiple releases already." He acknowledges the organizational cost — quarterly or six-month planning is what people in the domain are used to — "but it's our job to get used to the new AI era and learn how to work it efficiently," 09:01-09:41.
- Their coding-tool adoption ladder: start with the lowest-risk tasks — "writing unit tests, documentation. Those things are very easy to verify and the risk is super low." Doing that built confidence and let them "construct our own rules, skills, and build our barriers." Then push to the whole engineering team for all tasks, and when someone declines, "it's the time we really want to learn say, 'Why you don't do it?'" Current state: AI coding tools do essentially all implementation and "engineers really focus on reviewing, architecturing, and evaluation," 09:44-10:57.
- The review volume shift: a good engineer "used to… write hundreds of lines code every day. These days, they can easily write like thousands. If we keep do the code review as we used to do, we won't be able to keep up," 10:59-11:22.
- On AI review tools: "we also tried the multiple like AI coding review tools. It helps a little bit, but we don't feel comfortable 100% rely on them yet. We still found those feedbacks from our engineers are very, very valuable" — while still betting on them: "we should keep working on our AI code review tools because we are thinking that's the future," 11:22-12:57.
- The three review policies. *Self-certification with retained accountability*: "we allow engineers to self-identify whether they still need code review. If they think this PR is simple enough, I feel very confident, I don't need anybody to take a look… We let them merge, but we still hold them accountable." *Size cap*: "each PR shouldn't have more than 500 lines of code because nobody can do a meaningful code review with the ones has like thousands of lines code." *Stacked PRs*: a big feature is broken into multiple PRs so "people review the PRs and they can keep working on it," 11:43-12:32.
- The named failure mode: "one thing we really want to avoid is a rubber stamp… people submit code review, you cannot really do anything to it. You just say blindly approve it. This is the worst case… because that's just give us false confidence. We think we reviewed it, it's good, and we release it," 12:32-12:57.
- Stated end goal, explicitly not reached: automate the whole lifecycle end to end from design through implementation to release, and have AI tools "monitor the live traffic and be able to catch the issue early and automatically fix it. That's what we are still working on, and we're not there yet," 12:57-13:29.
- The reliability frame: traditional software "does what we implement there, no more, no less," but for GenAI solutions "hallucination is there. We cannot ignore it," and "completely eliminating them can be very costly. Sometimes is not necessary, either." So the holistic solution starts "even from the get beginning" by identifying "which failures is acceptable, which ones are not acceptable," 13:29-14:11.
- The tolerable case: appointment scheduling. "If we fail well to 1,000, probably it's okay. I'm not saying it's a good experience, but the users really can just click the button again, we will reschedule for them," 14:11-14:28.
- The zero-tolerance case: reimbursement claims. "If people ask of $200, we issue them 50 or they ask 50, we give them 200. Each case will cause a escalation right away." The extra step: "when we receive their receipt, we will use different models to review the same receipt. We only move forward if the results from different models agree with each other." The off-ramp on disagreement: "if we really have trouble to figure it out which one is right… it's okay to tell the customer, say, 'Hey, we have trouble to process your stuff. Do you want us to get you connect to a human agent?'" — which he calls an acceptable solution, 14:28-15:17.
- The release gate: "we have like hundreds of integration tests, for which pretty much covered all the use cases we know, and we are keep adding to the integration test suite." Passing once is no longer sufficient "because the LLM can do different things. So, for each test case, we run it to many times. We consistently requires the high pass rate, like for example, 90% for all the time," 15:17-15:50.
- Post-launch quality: an automated evaluation system "carefully evaluate[s] each conversation" against "a lot of rubrics, what we think is good, what is bad," producing scores that get reviewed. On top of that, "a dedicated group, their job is mainly review those conversations. We will spot check our conversations." The dual purpose is explicit — the human pass says "whether we need to come back to improve our systems, or our rubrics is too strict or too loose, and we need consistently improve it." For new feature launches, "not only spot check probably not enough, we really want to review like say 20%, and we can do it." The point of the whole process: "we are really confident whenever we release something, although we know hallucination is there," 15:50-16:50.
