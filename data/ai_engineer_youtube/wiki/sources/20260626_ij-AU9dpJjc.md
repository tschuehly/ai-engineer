# Stop Writing Tone Instructions. Layer Them.

Source: [Stop Writing Tone Instructions. Layer Them. - Isadora Martin-Dye, Isadora & Co](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ij-AU9dpJjc)
Uploaded: 2026-06-26
Transcript: `raw/20260626_ij-AU9dpJjc/ij-AU9dpJjc.en-orig.vtt`

## Summary

Isadora Martin-Dye runs a 225-year-old Virginia wedding venue and built the AI agents behind it — a couples-facing assistant ("In Bloom"), a version for other venues, a personal AI companion, and Thread Light, a utility for families of missing people. Her thesis: a brand voice that survives real users "isn't an instruction you write once — it's an architecture." A single system prompt asked to "write in our brand's voice" works on the happy path but breaks on "turn 21," the first case the examples never anticipated, because you are asking one prompt to do four incompatible jobs. Her fix is a four-layer prompt stack composed in a fixed order through one assembly function: (1) **immutable identity** — hard constraints the brand structurally cannot violate, overridable by nothing below; (2) **situational mode** — real-time conditions (who the user is, what they're going through) injected *before* generation; (3) **example-anchored voice** — the tone guide / phrase list where most teams start and stop; (4) a **post-generation veto** — the only deterministic, non-prompt layer, which reads the actual output and can reject it. The load-bearing distinction is that the first three layers are *instructions* ("probabilistic… asking nicely and hoping") while the fourth is *permission* ("deterministic… checking, and you are sure"), because "a prompt will eventually lose." She also surfaces a multi-tenant failure (silently-defaulted brand identity leaked one venue's persona into others) with the principle that identity must fail loud, not fall back.

## Notes

- Framing: "I'm not programming a robot, I'm managing a brilliant intern with an incredibly high IQ and a terrible EQ" — photographic memory for the first-morning briefing, no instinct for reading the room; "they will say something technically perfect and socially catastrophic in the same confident sentence." Managing an intern means you build structure and check the work before it goes out the door. (00:23-00:56)
- The happy path vs turn 21: examples work "for every question you have anticipated," but turn 21 is the first the example didn't cover; the model does "something technically correct that your brand would just never say… not wrong exactly, but it's not you." Matters most where "the voice is the product" — a luxury hotel, high-end real estate, a wedding venue, where "a single wrong sentence can cost more than a refund." "Write in our brand's voice" is a comment that says "just make it work" — it "does nothing that the model wasn't already going to try and do." The real problem: "you're asking one prompt to do four completely different jobs." (01:04-02:12)
- Pre-architecture state: "24 different system prompts scattered across the code base," personas named Sage, Less, Venue — "every surface had its own idea as to who it was." Replaced with one canonical assembly / single entry point that every surface composes its system prompt through. (03:22-03:54)
- **Order is load-bearing** — "hard rules first, task to last." Google Maps analogy: the destination is fixed, but the right route depends on constraints (a license, no reversing down a motorway) → real-time conditions → journey preferences → a check before you pull away; "you don't check for roadworks after you've already taken the wrong turn." (03:54-04:47)
- **Layer 1 — immutable identity:** "what the brand structurally cannot say," constraints not preferences, "cannot be overridden by venue config, voice profile, or user instruction." Example: AI disclosure — "Every AI in Bloom discloses that it is AI in its very first response. Not if asked, but before they ask" (a product decision, not a legal one: a couple who knows from the start trusts it more than one who "finds out that it's AI on turn seven"). Example: the physical-presence boundary — "You are software. You do not have a body," so "I'd love to show you around" is forbidden but "the team would love to host you for a tour" is allowed. When a user realizes they performed a relationship "with someone who was never there," trust "doesn't just dip, it inverts." (04:53-07:00)
- Cross-product proof of Layer 1: Thread Light (families of missing people) — same architecture, different stakes. Layer 1 forbids words like "confirmed, identified, matched, proven, linked, solved." The word "match" said to someone who's spent years not knowing where their child is "is not just a tone violation… it is the single most damaging thing that a product could ever do," and the model reaches for it "because statistically it is the natural word." (07:08-08:23)
- **Layer 2 — situational mode:** real-time signals "built into the prompt before it runs," the layer most teams never build. Two conditions: (a) *who you're talking to* — the same agent talks to couples and briefs venue staff/coordinators ("like a colleague, not a customer"); same identity and voice, "but the route changes based on who's in the car"; a coordinator asking a forecast should get "I can't forecast that confidently, here is the trend," which a couple should never be refused like. (b) *what they're going through* — soft-context notes used "for tone, empathy, and what not to say. Never quote them verbatim"; a couple in grief hears gentleness "not a quote about loss," a couple with a sick parent gets patience and slack on timing. The assembler "deliberately renders this before the numbers" — tone set from soft context first, then numeric constraints; reversing the order "makes the prose feel mechanically slotted because the model is already committed to the numeric framing." Heat-map example: if the system knows a client "has a mom in chemo for 3 weeks," a drop in engagement "reads as a family under strain, not a cold lead or a problematic couple to chase." (08:23-11:44)
- **Layer 3 — example-anchored voice:** the tone guide, phrase list, dials — "where most teams start and stop" because it feels like a brand problem, not a technical one (marketing owns it, the engineer wires it in, job done). The "induction pack" of good examples handed on day one. It teaches "what good looks like on the happy path," but on turn 21 the phrase list "has nothing to say — it's a catch-all error… examples are not the right tool for guarantees, they were never designed to be." In Bloom, a voice-training exercise let coordinators write actual AI responses and the AI "learns from the edits that the coordinators have made." (11:44-13:20)
- **Layer 4 — post-generation veto:** "the only layer that actually reads what came out" and "the only part of the whole architecture that isn't a prompt." Two types. *Soft flag* — an "honesty inspector" that flags a response that slipped a rail (e.g. "did it actually answer the question? It should not hedge"); the cost is asymmetric — a false positive means "someone double-checked a response," a false negative "remains a hallucinated number or a privacy violation that ships to a client." *Hard reject* — a "numbers guard" for confidently-stated figures that were never given; "the guard rejects the output anyway." (13:16-16:15)
- The booked-date failure that created Layer 4: the AI kept offering dates that did not exist. A couple writes in excited about a Saturday in October; Layer 3 does its job and says something warm about holding the date — "except that date is booked. The model didn't know that. It was never given the calendar." A "warm, confident voice offering something that isn't real is worse than a cold one," because the couple now believes they have a date — "you've given them a disappointment with a 48-hour delay on it." The lesson: "usually" is fine when the cost of being wrong is a slightly off-brand sentence, "not fine when the model is confidently inventing a fact that a real person is about to act on. A date, a price, a policy, a promise." (13:36-16:01)
- **Prevention is the prompt; the veto is the check — you need both** "because the prompt will eventually lose, and you don't want to find out that it lost by reading a couple's reply." The veto checks specifics against reality: "a date the model offered that isn't in the allow list doesn't ship." (16:01-16:34)
- **Multi-tenant seam:** "one architecture with completely different voices," the seam is "calling one function." Layer 1 is identical for every tenant; Layers 2-3 are per-venue. Failure: brand-identity fields were not defaulted-safe — "letting them default silently caused every venue to ship as sage@hawthornemanner.com… a critical white label leak" (in Google Maps terms, "every driver was getting the same saved home address"). Fix as a principle: "in a multi-tenant system, identity must never have a default. A missing brand identity is a crash, not a fallback. It must fail loud" — the quiet failure is a venue speaking in a stranger's voice and trust eroding before anyone notices. (16:15-17:52)
- Closing distinction: "The first three layers are all instructions… things you tell the model, and the model usually listens. Usually, they are a request. The fourth layer is not a request. It is reading what actually came out and decides whether to allow it to leave your business." "Instructions are probabilistic. Permission is deterministic. Everything before layer four is prompt engineering — you're asking nicely and hoping. Layer four is systems engineering — you're checking, and you are sure." "Users aren't testing your brand voice, they are trusting it… the four-layer pattern isn't a framework I'm trying to sell. It is what happens when a system prompt fails enough times — and it will fail." (17:52-19:25)
- Things she'd build differently: the veto "should be its own service, not wired individually into each surface" (a per-surface manual wire-in is "a checklist waiting to be forgotten"; a shared default gate means "you can't opt out by accident"); a central "condition resolver" instead of each surface deciding which conditions to load; and she notes the veto is deterministic pattern-matching ("it either matches or it doesn't… never gets it wrong on the patterns it covers") where "a small classifier might catch more edge cases… but it is a classifier that is probabilistic. Sometimes it gets it wrong. And right now I'm choosing determinism over coverage" — "a real trade-off and not an obvious win." (19:25-20:53)
