# Product Strategy

## Overview

AI engineering does not remove product strategy work; it makes weak product judgment easier to turn into shipped artifacts. Product success still depends on earning attention, guiding users to a clear first proof, and retaining advanced users through durable abstractions. For AI products and developer tools, that means generated copy, quick prototypes, and fast coding agents are not substitutes for taste, positioning, onboarding discipline, architecture-level product primitives, or deliberate AI coworker form factors.

The funnel is a useful operating model. Top-of-funnel work should create something worth sharing, not merely announce another feature. Onboarding should identify one aha moment and remove every step that delays it. Retention should avoid the false choice between simple and capable: build powerful primitives first, assemble the beginner path from them, and expose deeper controls as users grow.

AI-native product strategy also includes deciding where model behavior belongs in the experience. Invisible background work, ambient surfacing, inline artifact manipulation, conversational build flows, adaptive websites, and personalized creative pipelines carry different trust, interruption, and control costs. Teams should prototype those choices with runnable model probes because static screens cannot reveal whether the model is fast, accurate, aligned, awkward, or surprisingly capable in the proposed surface. Prototype energy still needs product restraint: a pile of model capabilities is not a product unless it is anchored to one reliable user outcome, earns trust in the first attempted job, exposes model edges honestly, and makes AI delight feel user-steered rather than like a trick.

The product-judgment work also has a concrete front end in an AI system's requirements phase. Before any architecture is chosen, the durable move is to quantify a *solution-agnostic* business problem — user-specific, current state, quantified pain, and no prescribed agent/multi-agent shape — and to classify the role of AI along three axes (critical vs complementary, reactive vs proactive, level of autonomy) before writing 1–2 SMART success metrics tied to that problem. Naming who the user is, what they do today, and by how much it hurts keeps the eventual system anchored to a reliable outcome rather than a pile of model capabilities, and treating denial-type or high-consequence steps as human-mandatory caps autonomy at the level the domain actually allows. This is the product-strategy foundation that the four-phase [requirements-first design framework](../concepts/design-ai-systems-requirements-first-with-a-four-phase-framework.md) builds on.

AI intensity gives product teams another product-shape lens: compare how much valuable output the system can produce for each human steering action. A higher ratio is not automatically better; it is useful only when the output is valuable, reviewable, and not annoying to control.

AI startup strategy should also assume that raw model capability is a moving commodity surface. The product moat is less likely to be a generic prompt box on one model and more likely to be a thick workflow wrapper: domain knowledge, automatic context collection, model orchestration, output presentation, review affordances, and execution speed that keeps improving as models get cheaper and stronger.

For vertical AI, that workflow wrapper needs a deliberate last-mile domain-context system. Strong general models can reach a useful baseline, but specialized products still need customer-specific workflow interpretation, expert-labeled failure modes, and domain-expert PM prioritization to close the final performance gap. The product surface should make expert judgment operational: specialists need tools to review evidence, label failures, and propose deployable domain knowledge rather than leaving those insights in meetings or spreadsheets.

Healthcare revenue cycle work illustrates a vertical AI wedge where the workflow is less flashy than clinical diagnosis but highly measurable. A product can create value by reducing payer-provider friction, preventing avoidable denials, assembling appeal evidence, and improving expert throughput, provided it can access the longitudinal operational data needed to connect upstream errors to downstream payment outcomes.

Tax explanation is another high-liability vertical workflow where the product goal is confidence, not autonomous calculation. A useful AI layer can make deductions, credits, withholding, and refund outcomes understandable, but exact numbers should remain grounded in deterministic tax engines and product design should account for model latency, fallback paths, peak-season traffic, and legal/privacy controls.

AI product management adds an operating role or mindset around that uncertainty. Someone must connect viability, feasibility, desirability, data, evals, probabilistic behavior, guardrails, and human-in-the-loop design before a team overcommits to a product surface. The product sequence often changes: validate the core AI capability first when feasibility is uncertain, use cheap AI-assisted implementation to explore many feature variants with real users, then remove the extras and focus the workflow. AI-speed prototyping does not remove the old requirement to ship something concrete to someone specific: the useful shape is a continuously viable product slice, tested with named users through high-bandwidth feedback before major product or platform contracts harden. Platform strategy needs the same caution; a custom AI client can remain useful while tools and data move behind MCP or other portable interfaces as enterprise defaults change, but shipped APIs and data structures become high-inertia customer contracts.

Demos are one of the fastest ways to search that uncertain product space. A demo does not need production completeness to be useful; it can reveal a latent model capability, make a new interaction mode imaginable, or show that an old computing idea has become newly practical. The risk is treating demos as proof of reliability. Their product value is strongest when they are used as capability probes and shared artifacts, then followed by the usual work of narrowing, evaluating, and hardening the product.

Agent-native products add a requirements problem: broad model capability plus free-form user surfaces can make exhaustive feature specs impossible. Product teams should define the affordances an agent may use, build runnable probes to feel the interaction, and let product evals express what behavior is acceptable often enough to ship. Customer trust then depends less on pretending to know every emergent behavior upfront and more on honest co-discovery, visible thresholds, and fast feedback loops.

AI product strategy should also accept that the full behavior surface cannot be specified upfront. As models and tool integrations become more capable, products create more undefined behavior, formats, intents, and edge cases. The practical strategy is to ship narrow useful experiences, watch production signals, identify user intents, and harden the workflows where the expected lift, negative sentiment, volume, and strategic relevance justify the work.

Agent coordination platforms add a product-strategy constraint: agent functionality should often be embedded into the system where users already manage work instead of shipped as a separate copilot shell. Linear's approach favors small hidden AI assists, product-intelligence relationships, and first-class agent teammates inside issues, projects, Slack handoffs, and customer-feedback workflows, while rejecting visible AI surfaces that do not meet the product's quality bar.

Commerce gives the same distribution problem a consumer-facing shape. As shopping starts inside AI chat products, merchants still need surfaces they can own, brand, measure, and update. A practical transition path is not to assume websites disappear, but to make product data, brand systems, generated UI components, and checkout flows transportable into chat clients while preserving the merchant's representation and control.

AI-engineer founders also need to translate technical taste into a market narrative when raising early capital. At pre-seed or seed, investors may not require revenue or even a finished product, but they still need to understand why this team has a timely, non-obvious insight and how the first wedge can become a company rather than a feature. Design partners, customer interviews, ICP hypotheses, pricing hypotheses, and product-velocity plans help convert early conviction into a credible path from prototype to scale.

AI team design is also a product-strategy decision. The right team depends on the company's bottleneck: domain/data gaps, weak technology capability, missing customer empathy, product integration, monetization, reliability, observability, or specialist model performance all imply different staffing. Generalist AI engineers often fit early transformation work because they can build, use available platforms, understand tradeoffs, and talk to customers, while narrow specialists are most valuable after the baseline is strong and the remaining constraint is specialized performance.

At the field level, the deployment frontier is shifting from horizontal tooling toward specific high-value verticals as general model capability commoditizes. The practical reading is that coding is the first domain to mature and adjacent high-stakes knowledge work follows: finance is treated as the next vertical "closest to takeoff," with healthcare, law, deployed engineering (FDE), agentic commerce, and GTM close behind. This is the top-down complement to the bottom-up last-mile-domain-context view: choosing the right vertical sets the ceiling, while domain context, expert-labeled failures, and measurable workflow outcomes determine whether a product reaches it.

AI-first SaaS strategy adds a roadmap-level version of that judgment. The product question should move from where to add intelligence to an existing feature toward which cross-workflow customer value becomes possible when AI can combine signals across teams and roles. That future cannot replace current customer commitments outright, so planning needs ritualized discovery, MVPs built for learning, and enough permission to change direction when model capability, customer expectation, or evidence shifts. Product teams should also avoid turning AI into a separate sideshow: isolated AI strategy, quarantined risk, technology-first demos, and top-down feature pushes tend to create bolt-on chatbots or summarizers. A stronger pattern integrates AI risk, evals, customer problems, and bottom-up product discovery into the normal roadmap, then matures through embedded assists, contextual copilots, and only later cross-product agentic experiences.

When the core capability is itself a managed, already-scalable API, the strategic center of gravity shifts again. A viral "talk to a statue" app built in two hours from one Cursor prompt — gluing OpenAI deep research, the ElevenLabs Voice Design API, and a managed agents platform — illustrates that stitching such APIs collapses the prototype-to-production gap (a single app cannot dent the providers' API volume, and remaining surface like auth can be one-shot with Supabase), so the durable work and the differentiation move to the integration glue and the *story about the glue* rather than to solving a hard technical problem the APIs already solved. This sharpens the existing point that distribution, not implementation, is the scarce skill once tools are easy: the launch that mattered was the vibe-coding repost (50k → 1.5M impressions), and the practical craft is concrete (publish the prompt, front-load a hook for a 6–12s median view time, add captions and generated music, edit on a phone). The same example carries the standing caveat that managed-API content is a demo shortcut — production needs curator-designed narratives grounded in first-party data, not ad-hoc web research.

When the product *is* the agent platform, two more strategy choices come to the fore. OpenAI's Codex keynote makes a single-stack dogfooding bet: build your own product on the exact primitives you sell — same responses API, open-source harness, `AGENTS.md`, and app server — instead of a privileged internal path plus a simplified external one, because "at every layer we use the thing we give to you" turns every external fork and edge-finding into a learning signal, and a reference implementation beats leaving developers to reverse-engineer you. The companion surface bet is chat-first with a hands-on collaborative UI: since the model beats the user on the average task, the empowering shape is a single ask-anywhere entity plus a place to inspect/steer/shape (not a CLI, which is "mostly chat," nor an IDE, where "the order is wrong"), designed to preserve the human's "feeling of mastery" rather than to automate engineers away. Both extend the primitives-before-features stance: reject the simple-versus-capable tradeoff by doing more upfront design.

A closing-keynote provocation reframes ambition-sizing itself as the strategic variable. Theo Browne argues that each model jump (framed as tool-call → long-running-task → orchestration eras) doesn't just make the same work faster — it shifts what scope a solo builder or small team can own down one tier, so a former startup becomes a side project and a whole class of former services collapses to a "markdown file on a cron" piped to an agent. The strategic response is to *rescope ambition up* to whatever now feels oversized ("if your idea doesn't feel stupid, it's not big enough"), because the gain is wasted on work the previous model already handled. He also reframes "bigger" as "wider": AI collapses the cost of *breadth*, so small teams can span an incumbent's surface area (build a database platform in a day or two of prompting) if they architect for user-driven extensibility — cover enough of the spectrum to onboard most users, then let users build the vertical features you don't support (Slack accidentally became "the platform people run their agents in" because it was the right *shape*, not the most complete product). This is the demand-side complement to build-for-a-multimodel-market and workflow-wrapper moats: the same commoditizing capability that erodes a thin prompt-box also lowers the cost of owning wide, extensible product surface.

Victoria Melnikova (Evil Martians) pushes that distribution point earlier and harder for developer tools and AI startups: since "building software has never been easier," the binding constraint has moved from product to distribution, and AI-generated slop in inboxes and feeds makes cutting through the noise the real work. Her operating model is a diagnosis plus a foundation plus tactics. The diagnosis is a PMF Compass with two axes, product signal versus revenue: signal above revenue means invest in distribution, revenue above signal means invest in the product, and roughly nine of ten early-stage devtools sit in the distribution-deficit quadrant because technical founders avoid marketing. The foundation is six GTM-hygiene steps (validate a value proposition with paying customers, understand your "shelf space" in a developer's life, become the authority on the problem, map ecosystem friends and foes, and distribute early — even before the product ships). The tactics are concrete and mostly SF-centric: come to SF as a network game, get loud with banners that both signal existence and buy subconscious mind share (a credibility proxy when there are no funding milestones to point at), run events up to your own user conference, and use fun or "crazy" stunts and fail stories to earn memorable attention. Underneath all of it is a moat claim that complements "personal brand is the product-strategy lever" from the other direction: the durable advantage is the founder — "it's not your model, it's not your team, it's not your funding, it's you" — because founder-led sales builds trust ("people want to see a real human behind the product") and AI "cannot really replace you, but it can amplify your signal." The practical instruction is to be unapologetically yourself and turn your quirks into a "genius zone," since the market pendulum is swinging back toward craft that AI can amplify but not fake.

Sanja Grbic (Automattic) adds an organizational-transformation lever for a large, established company rather than a 0→1 startup: velocity from AI looks entirely different when the codebase is 5–20 years old and there are thousands of people, so the strategic move is to engineer the conditions for behavior change, not just to buy tools. Automattic's "Radical Speed Month" paused the roadmap for 30 days and gave ~500 people (a third of the ~1,400-person company) full autonomy in two-person pairs to ship something real, producing ~794 projects — but the sprint only worked on top of scaffolding that predated it: a role-specific two-week immersive AI enablement course, a systems/operations team that made security, processes, and docs good enough that a non-engineer can spin up a working dev environment, and an internal knowledge MCP server ("Context") exposing decades of documented company knowledge to AI tools. The transferable claim is that changing tools or process in a large org "requires shifting the human behavior behind them," so the levers are access to tools, enablers and champions, space to experiment, and enough agency for people to break their habits. This is the enterprise complement to "integrate AI strategy into core product strategy" and "ritualized discovery": the roadmap pause is the discovery ritual, but its value is the agency and the removed negotiation/handover cost, not the calendar.

Angel Ortmann Lee (Duolingo) makes the interaction surface itself a strategic lever, not just a UX finish. Her thesis is that "every AI system trains its users, the question is whether you're doing it deliberately": the interaction loop is a compounding flywheel (model → interaction → human behavior → data → evals → model), so how you frame a decision determines both how good the human's judgment is *and* what data you can learn from. Two product-design principles generalize. First, **match friction to the stakes** — high-consequence flows should add deliberate review gates so a human never becomes a rubber stamp, while low-oversight delightful surfaces should be frictionless; the same AI output can be surfaced as an overwhelming wall of text or as a hover-for-detail markup UI, and the surfacing is the product decision. Second, treat every interaction as an already-existing label and design it to capture honest, structured signals (splitting conflated yes/no questions, capturing the diff a user actually made, collecting explicit feedback at the right touch points) so the product improves itself. The strategic point echoes "nail deterministic UX before probabilistic delight" and "earn trust before delight," but sharpens it: the highest-leverage fix for an underperforming AI product is often the interaction design, not a better model or more human oversight.

Iris ten Teije (Differ) reframes the product's *shape* rather than its interaction: because [the frozen-artifact pipeline was a cost constraint, not a law](../concepts/frozen-artifact-pipeline-is-a-cost-constraint-not-a-law.md), "one version of your software for everyone" was never chosen — it was the only option when producing a per-user version meant forking and hand-maintaining a code base. Demand for software that "fits you" is decades old (the forward-deployed engineer / professional-services industry, developers rebuilding dotfiles by hand, Excel as "millions of people that all build their own programs"), but was gated by cost and forced into pre-declared buckets by feature flags, segmentation, and A/B testing. As change-production cost collapses, a horizontal SaaS can [deploy a canonical stem plus bounded per-user divergences](../concepts/deploy-a-canonical-stem-plus-bounded-per-user-divergences.md) and let each user's software adapt live — the system observes what they skip and reprioritizes, and they can proactively request changes "within the boundaries the developer sets" and "within the spirit of the software" without going back to the developer — so a product can "address a much wider number of customer personas without increasing your R&D spend." This is the runtime-personalization counterpart to Theo Browne's "think wider, compete on breadth via user extensibility": both let a small team span more of an incumbent's persona surface, but where extensibility invites users to *build* features, adaptive software has the running product *diverge* per user against developer-set guardrails. It also carries a product caveat that maps onto this topic's eval discipline — a change can be "perfectly correct and working" yet undesirable, so the hard product question becomes proving an *uplift* on the metric that matters (retention, churn, support tickets), not just shipping the change.

Veronica Hylak (Hey AI) narrows the same distribution problem down to the single act of *explaining what the product does*: shipping is easy now, but "you only have one shot to make your mark" — about an elevator ride — and "great tech that nobody understands dies quietly," so the binding gap is being able to answer "why does this matter to an average user?" Her fix is a communication discipline, not a virality tactic — [explain the product as a wound → click → transformation story](../concepts/explain-ai-products-as-a-wound-click-transformation-story.md): open on the user's pain rather than what you built (in ~20 seconds identify the wound, say "we fix that," show how), make it instantly [click with a picturable mental image instead of a category label](../concepts/give-a-concrete-mental-image-instead-of-a-category-label.md) (pass the 17-year-old test; tie to a viral story people already understand; "Devin, the AI software engineer" / "a smoke alarm for AI behavior" as front doors, saving the technical definition to close the sale), then prove value with an explicit before/after ("30 minutes digging through docs → one question, answer in 10 seconds with sources"). This is the user-facing complement to "AI does not replace shareable product marketing" (which earns attention) and "fundraising pitches should explain why-you/why-now" (the investor narrative): its unit is the first sentence a buyer hears, and its failure mode is the reflexive "we're building an agentic orchestration platform for enterprise X" that makes people feel nothing. It also sharpens "anchor on one reliable user outcome" — the wound and the transformation are that one outcome, stated as pain-before and relief-after.

Pietro Zullo (Manufact) points at where that distribution is increasingly won: [inside the agent clients themselves via MCP stores and dynamic discovery](../concepts/distribute-mcp-apps-through-stores-and-dynamic-discovery.md). The client stores (ChatGPT apps, Claude connectors, Cursor directory) opened for self-serve submission and replaced shared JSON configs with one-click install URLs, but the sharper strategic point is *dynamic discovery* — Claude already searches the MCP registry for the right connector when a task has no matching tool, so with 1B+ users expressing intent in chat, being the connector the model selects becomes a high-intent organic acquisition channel. His framing ("AI apps are the new browsers, ChatGPTs are the new websites") makes an MCP server a basic buying-decision checkbox and a distribution surface, the go-to-market counterpart to "compete on glue and distribution when building on managed APIs."

Ted Johnson (JoinIn AI) pushes the interaction-as-lever point to its root by [treating AI as an interface technology, not only an intelligence one](../concepts/treat-ai-as-an-interface-technology-that-removes-human-burden.md). His diagnosis separates any interface into channel (transport/bandwidth), expression (the meaning it's permitted to carry), and protocol (the rules of the exchange): LLMs exploded expression while the channel stayed a prompt box and the protocol stayed batch, so [the prompt is still a punch card](../concepts/treat-the-prompt-as-a-batch-protocol-not-a-conversation.md) — you package a complete turn, submit, wait, and resubmit, and "prompt engineering" is just the operator's skill of assembling a good deck. The product consequence is that when a capable model "feels like work," the fix is usually the interaction protocol, not a better model: ask "what burden are we still putting on humans only because the machine used to be too limited to carry it?" and move timing, modality, context-gathering, and repair off the user, letting the AI participate mid-thought with the affordances humans already use (a question, a pause, a checklist, a quiet aside, silence). This is the same interface-is-the-product-lever claim as Angel Ortmann Lee's "engineer the interaction, not the model" and the form-factor thesis in "choose AI coworker form factors by interaction mode," but stated as a protocol upgrade — from batch turns to participation — rather than a friction-tuning or surface choice, and it warns that the reflex answer (everything is chat, or voice, or a wall of markdown) is usually wrong.

Balázs Horváth (VisualLabs) locates the durable product work upstream of all of this: once AI makes code, specs, and tests cheap, [requirements elicitation becomes the non-automatable bottleneck](../concepts/elicit-requirements-as-the-non-automatable-bottleneck.md) — "you can prompt your AI… but you can't prompt the room." His evidence is a hackathon that abandoned 17 of 21 agent ideas for lack of business value (no data access, no owner, no measurable value); the 4 that qualified run in production. Because a model "is coded to give you the most common answers," naive use replicates a Ford "faster horse," so a human must read the room and name the problem to reach the car. His remedy resurfaces the business-analyst toolkit as newly scarce and, importantly, AI-native: [capture what to build with value discovery, story maps, and trained user-story structures](../concepts/capture-ai-build-requirements-with-story-maps-and-user-stories.md) — run the VAD path (Value → Architecture → Design) and a 4-question value framework (whose problem, what winning looks like, what causes refusal, what decision it changes) tracked in a repo markdown file for AI context, then story-map the process backbone and write persona/what/need/why user stories with acceptance criteria (which become test cases), because models were trained on that structure and reward the familiar shape. The delivery consequence is a metric shift: [measure feature adoption, not shipping velocity](../concepts/measure-feature-adoption-not-shipping-velocity.md) — replace "features shipped" with "features used more than twice," watch activity frequency instead of time-on-site, and reject the demo-as-deliverable and testerless-PRD anti-patterns. This is the discovery-side complement to "AI-amplified shipping speed needs stronger product taste" and "product engineers need direct customer context": where those argue for judgment and proximity, Horváth supplies the concrete elicitation method and the KPI that tells you it worked, and pushes the org lever of moving the smartest people from writing code to naming customer problems, "because that's the expensive part."

Isadora Martin-Dye names the product category where getting the persona wrong is uniquely expensive: relationship-driven, emotionally heightened brands — a wedding venue, a luxury hotel, high-end real estate, a tool for families of missing people — where "the voice is the product" and "a single wrong sentence can cost more than a refund" because the users "are exactly the kind of people who notice." Her product argument is that treating brand voice as a one-shot prompt-engineering problem ("something you solve once and ship") loses "the thing you were trying to protect," because users "aren't testing your brand voice, they are trusting it." Her build answer, [layering brand voice into four composable prompt tiers](../concepts/layer-brand-voice-into-composable-prompt-tiers.md), doubles as a multi-tenant productization pattern: one architecture serves venues with completely different personalities where "the seam is calling one function" (a shared immutable-identity tier, per-tenant situational and voice tiers). Two of her rules are product decisions, not just engineering ones — mandatory AI self-disclosure "before they ask" is "a product decision, not a legal one" (a couple who knows from the start "will trust it more than someone who finds out on turn seven," where trust "inverts"), and a missing brand identity must "fail loud" rather than silently default, because the quiet failure is a tenant speaking in a stranger's voice and trust eroding "before anyone on your team even knows there's a problem." This sharpens the topic's taste/proximity threads with a concrete high-stakes-voice category and the reliability discipline that protecting a relationship brand requires.

Dan Feng (Maven Clinic) attacks the planning cadence that most of the above assumes. If Horváth's point is that elicitation becomes the bottleneck once code is cheap, Feng's is that the *horizon* has to shrink to match: "with AI, building is super fast… Argument is really expensive one," so the team [commits at sprint scale](../concepts/commit-at-sprint-scale-when-implementation-is-cheap.md) — dream big for the year as direction, commit for two to four weeks, keep PRDs to one or two pages meant to be argued with, and accept that "the really awkward part is mid-term goals… three months, six months," because "I don't know what AI models will be capable in three months." The consequence for product roles is the same blurring Horváth describes from the other side: engineers who understand the product now contribute "way more than a traditional engineer who only focus on software side," and the reversal of a two-week-old decision is treated as normal rather than as a planning failure. His reliability framing is also a product decision more than an engineering one — [sorting failures by whether the user can retry](../concepts/sort-failures-by-whether-the-user-can-retry.md) is a judgment about what a customer will absorb, made "even from the get beginning" rather than discovered in production, and it decides where the product is allowed to be probabilistic at all.

Rashi Agrawal (Hinge Health) supplies the decision procedure the rest of this topic assumes exists. Five days before launch, one open issue draws five different readings — clinical sees member safety risk and wants to hold, legal sees regulatory exposure, compliance sees audit risk, product sees adoption risk, engineering sees velocity risk — and "five rational people, five different risks, and five very different fixes" is not a problem seniority should settle. Her answer is to [decide ship-or-hold with explicit severity rules](../concepts/decide-ship-or-hold-with-explicit-severity-rules.md), and three of the five are worth stealing outside healthcare. *Worst case sets severity, not frequency*: "a bug that lightly annoys 100% of users is way less severe than one that could cause serious harm in 0.1% of cases," so the triage question changes from "how often does this happen" to "what's the worst version of this" — the move that frequency-weighted metrics structurally cannot make. *Severity is not capacity* — it "comes from the harm that it causes. Not who owns it, not whether your team has the capacity to fix it" — which leaves exactly three legal moves (fix, delay, or accept the risk with explicit sign-off) and forbids the fourth everyone actually uses: "you never quietly downgrade a bug just because you can't get to it." And *calibrate to revealed rather than stated risk tolerance*: if a behavior has been live for months "without escalation, without member complaints, without leadership concern, you cannot call it a launch blocker just for a new thing," because "your stated risk tolerance might be no bugs in production, but your revealed risk tolerance is what's actually shipping today." Two coda rules bind the framework to delivery reality — the default flips by bug class (hold on a safety bug, ship on a polish bug, since "the framework doesn't decide for you, it just tells you which way to lean"), and "fast follows are committed debt, not an optional backlog," which is what keeps the accept-with-sign-off option from being a polite way to drop the issue. Read against Feng's retryability classifier, the two compose: retryability sorts *which* failures a product may tolerate at all, and these rules decide what to do about a specific one on a specific date.

Ayush Bhardwaj (Allos AI) attacks the vertical-AI thesis running through this topic from the inside, having done applied AI at a hedge fund and then at a pharma-tech startup and found "the core part of my job, applied AI, remained the exact same" despite opposite operating cultures ("fast and mostly right" versus "we have to be absolutely right. You can take a week more"). His abstraction is seven steps, and its value is where it places the difficulty. The first four — pick a narrow task ("there was no tax on building more AI agents. So why do you want your single agent to do everything?"), source the data, write the prompt "after the person who you are trying to replace," wire observability — "fit one screen. The mythical 10x engineers can do this stuff in minutes… that's why it's not the moat." The fifth step, iteration, is where [you cannot iterate on output you cannot judge](../concepts/you-cannot-iterate-on-output-you-cannot-judge.md): an engineer instantly knows a weak coding model because "you've been trained for this for life," and nobody on a team of engineers has that instinct for a trade thesis or a drug candidate. This is the sharpest available statement of *how* the last-mile domain-context gap kills a product — not with a bad metric but with a finished-looking demo that no expert buyer will purchase, "the same way you won't use an inferior coding model." His moat claim then lands harder than the usual version because it comes with a mechanism: [high-value vertical data is withheld by design](../concepts/high-value-vertical-data-is-withheld-by-design.md), so "neither OpenAI nor Anthropic has this data," and "model, infra, ecosystem… is just commodity." Two of his framings are worth stealing wholesale. Production is not the bar — against the Stanford AI Index claim that 89% of enterprise agents never reach production, he counters that "every AI reaches production, but it just fails to work or justify its own cost," and in finance and pharma "if it does not make money, it's shown the door" with no credit for "maybe it'll work in 2 years." And the sequencing rule is a hiring decision rather than a tooling one: build the first four steps, then "you don't iterate yet, you hire the user."

Chaitanya Asawa (Abridge) supplies the case where the vertical thesis has already paid out, and his account sharpens two of its claims. The wedge was chosen for adoption rather than for ambition: clinical documentation is a decades-old pain point ("2 hours a day" of after-hours "pajama time"), clinicians "care a lot about getting these notes high quality and right," and the product returns provider satisfaction, more patients seen, and a better record for billing and auditing — which is what got an AI product into "300 of the largest health systems in the United States" in two to three years in an industry he describes as "technology reticent." The expansion argument is a structural observation rather than a roadmap: "everything is around the conversation… billing, clinical trial matching, clinical decision support. It's all about the conversation … and we've just built all this administrative machinery around that," so the wedge that captures the conversation is upstream of every product built on it. Against Bhardwaj's moat mechanism, Abridge's data asset is the complementary case: not a corpus withheld by its owners, but one the product itself generates at "a hundred million medical conversations a year" that "as far as we know, no one else has." That converts the usual will-the-labs-eat-you question into a testable one — [train your own models only where you have a right to win](../concepts/train-your-own-models-only-where-you-have-a-right-to-win.md), which requires both data they cannot get and "the focus on a problem that they may not be focusing on," and whose claim is about beating the frontier's *rate of change* rather than its current level. Notably, this is a vertical-AI success story whose stated hard problem is neither the model nor the domain data but evaluation, and the fix is procedural rather than a hire, since the clinicians are already on staff.

CoupleWork raises the question this topic's metrics chapter usually assumes away: what happens when the standard engagement objective is the thing harming the user. Clay Cockrell's account of consumer AI as the world's largest relationship therapist — "900 million active users per week," enough of them opening it at 11 p.m. after a fight — is a market observation, but the design conclusion is a product-strategy one. A model "optimized for one thing above all else, keeping you engaged" produces more sessions, longer sessions, and "more emotional reliance on the AI," which is "amazing" on a SaaS dashboard and "a fire alarm" clinically, so [agreeableness becomes the failure mode when the product's job is to change the user](../concepts/agreeableness-is-a-failure-mode-when-the-job-is-to-change-the-user.md). The counter-metric he proposes is deliberately awkward to instrument — the user should "return to the relationship better than they came in," and "my job is to work myself out of a job" — which puts it in direct tension with the aha-moment and retention framings elsewhere on this page, and makes it the clearest example in this wiki of a category where user satisfaction and user benefit diverge. His framing of the opportunity is also a positioning claim worth borrowing: "this is not a consumer app opportunity. This is a public health opportunity hiding inside consumer behavior," argued from the observation that "a majority of couples wait 6 years before seeking professional help," so the product's distinctive value is timing rather than depth — "an informed intervention 6 seconds before you send the text that makes everything worse." The trust bar that follows is the second strategic move: in a category with no regulator, [holding intimate data to the professional-privilege bar](../concepts/hold-unregulated-intimate-data-to-the-professional-privilege-bar.md) is an adopted standard competing against the same engagement instrumentation, which is why it has to be stated as a rule ("not is this legally defensible") rather than left to review.

Krea shows the same "don't inherit the incumbent's objective" logic in a market where the incumbents are model labs rather than engagement-optimized assistants. Sangwu Lee's read of the production image models is that their reliability is a chosen collapse of the output distribution — "the easiest and most reliable way to like render a person is render the most boring average person that exists and then like put it in a center frame" — which is correct for a user who wants a specific poster and wrong for a studio that "[doesn't] quite know what you want yet." Krea's position is the other end of that dial, and the strategic point is that [reliability and stylistic range are opposite model positions](../concepts/reliability-and-stylistic-range-are-opposite-model-positions.md) rather than quality tiers, so the segment you serve is the one whose users differ in how much certainty they arrive with. What makes this a strategy page rather than a modeling one is that the position dictates the supply chain: generic aesthetic scores cannot be used as data filters because they pull the corpus back toward the conventional center, and the fastest available quality shortcut is off the table because [refusing AI-generated training images](../concepts/refuse-ai-generated-images-when-style-is-the-product.md) is the only way to avoid shipping a detectable copy of the competitor's look. This is the differentiation-by-constraint pattern this topic sees elsewhere in vertical AI, applied to a horizontal consumer-creative market: the moat is not withheld data but the discipline to reject data everyone else can cheaply use.

Reactor's Ahmed Ahres offers a different way to find the opening in a market the labs already occupy: change what the user can do with the output rather than how good it is. His argument is that [real-time generation changes the medium, not just the latency](../concepts/real-time-generation-changes-the-medium-not-the-latency.md), supported by two cases where a latency change created industries rather than improving one — GPS, without which "Uber would not exist," and the camera viewfinder, without which "Instagram and TikTok would not exist," because on film "they can't see what they're shooting." Applied to generative video, the incumbent shape is a returned file, "a slot machine. You cannot change it," and the product claim is that steerability, not fidelity, is the scarce good: "the big problem that content creators all have is I don't have the control I need… instant feedback is the ultimate level of control." That is a positioning argument of the same family as Krea's — do not compete on the incumbent's axis — but it moves along interaction rather than distribution shape. Two cautions belong with it. This is a vendor talk arguing by historical analogy with no measurement, and Ahres is unusually candid about where the thesis is not yet cashed: avatars "hasn't actually been cracked," video-editing products on the platform are "not very good yet just because of the quality of the models," and the first commercial application he expects — ads generated live in front of the viewer, with no pre-production — is gated by brand risk aversion rather than capability, "if their logo has one pixel that is white instead of dark."

Arturo Nunez (Nereu) stakes out the position that refuses the generation axis entirely, and it is a useful counterweight to this topic's prompt-to-artifact thread. Building a game-making tool for amateurs, he [scopes the assistant to getting the user unstuck rather than one-shotting the artifact](../concepts/scope-the-assistant-to-getting-users-unstuck-not-one-shotting.md): "I don't want us to one-shot games that nobody is going to play and I don't see the point in that," while conceding "there's in the industry, of course, the need for games that are one-shotted." The reasoning is that his product's value is the user's engagement rather than the output — "enjoying the process rather than the end product" — for people making games as a creative outlet and sharing them with family and friends, not shipping into a crowded market. The intended residue is domain literacy: users should "learn along the way the language of making games, the language of game design, not necessarily the coding or programming." That makes the assistant's vocabulary a strategic choice rather than an interface detail, since whatever language it speaks is the language the user acquires. Held against the wiki's one-shot-builder page, both are coherent and the discriminator is simply what is being produced — a disposable artifact fast enough to judge and discard, or a user who can now do the thing. The caveat is that this is a closed-alpha founder's stated intent with no retention or learning data behind it.

Keegan McCallum (uRun) argues the same real-time thesis from the demand side, and reaches a market claim the affordance argument does not: [text-first AI interfaces exclude people who don't think in text](../concepts/text-first-interfaces-exclude-people-who-dont-think-in-text.md). "Working with AI involves a lot of reading and a lot of text. For some people that's more difficult. For some people they just don't think in text. They think visually and learn better that way" — and the bar he sets is not illustrated chat but parity of experience, "companions or visual mediums that are going to allow more people to experience the things a lot of us have with coding models." What makes this a strategy claim rather than a wish is the price: continuous generated video now runs about $10 for three hours, so a visual medium can be an *interface* rather than a content-production line item. The consumer shape he demonstrates is a webcam magic mirror — see yourself in an outfit, a car, a haircut you are considering — where the input is the user's live situation and there is nothing to describe in words. Two cautions belong with it. It is an aspiration in a vendor talk, with no user research or accessibility evaluation behind it; and generated video is strong per frame and weak on session consistency, so an interface built on it fails in ways a text interface does not. The adjacent thread in this wiki is the voice-in/visuals-out result — non-text output already buys a more forgiving latency envelope than speech — but McCallum's argument is about *who can use the product at all*, not about response budgets.

Ekaterina Deyneka (Reelful) takes the same complaint about the prompt as an interface and answers it with packaging rather than a new medium. Having built a multi-stage agentic video editor — media understanding, creative plan, sandbox, skills, composition code, verification — she names the delivery problem as the larger one: "it's a lot, right?… ideally we don't want our users to even know anything about it. And this is even maybe a bigger problem how to deliver this complex agentic workflow to mass consumer." The answer is to [hide the pipeline behind templates and a manual editor](../concepts/package-agentic-pipelines-behind-templates-and-a-manual-editor.md): a mobile surface because consumers capture and post while "driving, walking or maybe lifting weights"; directional templates (speak-to-camera, add b-rolls, voiceover) because "prompting videos can sometimes be also challenging," so the user selects an outcome, drops media, and "even without any prompt it will work"; and a conventional editor after the agentic pass, since "a lot of people are already sort of using regular video editors" and a near-miss should cost a drag rather than a regeneration. The one place the pipeline stays visible is a creative plan shown for approval before editing begins — consumers are asked to judge intent, which they can, rather than a composition, which they cannot. Read against McCallum's argument, this is the cheaper half of the same problem: he changes the medium so non-text thinkers can work with AI at all, while she keeps the medium and removes the prompt. Her market premise is a behavioral gap rather than a capability one — almost everyone in the room had recorded video at the conference and almost nobody had posted it.

Selling infrastructure into AI teams has its own scoping pattern, and Oxylabs' Patricija Žemaitytė describes it as adaptation rather than roadmap. A single video-download request carrying a two-week deadline and a 5-petabyte-per-month floor was really a request for a pipeline, and it kept extending — transcripts, then subtitles (the first build failed wholesale because the client's word for the artifact was not the artifact they needed), then language search, then metadata and channel information — into a product suite in about three months. Her conclusion is a positioning claim worth keeping: "the client actually doesn't buy the first product iteration, they buy your ability to adapt," in a market where targets, layouts, detection, and client needs all change, so it "is not a build-once business… an adapt-forever business." Two commercial caveats travel with it. Being early is not the same as being right — a sub-second search product built in 2024 was shelved because the client never tested it and "the market wasn't ready," then shipped in 2025 when demand arrived. And consumption can outrun collection: the video client had gathered 30 petabytes by 2026 and "we're still waiting for a payment."

Paul Klein IV (Browserbase) supplies a market thesis for the capability this wiki mostly treats as an engineering topic: "solving computer use accelerates the diffusion of AI to the real economy." The segment he points at is deliberately unfashionable — "as much as I love our bubble here in San Francisco, the real economy is companies like the logistics company in Singapore, the bank in South Africa, or the lumber factory in Mexico. These people are built on PHP websites with forms and human beings clicking buttons every single day" — and the strategic content is that this segment's defining property constrains the architecture, not just the pitch. Those businesses will not publish an API, adopt WebMCP, or staff an AI engineer, so [driving the existing UI is the only permissionless interface](../concepts/use-browser-ui-control-when-apis-are-absent.md) and browser-agent reliability *is* the product; see [Computer Use Diffuses AI Into the Form-Filling Economy](../concepts/computer-use-diffuses-ai-into-the-form-filling-economy.md). He sizes the opportunity against coding, where task completion "is so much higher… because we haven't actually really pushed the models far enough," and draws the timing conclusion that most distinguishes this from a wait-for-the-models position: "the wrong answer is to sit around and just wait for the models to get better." Read as a founder's thesis in a vendor talk, the structural half (legacy forms, no API, manual clicking, no cooperation from the site owner) is the durable part; the sizing is unquantified.

Dhruv Batra (Yutori) reaches the same segment from the supply side and turns it into a bet about which layer of the stack accrues value. His argument is that [the long tail of the web will never ship the APIs](../concepts/the-long-tail-of-the-web-will-not-ship-apis.md) that agent-integration strategies assume — the constraint being access and institutional inertia rather than technology, since "you're not going to get unfettered access to these institutions, and these institutions change very slowly. There are still places that are faxing each other" — so a company betting on protocol adoption is betting on ~200 million site owners doing work they have no reason to do. What replaces it is not a rewritten web but a task endpoint over browsers: "you will issue a task, behind the scenes there will be hundreds of browsers that are pretending and clicking buttons just like humans would, and they will give you a result in a structured format. It will cost less than a penny… And at some point you will say, yeah, that's an API." That is a falsifiable product position rather than a slogan, and its dependency is explicit — it holds only while accuracy rises and [latency and cost per task keep falling](../concepts/compete-on-latency-and-cost-per-task-when-computer-use-accuracy-ties.md). Note where he refuses the pitch: where an aggregator already returns JSON, an agent clicking through the UI "is bizarre," which makes this a claim about the tail rather than a claim that computer use eats integration everywhere.

Bright Data's Omer Primor names a category forming one layer below the browser-agent thesis above: [context as a service](../concepts/context-as-a-service-is-vertical-search-for-agents.md), vendors who crawl a single vertical, entity-dedupe it into a knowledge graph, enrich it from many sources, and sell agents the result over MCP or an API. His own framing is deflationary in a useful way — "the way I like to think about them is really vertical search engines… a very, very, very good search engine for something very specific" — and he traces the lineage plainly, calling it "an evolution of DaaS" and pointing at ZoomInfo standing up `gtm.ai` as an agent-facing second brand. What makes this a strategy question rather than a procurement one is the buy-side move it forces: because the same collection can be rented per query or built once, every team eventually has to [find its crossover point between renting and owning context](../concepts/find-the-crossover-point-between-renting-and-owning-context.md). He prices a week of pipeline setup at $5,000 and puts his own crossover "just over 15,000 entities or queries," while refusing to generalize the number ("maybe it's 30, maybe it's 100,000"). Two caveats belong with it. The arithmetic compares a one-time build against recurring per-query fees without pricing ongoing re-collection, which his own decay measurements make unavoidable. And this is a vendor talk: the speaker leads product marketing at Bright Data, and the build-it-yourself path he recommends runs on his employer's paid products — his structural claim (persistent, repeated, growing needs favor ownership; ad hoc needs favor plug-and-play) is more durable than his number.

Shlok Khemani supplies this topic's clearest example of a capability that is a product decision wearing a technology costume. His ChatGPT profile says he travelled to Turkey in 2025; he never has, and the memory came from conversations where he was choosing between Turkey and Thailand. The mistake is forgivable — the deciding conversation happened in person, where no product could hear it. What he objects to is that nothing notices the entry contradicts itself and nothing goes looking for the flight and hotel bookings sitting in an inbox the product is already connected to: "it's not a technology problem. It's a product problem. There is no fundamental reason from an LLM level that these things can't be solved." That reframes two roadmap questions. First, transparency is a shipped-product variable, not a principle — [make the memory profile visible and editable](../concepts/make-the-memory-profile-visible-and-editable.md), given that one flagship hid the artifact well enough that its own user resorted to jailbreaking, then "fixed" it by showing an LLM summary of an LLM summary, while the other exposes the raw profile and treats a user edit as an input to resynthesis. Second, the personal-AI opportunity is bounded by acquisition rather than architecture: [memory quality is capped by the context a system can reach](../concepts/memory-quality-is-capped-by-the-context-it-can-reach.md), and today each chatbot, assistant, vertical app, agent, and device rebuilds its own private memory of the user while email, calendar, and photos go unread — "none of this feels like 2026."

Yu Su supplies the market-shaped version of the same instinct, and its usable form is a claim about scarcity. Frontier intelligence "is already becoming abundance" while "expertise is still scarce," so the argument is to [scale expertise rather than capability](../concepts/scale-expertise-once-intelligence-is-abundant.md) — the payoff being that lowering the friction on expert work moves "many of the new type of work across the threshold of worth doing," a market-expansion story rather than a cost-reduction one. Two consequences bear on strategy. The buildable asset is a learning loop rather than a model: "every company can build their own learning loop," turning into institutional memory and, in his words, a moat that keeps a company "in charge of their means of production" — which is the same asset argument this topic already carries from Bright Data's owned-versus-rented context material. And [private microworlds are named as the next training-data opportunity](../concepts/private-microworlds-are-the-next-training-data-opportunity.md), specialized agents learning in situ and channeling that learning back to a general model. Those two positions sit uneasily together — a vendor's channel-back is a customer's moat leaking — and the talk does not reconcile them, which is exactly the term to examine in any in-deployment-learning contract. Nothing here is measured; it is positioning from the COO of a continual-learning company.

Jack Morris supplies a durable planning argument for anyone deciding whether to wait for the models to cover their domain. The answer is structurally no, and the reason is not that the labs are slow: every scaling axis runs on public material, including the expert-annotation layer bought from vendors, which is "still by definition creating publicly available data because it's something that the model could tell to a user" ([Post-Training Data Is Public by Construction](../concepts/post-training-data-is-public-by-construction.md)). Capability gains therefore keep landing on public tasks — "coding in the way that is public on GitHub," math "written in public textbooks" — while "they're not getting more knowledge of you or your life or your work." That is a stronger version of the wiki's existing case for [private microworlds as the next training-data opportunity](../concepts/private-microworlds-are-the-next-training-data-opportunity.md), because it does not depend on the contested claim that public data is exhausted. The strategic counterweight, delivered by a founder-adjacent researcher against his own pitch, is that owning the private axis is not a scaling story either: every technique for turning a corpus into model capability hits [a synthetic data wall](../concepts/the-synthetic-data-wall-caps-every-define-then-train-loop.md), and Engram's own early curves plateaued. Read together, the two halves argue for treating domain adaptation as a bounded project with a defined stopping point — and for asking any vendor in this space the question Morris frames the field around: how does adding compute keep buying depth, rather than finishing the dataset faster?

Sara Hooker, speaking at the same event, argues the opposite side of that ledger — not what a buyer can build on a private corpus, but who is permitted to build frontier models at all — and the strategic content is three separable claims rather than one. First, the scarce input is not capital but people: fewer than 5,000 people worldwide have trained a frontier model and the know-how is [apprenticeship, not literature](../concepts/frontier-training-know-how-is-apprenticeship-not-literature.md), passed by working next to someone who has done it. That is a hiring-market fact with a direct product consequence — tacit knowledge that lives in configuration choices is exactly the kind an automated search can attack, which is the business Adaption is in. Second, the compute barrier is claimed to be softening in *shape* rather than size: post-training and inference compute is [distributable](../concepts/distributable-compute-lowers-the-barrier-to-frontier-work.md) where pre-training compute must be colocated, so the workloads that now carry the returns do not require a single datacenter. Third, and most useful when writing policy or procurement language, [customization control is a separate question from open weights](../concepts/customization-control-is-a-separate-question-from-open-weights.md): weight distribution, the ability to adapt a model yourself, and where it runs are three axes, and a rule or a vendor promise written against only one of them leaves the others open. Read against Morris, the two talks agree on the diagnosis (the public-data frontier is not going to cover your domain) and disagree on the remedy's owner — Morris says the private axis is bounded, Hooker says the frontier axis is opening. Both are founder talks; treat the direction as argued and the timing as unmeasured.

Ben Hylak, returning to the wiki thirteen months after his first appearance, supplies the sharpest available reframing of what an agent product should be judged on, and it is a prioritization claim rather than a technical one. Score the product on two axes: the **ceiling**, "the craziest capability, emergent capability that your product or agent is capable of," and the **floor**, "the worst thing your agent can do." Almost all published eval effort measures the ceiling; the floor is where trust is lost, and his floor examples are mundane and product-shaped rather than catastrophic — "recommend a competitor," "delete a bunch of data," "accidentally send a you know, AI slop email to a customer because it like technically had access to like your email." Two of those three are consequences of access the agent legitimately held, not of a model error, which puts them squarely in the scope of a product decision rather than a model upgrade. The framing is not a call for restriction, because the floor and the ceiling have the same cause: agents that hit roadblocks "start getting really creative… that's what makes agents really powerful, but like that's also what makes them like catastrophic." You cannot delete the floor without deleting the ceiling; you can only raise it — and the slogan for how is "you want more safety but you don't want theater" ([Raise the Floor Before Maxing the Benchmark](../concepts/raise-the-floor-before-maxing-the-benchmark.md)). The question worth carrying into a roadmap review is his customer-facing one: "are you a benchmark maxer or a floor raiser?"

Two corollaries make this actionable for planning. First, where the floor sits is a property of the *product's* division of responsibility with the user, not of the model — tab complete, where "if it gets something wrong like you can just delete it"; a coding CLI, where "there's a lot more like user error… you leave a lot more up to the users"; Devin; and an AI doctor are four positions on one spectrum, and a program that raises the floor at one position is not the program the next one needs. The same observation explains why copying a lab's eval methodology misfires: labs build "super general purpose things" while application teams "imbue all this like company specific domain knowledge," so the shared word "eval" hides two unrelated activities ([Lab Eval Vocabulary Does Not Transfer to Application Teams](../concepts/lab-eval-vocabulary-does-not-transfer-to-application-teams.md)). Second, the available quality methods are set by scale before anything else — his first question to any team is "how many users do you have?", because at "10, 20, 100 million messages a day" you can experiment on a slice of the free tier and at "five or 10 users" A/B tests are meaningless, while a five-user internal enterprise app "giving like very critical information" carries no less consequence per interaction ([Match the Quality Method to Your User Count](../concepts/match-the-quality-method-to-your-user-count.md)). Consequence and volume are independent axes; only the second decides which instruments exist. Provenance caveat throughout: this is a vendor talk by a founder selling production floor detection to an audience of competitors, no figures from Raindrop's book of business appear anywhere in it, and the market claims generalize from a self-selected customer base.

## Key Concepts

- [Raise the Floor Before Maxing the Benchmark](../concepts/raise-the-floor-before-maxing-the-benchmark.md) - score the product on the worst thing it can do, not only the most impressive; the floor is where trust breaks, and it cannot be removed without removing the ceiling.
- [Lab Eval Vocabulary Does Not Transfer to Application Teams](../concepts/lab-eval-vocabulary-does-not-transfer-to-application-teams.md) - "eval" names two unrelated activities, and how much responsibility the product leaves to the user is the axis that actually sets the design.
- [Match the Quality Method to Your User Count](../concepts/match-the-quality-method-to-your-user-count.md) - ask user count before recommending anything; consequence per interaction and interaction volume are independent axes.
- [Customization Control Is a Separate Question From Open Weights](../concepts/customization-control-is-a-separate-question-from-open-weights.md) - weight distribution, adaptation control, and deployment locality are three axes; policy and procurement language that names only one leaves the others open.
- [Frontier Training Know-How Is Apprenticeship, Not Literature](../concepts/frontier-training-know-how-is-apprenticeship-not-literature.md) - fewer than 5,000 people have trained a frontier model, and the knowledge moves by working next to them.
- [Distributable Compute Lowers the Barrier to Frontier Work](../concepts/distributable-compute-lowers-the-barrier-to-frontier-work.md) - the workloads carrying the returns no longer need a colocated datacenter, which is an argument about who can enter.
- [Post-Training Data Is Public by Construction](../concepts/post-training-data-is-public-by-construction.md) - why buying more expert annotation cannot close a private-domain gap.
- [Only the Compute Axis Is Available on Your Own Corpus](../concepts/only-the-compute-axis-is-available-on-your-own-corpus.md) - the structural reason a private-data project cannot borrow the frontier playbook.

- [Scale Expertise Once Intelligence Is Abundant](../concepts/scale-expertise-once-intelligence-is-abundant.md) - the scarce input is situated competence, and lowering its cost expands what work is worth doing at all.
- [Private Microworlds Are the Next Training-Data Opportunity](../concepts/private-microworlds-are-the-next-training-data-opportunity.md) - in-situ learning inside customer environments, with ownership of the resulting capability left unaddressed.
- [Make the Memory Profile Visible and Editable](../concepts/make-the-memory-profile-visible-and-editable.md) - the artifact shaping every conversation should be readable in its served form and correctable by the person it describes.
- [Memory Quality Is Capped by the Context It Can Reach](../concepts/memory-quality-is-capped-by-the-context-it-can-reach.md) - offline events, connected-but-unread sources, and per-product silos bound the personal-AI experience.
- [Do Not Outsource the Memory System](../concepts/do-not-outsource-the-memory-system.md) - every top consumer AI product has memory and none of them buy it.
- [Context as a Service Is Vertical Search for Agents](../concepts/context-as-a-service-is-vertical-search-for-agents.md) - a forming category of vertical, entity-resolved, agent-addressable data vendors, and the DaaS lineage it comes from.
- [Find the Crossover Point Between Renting and Owning Context](../concepts/find-the-crossover-point-between-renting-and-owning-context.md) - per-query pricing versus a one-time build has a break-even you can compute, and the answer is use-case-specific.
- [The Long Tail of the Web Will Not Ship APIs](../concepts/the-long-tail-of-the-web-will-not-ship-apis.md) - betting on protocol adoption is betting on 200 million site owners doing work they have no reason to do.
- [Computer Use Diffuses AI Into the Form-Filling Economy](../concepts/computer-use-diffuses-ai-into-the-form-filling-economy.md) - the segment that cannot cooperate with agent-facing standards is the one that most needs agents, which makes UI reliability the whole product.
- [AI Training-Data Requests Compound Into Pipelines](../concepts/ai-training-data-requests-compound-into-pipelines.md) - one-off data asks from AI teams extend into product suites, and adaptation speed is what is actually being bought.
- [Hide an Agentic Pipeline Behind Templates and a Manual Editor](../concepts/package-agentic-pipelines-behind-templates-and-a-manual-editor.md) - for consumers, put the surface where the behavior happens, replace the prompt with named outcomes, and leave a familiar tool for the last mile.
- [Text-First AI Interfaces Exclude People Who Don't Think in Text](../concepts/text-first-interfaces-exclude-people-who-dont-think-in-text.md) - a chat-shaped product excludes visual thinkers; cheap continuous generation is what makes a visual medium affordable as an interface.
- [Scope the Assistant to Getting the User Unstuck, Not One-Shotting the Artifact](../concepts/scope-the-assistant-to-getting-users-unstuck-not-one-shotting.md) - when the process is the product, aim the AI at the current blocker so the user keeps authorship and picks up the domain's language.
- [Real-Time Generation Changes the Medium, Not Just the Latency](../concepts/real-time-generation-changes-the-medium-not-the-latency.md) - compete on what the user can do with the output mid-generation rather than on output quality; the GPS and viewfinder cases are the argument.
- [Reliability and Stylistic Range Are Opposite Model Positions](../concepts/reliability-and-stylistic-range-are-opposite-model-positions.md) - segment creative products by whether the user already knows what they want, and let that choose the model's distribution.
- [Refuse AI-Generated Training Images When Style Is the Product](../concepts/refuse-ai-generated-images-when-style-is-the-product.md) - the cheapest quality shortcut hands your differentiator to the model you distilled from.
- [Agreeableness Is a Failure Mode When the Product's Job Is to Change the User](../concepts/agreeableness-is-a-failure-mode-when-the-job-is-to-change-the-user.md) - in advice domains the engagement objective and the user's interest point in opposite directions, and the harm shows up as a trajectory rather than a bad response.
- [Hold Unregulated Intimate Data to the Professional-Privilege Bar](../concepts/hold-unregulated-intimate-data-to-the-professional-privilege-bar.md) - when no regulator covers data this sensitive, the handling standard has to be imported from the corresponding profession and stated as a bar the team holds itself to.
- [Train Your Own Models Only Where You Have a Right to Win](../concepts/train-your-own-models-only-where-you-have-a-right-to-win.md) - a proprietary corpus is only a moat when paired with a problem the labs are not focused on; the bet is on out-pacing the frontier's rate of change, not out-scoring today's model.
- [You Cannot Iterate on Output You Cannot Judge](../concepts/you-cannot-iterate-on-output-you-cannot-judge.md) - vertical AI projects die at a finished-looking demo the team cannot evaluate and an expert buyer will not buy; reaching production proves nothing, ROI does.
- [High-Value Vertical Data Is Withheld by Design](../concepts/high-value-vertical-data-is-withheld-by-design.md) - finance and pharma keep the reasoning data off the internet because publishing destroys its value, so the frontier labs lack it too and curation is the moat.

## Open Questions

- Is the ceiling/floor split actually a tradeoff on a roadmap, or only a sequencing claim? Hylak argues both come from the same creative-under-obstruction behavior, which implies raising the floor costs ceiling — but no product in the talk reports having measured that cost, so "raise the floor first" is offered without a price.
- What quality method fits the middle of the user-count range? The advice is stated only at the ends — free-tier experiments at 10-100 million messages a day, trace reading at five to ten users — leaving the thousands-of-users band where experiments are underpowered and hand review no longer scales entirely unaddressed, which is where most products sit.
- If the scarce input to frontier work is ~5,000 apprenticed practitioners rather than capital, what does that imply for a company that cannot hire one? Hooker's answer is her product — automated search substituting for the tacit knowledge — but the talk reports no comparison of a searched configuration against one a hired expert would have picked, so the substitution is asserted rather than priced.
- What should a buyer ask a continual-learning vendor to demonstrate? Morris's own framing suggests the test — show that added compute keeps buying depth rather than finishing a fixed dataset faster — but his talk reports no method that passes it, including his company's.

- Who owns competence an agent acquires inside a customer's environment? Su argues both that private worlds are the industry's next training-data opportunity and that companies should keep their own learning loop as a moat, without saying how a channel-back to the vendor's general model is squared with that — a contract term, not a research question.
- Do directional templates hold as users get more ambitious, or does a template catalog grow until choosing one is as hard as writing the prompt it replaced? Deyneka reports three templates and no data on where the catalog stops scaling.
- When the process rather than the output is the product, what measures whether an unblocking assistant is working — session completion, return rate, or the user's growing fluency? Nunez asserts the learning outcome without an instrument for it.
- Does a visual interaction medium actually reach the users McCallum names, and what would demonstrate it — comprehension, task completion, or retention against a text baseline? The source offers an argument, not evidence.
- Does the real-time thesis hold at today's output quality, or does the steerable-but-worse product lose to the batch-but-excellent one until model quality catches up? Ahres concedes the quality gap but does not say what closes it.
- Is a stylistic-diversity position defensible, or only early? Krea's differentiator is a curation discipline rather than an asset, and nothing in the source says what stops a larger lab from shipping a diversity-tuned variant once the segment is proven.
- Who sells a browser agent to a lumber factory in Mexico? Klein's market argument identifies demand but not a distribution path — the segment has no AI budget line, no integrator relationship with an agent vendor, and no way to evaluate reliability claims, which is the same distribution-not-capability bottleneck this topic records for developer tools.
- A wedge chosen for adoption (documentation) is not obviously a wedge that transfers to the higher-stakes products built on it. Abridge reports 300 health systems on documentation and then moves into decision support, where a wrong answer means "we entirely lose our trust" — what evidence should a team require before spending wedge-earned trust on a product with a different failure profile?
- If the moat is proprietary data generated by an in-house expert loop, what makes it durable against a competitor who hires their own practitioner? Bhardwaj asserts domain expertise and curated data as the only non-commodity layer, but the talk does not describe what prevents the same loop from being reproduced.
- If a product's success means the user needs it less, what does its business model look like? Cockrell states the clinical goal ("work myself out of a job") and the product's availability advantage, but nothing in the talk reconciles the two into pricing or retention that a subscription product could actually run on.
- Which AI-product onboarding metrics best distinguish a real aha moment from a merely completed signup or first prompt?
- Which AI-product surfaces should increase output-per-input, and which need more explicit user steering for trust?
- How can teams evaluate "shareability" before a launch without reducing product marketing to generic virality formulas?
- How should teams measure whether a delightful probabilistic feature is expanding agency rather than masking an unclear core job?
- When should a custom AI client remain the primary product surface, and when should the team prioritize portable tool/data access through MCP or other standards?
- How should teams distinguish demos that reveal real latent capability from demos that only succeed through brittle scaffolding?
- Which domain insights should become prompt context, retrieval knowledge, rules, fine-tuning data, or product workflow changes?
- Which agent affordances should be exposed as open-ended capabilities, and which need hard deterministic constraints before customers can safely compose them?
- How much early design-partner evidence is enough to distinguish a fundable AI startup wedge from a promising but narrow feature?
- Which AI team bottlenecks should be solved by hiring, and which should first be solved by upskilling, tooling, process, or clearer product scope?
- How should AI-first SaaS teams decide when discovery evidence is strong enough to change committed roadmap work?
- For a devtool/AI startup diagnosed as distribution-deficit, which GTM tactics generalize beyond the SF network game to founders who can't or won't relocate?
- For per-user adaptive software, how do teams prove a per-user divergence is a desirable uplift (not just a correct change) without an artifact-wide A/B baseline, and what governance keeps user-requested divergences inside the product's intended purpose?
