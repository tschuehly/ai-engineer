# Go To Market

## Overview

Go-to-market has become an AI engineering surface, and the useful reframe is that it is a data problem before it is a channel problem. Researching accounts, finding the right person at a company, deciding which segments exist, knowing when something changed with a customer, and building a demo are not separate automations — they are queries against an artifact most companies never build: a live, joined model of internal data (customers, colleagues, product usage) and external data (companies, people, news) that agents can read and act on. Build that substrate first, and the visible surfaces become readers of it.

Two surfaces recur once the substrate exists. The first is a market model built by classification rather than search: with embeddings over a large corpus, labelling every company in the addressable market is affordable, which converts segment definitions from anecdotes into a list that can be counted, re-sliced, and revenue-weighted. The second is an event layer over customer state — signup, usage surge, usage stop, watchlist arrival — where the most valuable trigger is the one that is an absence, because a stop produces no rows and cannot be caught by a threshold on observed activity.

The agent layer sits on top and is mostly unremarkable except for where it lives and who may call it: a shared fleet in the chat tool the team already works in, with broad read access to internal data, invoked by non-engineers who have been trained to operate tools rather than to build them. The sharp design question is permissions. An agent cloned from a high-access person cannot be shared at that person's privilege level, so bind the capability set to the caller — full read/write and full tools for the owner, drafts and a reduced tool set for everyone else — noting that drafting constrains authority and does nothing about what the assembled context can reveal.

Three cross-cutting decisions shape the stack. Agent-first requires API-first: internal dashboards and internal agents both fail without a programmatic surface over internal and external data, and the requirement extends to purchased systems, which is why buy-versus-build is better replaced by a single test of whether the system is arbitrarily customizable — a bought system of record with a good MCP server passes, and one with only a configuration screen does not. Not everything should be a chatbot: a use case someone runs repeatedly deserves a crystallized interface they can learn once, while open-ended questions belong to the flexible agent. And the staffing consequence is a forward-deployed group that both runs deals and builds the systems the deals run on, a collapse of two roles the speaker attributes directly to AI.

Two cautions run through this material. Almost none of it is measured — the strongest outcome claim in the anchor source is "very lean, but very productive" — and much of it is a vendor demonstrating its own product on its own market. The product-versus-distribution argument is treated as settled by conjunction rather than by evidence: you need both, and the new part is only that the distribution half is now engineerable by a small number of people.

A second shape appears when the buyer of the GTM stack is an internal sales organization rather than a lean founding team. At Snowflake — close to 10,000 people, about half of them in sales — the felt problem is the same missing joined model, but experienced as tool sprawl: reps use 15 tools because each holds one data point, then stitch the results together in spreadsheets across 1,000 assigned accounts. The answer there is consolidation into one governed store so agents inherit role-based access control, rather than programmatic access to systems left in place, and the hard parts move from data engineering to deployment: earn trust with a deliberately narrow, high-accuracy question set; stage the launch so each phase risks a bounded number of first impressions; and then discover that the binding constraint after general availability is that four-fifths of the organization has not tried the product, which no amount of product work will fix. The roadmap after that is a ladder rather than a feature list, because the capability that impressed people becomes their baseline within months.

A third shape is the one an engineering team draws when the buyer is its own company and the mandate is architectural rather than adoptive. Notion's GTM engineering team reclassified the problem outright — a marketing-ops problem a year ago, "one of the most interesting distributed systems problems that I've worked on" now — and reduced every workflow to four questions that became four layers: know, decide, act, learn. The know layer splits the live world model into a compute half and a serve half, with the warehouse producing a small set of modeled, versioned entities from every vendor feed and a key-value store publishing a denormalized profile agents read without joins, with agent-generated research keyed to the same IDs. The decide layer is signals — customer events important enough to change what happens next, including external ones nobody triggered — and the signal service assigns an owner and emits a task, falling through to predictive lifecycle marketing when no signal fires. The act layer is durable Temporal workflows encoded from shadowed top reps, and the learn layer threads outcomes back to the decisions that caused them. Two decisions bind the whole thing: humans and agents read the same substrate rather than an AI layer bolted on top, and no agent talks to a customer — which is simultaneously a quality policy and the control that keeps an untrusted contact-sales form from reaching an outbound channel. Build-versus-buy is settled per layer: rent orchestration, email, CRM, and enrichment; keep the context layer, because a generic tool cannot hold esoteric data models and because you cannot debug what you do not own.

Go-to-market now has a channel whose users are agents, and it can be instrumented like any other. Jarmak's generative-engine-optimization pilot is the concrete method: write prompts the way your ideal customer actually asks at the moment of need, run them against assistants, and count mentions and recommendations separately per prompt type. The result argues that the default measurement flatters you — Sourcegraph appeared in 65% of comparison-shopping answers and in none of the answers to a prompt describing the pain its product exists for, which reads as a messaging gap rather than a ranking one. Two operational findings sit under it. Stale material about you compounds rather than decays, because superseded models publish content that newer models read, so a newer release can make the problem worse and a deprecation is a measured content campaign rather than a changelog line. And distribution has an adoptability half: being listed in the marketplaces and MCP registries where agents look is necessary, but an agent will not recommend a tool whose adoption path runs through demos and sales email.

A fourth vantage point is the back office of a large sales organization, where the constraint is neither the world model nor the deployment but the operations team in the middle. Cloudflare's revenue-operations account names two deficits on the frontline that are usually conflated: the context gap, the assembly cost a rep pays switching between a prospect call, a customer call, and an adoption conversation, and the expert gap, the distance between the best rep's handling of a situation and a ramping rep's. They take different fixes — the first is retrieval and generation on demand, the second is curated expert knowledge someone has to author — and shipping only the first buys a faster path to the same uneven quality. The delivery architecture is three concurrent modes rather than a ladder: a human request queue people prefer, a pushed weekly narrative for the population that never opens a dashboard, and a self-serve agentic workspace giving each rep their own persistent compute for forecast briefs, QBR decks, account plans, and renewal prep. Curation is claimed as the foundation of the whole thing, through a central submission alias reviewed jointly by the business and operations teams, with skill proliferation during an "explosion of excitement" named as the live risk to source-of-truth alignment. And the honest limit is visible in what has not been built: every deployed use case is a read, while quoting, approvals, and CRM writes are named as the harder class still ahead — a sequencing that recurs across internal GTM deployments and deserves to be treated as a rule rather than as an accident.

A fifth vantage completes the set, and it is the vendor's: Clay's Everett Berry walks the same territory as four problems in dependency order — data, orchestration, agents, execution — and supplies the mechanics the other accounts assume. The world model has a price. No data vendor covers a field for your whole population, so values are assembled by waterfalling providers and the ordering is decided by running evals against them; refreshing everything is unaffordable when the data is purchased, so freshness becomes a per-field budget ranked by volatility, with employee count and headquarters location at opposite ends; and because each vendor represents the same company differently, entity resolution is an admission requirement rather than a cleanup pass. Orchestration is then reclassified as data engineering across 10-30 tools with disagreeing world views, heterogeneous refresh cadences, fan-out, and constant partial failure — including a race that no amount of model quality fixes, where the tools sync to each other outside your orchestrator so a contact created in the CRM is not yet actionable in the sequencer. The execution layer is where the constraints stop being technical: reply rates of half a percent to one percent mean the program's whole value lives at a margin a mediocre agent erases, sending as a named rep puts company-wide deliverability behind the model's output, and a booked call has to suppress the email sequence and the lifecycle campaign in two other vendors. Two positions here cut against the rest of the cluster. Berry's agents write to the CRM without an approval gate, contained instead by giving them their own columns — a third option between read-only and human-approved. And asked for the hardest problem across the whole stack, he names none of his four: it is the interface between the human and the agent, because once the agent is the decision layer, the rep may disagree with what it decided or never learn that it happened.

The agent channel also runs in the opposite direction from the outbound machinery most of this topic describes: not your agents reaching a market, but someone else's agent recommending you. The measurement problem there is that a recommendation made inside an assistant leaves no referrer and often no click — the agent installs the package itself — so the ordinary analytics stack cannot see the channel at all. The cheapest working instrument is an ordinary onboarding question, "how did you hear about us?", read as free text: one open-source library saw the answers spike from a specific date and now ranks assistant recommendation as its largest single inbound source. Recording the date matters more than the share, because it gives the change a boundary to line releases and content work up against. This is the demand-side complement to the prompt-harness method already recorded here, which counts how often an assistant mentions you but cannot tell you whether anyone acted on it.

A sixth vantage asks a question none of the others do: not what the system should know or decide, but why a good idea takes months to reach the field. Ramp's answer is that the bottleneck was never ideas — product, data, engineering, and sales all have them — but everything downstream, and specifically the persuasion step, where someone writes a proposal and enablement material and then convinces channel owners to adopt a playbook. Data inconsistency, rep busywork, and coordination cost are three separate bottlenecks with three different fixes, and only the third is what an orchestration layer is for. The stated end state is an interface rather than a workflow: describe a motion in a sentence, and get an audience, outbound sequences, ad and web creative, and in-app nudges, each reviewed and signed off by the person who owns that channel. The foundations underneath are familiar — an internal customer data platform, durable Temporal threads with every tool and model call as an activity, chunked and embedded transcripts and playbooks searched with vector, attribute, and keyword filters scoped to one account — with two departures worth recording. The serving store is Postgres, chosen for transactional guarantees and cross-system referential integrity rather than for join-free millisecond reads, which is the direct opposite of Notion's denormalized key-value profile and the sharpest unacknowledged disagreement in this cluster. And the skill library is deliberately permissive: users write their own brief formats, and that permissiveness is credited with adoption, where Cloudflare's central alias exists to prevent exactly that proliferation — reconcilable once you separate shared skills, which are a shared liability, from personal formatting instructions, which are not. The build sequence is vertical-first and mirrored sideways, with an unusually specific account of what transfers: the embedding pipeline and custom instructions generalize, the data sources and skills do not. And the most portable idea is organizational: expose the background agents' exact tool surface to employees over an internal MCP server, so every shipped capability federates for free, and read what people then build — prompts, skills, vibe-coded apps handed over — as a backlog that arrives with its own prototype and a validated user attached.

A seventh vantage is the only one in this cluster that looks at the buyer rather than at the machine. Aliisa Rosenthal ran OpenAI's enterprise side from a couple of million in revenue to several billion, and her retrospective is a sequence of ordering errors rather than architecture. The one that organizes the rest is a sampling problem: for nine months the only enterprise requirements anyone could hear came from the companies persistent enough to reach one person, which meant large ones, so the first paid tier was specified upmarket, priced from cost to serve at sixty dollars a seat, and shipped before any self-serve option existed. Self-serve arrived four months later, grew faster, cannibalized the enterprise business, and left reps competing with their own funnel — and the corrected order became policy for every subsequent launch: ship self-serve, read what it fails to cover, build the paid tier from that, then hire people to sell it. The same inversion applies to the org itself. Build the automated motion first, run it until it visibly breaks, and hire at the breaks, because a bottleneck you have observed is testable and one you have projected is not; the stated hiring trigger is buyers explicitly asking to talk to a human, which arrives naturally as you move upmarket and comes attached to a warning about letting a single large customer consume your legal, security, product, and engineering capacity. Underneath sit four operational rules that the GTM-engineering accounts in this topic do not cover because they live on the buyer's side of the table: capture more at signup than you can currently use and reply to everyone even automatically, because the leads you triage away buy something else while they wait; never hand the buyer a task, because a handoff stops the clock somewhere you cannot see it; treat a supervised pilot as a second sales process you run for free and answer the objection with a reference call, an eval on a slice of their data, or a contract with an opt-out that puts the validation clock on them; and automate the security path — trust portal, auto-signed NDA, questionnaire autofill — then require the buyer to name what they could not find before granting a call. The counter-intuitive closer is that automating the funnel raises the value of what remains human: conferences, booths, and dinners work precisely because everything cheaper has been automated, and value selling is named as the capability that has not yet transferred.

## Open Questions

- Does self-serve-first generalize past a company with consumer-scale inbound? OpenAI's self-serve motion grew into demand for a product the public already knew; a company with no brand may get nothing from a self-serve launch to learn from, and the claim that launching in the other order would have worked better is a counterfactual asserted rather than tested. ([Rosenthal](../sources/20260826_wdTRsfw0KG0.md), 02:59-04:14)
- Which pilot substitute actually satisfies a buyer's uncertainty about an AI product? A reference call and a seller-driven demo answer whether it works in general; only an eval on the buyer's own data answers whether it works on their distribution. The source lists all four as interchangeable, and none of them is reported with a conversion or win-rate comparison. ([Rosenthal](../sources/20260826_wdTRsfw0KG0.md), 07:27-08:15)
- What does an autofilled security questionnaire get wrong, and who checks it? Questionnaire autofill is recommended with no review step, though a wrong answer is a contractual representation rather than a marketing claim, and AI-specific questions — training-data use, model subprocessors, prompt retention, agent write scope — are exactly the ones least likely to be answerable from an existing document corpus. ([Rosenthal](../sources/20260826_wdTRsfw0KG0.md), 08:33-08:51)
- Which serving-store tradeoff is right, and where does each break? Notion serves a denormalized profile "in milliseconds with no joins" while Ramp keeps transactional guarantees and referential integrity in Postgres, and neither source gives a latency, volume, or entity-count figure — so the wiki records two opposite answers to the same design question with no evidence for choosing between them. ([Liu](../sources/20260826_L4I7WgiEquo.md), 09:36-10:07; [Vaziri](../sources/20260826_VjEP0xqTUI0.md), 06:28-07:03)
- How is a fuzzy event-to-account mapping validated? Attributing a meeting to an account is named as a "sneaky hard problem" because one email can act for several businesses, and the described remedy is only to persist the result — with no matching method, confidence threshold, correction path, or account of what a rep sees when the brief is about the wrong customer. ([Vaziri](../sources/20260826_VjEP0xqTUI0.md), 09:53-10:11)
- Does exposing a full internal tool surface over MCP survive its own success? Config-scoped tool calls are mentioned for background agents, and nothing is said about what an employee's own client may read or write through the same server, nor about audit, rate limits, or the blast radius of a tool that writes. The distribution win is reported; the access-control model is not. ([Vaziri](../sources/20260826_VjEP0xqTUI0.md), 10:49-10:58, 14:11-14:40)
- Does the read-before-write sequencing hold because writes are genuinely harder, or because nobody has tried? Joyce's deployment is entirely read-side with quoting, approvals, and Salesforce writes named as "harder problems" mid-build; Liu's writes are outbound drafts behind human approval; and no source here describes an internal GTM deployment that started on the write path, so the pattern is consistent but untested. ([Joyce](../sources/20260826_Qw_tC68KKes.md), 17:51-18:11)
- Does content written to close a pain-prompt gap actually move assistant recommendations, and on what timescale? Jarmak's content campaign was in progress at the time of the talk, with the lift to be measured through agents' web-search tool calls rather than through training-data inclusion. ([Jarmak](../sources/20260826_Lrw0jqBNaw0.md), 09:32-10:10)
- How much of the activation cost is transferable? Izmit spends 60-70% of his time on demos, adoption dashboards, and executive sponsorship, and credits it with roughly half of current usage — at a technically fluent data company with executive reach into its own sales org. No source here reports what the same push costs, or achieves, in an organization without those advantages. ([Izmit](../sources/20260826_DrTdD-ttjCY.md), 07:53-08:41)
- What is the accuracy of an exhaustively classified market? Wang reports classifying "basically every possible company" in the TAM and attaching anticipated spend per account, with no sample, spot check, precision figure, or method for the spend estimate. Segment counts derived from a labelled market are only as good as the labels, and no source here measures them. ([Wang](../sources/20260826_6pbQgnJ9Voc.md), 05:22-06:58)
- Does a decision-calibrated clone actually produce accepted output? The evals built from hundreds of past decisions are the strongest methodological claim in the anchor source and carry no pass rate, baseline, or held-out split, and nobody reports whether the drafts colleagues receive are sent, edited, or discarded. ([Wang](../sources/20260826_6pbQgnJ9Voc.md), 09:10-09:27)
- Where does "arbitrarily customizable" run out for a bought system? An MCP server is a narrower surface than source code, and no source here describes a case where a purchased system's customizability was exhausted — which is exactly the evidence the criterion needs to be a decision rule rather than a preference. ([Wang](../sources/20260826_6pbQgnJ9Voc.md), 11:56-13:16)
- Does the everyone-does-everything forward-deployed model survive growth? Eight or nine FDEs at ~115 people is reported as working, and the speaker's own answer to whether it scales is "probably not," with no account of what breaks first. ([Wang](../sources/20260826_6pbQgnJ9Voc.md), 18:07-18:31)
- Does the shared-substrate rule survive a company whose substrate is not its own product? Notion's context layer is Notion, which makes "humans and agents read the same thing" nearly free and the claim unfalsifiable from this source; no source here reports what the same architecture costs when the substrate has to be assembled from a CRM, a wiki, and a warehouse. ([Liu](../sources/20260826_L4I7WgiEquo.md), 11:15-11:17, 18:18-18:44)
- What does the learn layer actually change? Outcome-to-decision threading is presented as the mechanism that makes the system self-improving, with three branches (continue, advance, pivot) and no worked example, attribution window, or measured effect — and the two reported figures measure the system, not the loop. ([Liu](../sources/20260826_L4I7WgiEquo.md), 14:53-15:37, 18:48-19:14)
- Who catches the note-borne constraints? "The champion just left," "don't contact this customer again," and "they're blocked in legal" are named as the facts an automation must not miss, and the described remedy is only that notes live in the same context layer — no extraction, validation, or enforcement path is given for turning such a sentence into a hard stop on an outbound action. ([Liu](../sources/20260826_L4I7WgiEquo.md), 05:00-05:27)
- What is the ground truth for a data-provider eval? Berry makes provider evals the mechanism that decides a waterfall's order and allows the judgment to be outsourced to the intermediary, and gives no sample design, per-field metric, or truth source — which is the hard part, because the reference answer for a phone number is the same market the vendors are selling into. ([Berry](../sources/20260826_UhCY231d0FQ.md), 05:18-05:28)
- Does agent-field separation actually contain a bad write, or only relocate it? Containment holds until a routing rule, a report, or another agent reads the agent-owned column, and no source here describes enforcing the boundary on the read side or a policy for promoting an agent field to a human one once it is trusted. ([Berry](../sources/20260826_UhCY231d0FQ.md), 12:23-12:43)
- How is a months-long per-account agent evaluated at all? Berry asserts a high bar for error on customer communication and offers no trajectory metric, sample review, or acceptance procedure for an agent whose decisions play out over a deal cycle — and the learning phase that would close the loop is called "not fully solved yet." ([Berry](../sources/20260826_UhCY231d0FQ.md), 10:38-12:10)
